'''''New Year's Evil''''' is a 1980 American [[slasher film]] directed by Emmett Alston, and starring [[Kip Niven]], [[Roz Kelly]], and [[Chris Wallace (entertainer)|Chris Wallace]]. The plot follows a Los Angeles [[punk rock]] and [[new wave music|new wave]] show host who receives a series of phone calls during a televised [[New Year's Eve]] bash from a killer warning of impending murders that he plans to exact as the New Year dawns on each time zone.

==Plot==
One [[New Year's Eve]], popular punk rock/[[new wave music|new wave]] disc jockey Diane Sullivan (known as "Blaze" among her fans) is hosting a late-night countdown celebration, televised live from a Hollywood hotel and simulcast on local radio. All is going well until Diane receives a phone call from an odd-sounding stranger, who claims his name is Evil. He announces his intention to murder one "naughty girl" at the stroke of midnight in [[Time in the United States|each US time zone]]; he warns that Diane, located in the [[Pacific Time Zone]], will be the last victim. Meanwhile, Diane's son Derek arrives at the studio, but is mostly ignored by his mother; he begins behaving erratically throughout the night as he watches the show on TV.

The studio crew takes safety measures and heightens security, but a string of murders begins to occur across Los Angeles; a nurse at a sanitarium is found dead shortly after midnight strikes in the [[Eastern Time Zone]], and two women are found dead near a liquor store after midnight in the [[Central Time Zone]]. The killer uses a radio/cassette recorder to tape the sounds of his victims as he murders them and calls back the station each time, replaying the tapes over the phone to prove that he is serious. 

While the killer searches for a victim to kill at the stroke of midnight in the [[Mountain Time Zone]], he inadvertently angers a gang of bikers, who chase him into a [[drive-in theater]]. He murders one of the bikers, steals a couple's car to avoid being recognized, and drives off with the girl still in the backseat, intending to make her his next victim, but she manages to escape.

Eventually, the killer manages to sneak into the hotel, which by now has been locked down by the police. It is revealed that the killer is Diane's husband, Richard, who was previously thought to be too busy to attend. After knocking Diane out, he castigates her over her treatment of their son, perceiving her and other women's treatment of him as a slight on his character. Richard tells his wife that he [[misogyny|hates women]]. He reveals his intention to kill her by chaining her to an elevator and forcing it to ride "all the way up, then all the way down," having hacked the elevator controls. While he is doing this, the police, alerted to his presence, locate him. A brief firefight ensues, and the elevator controls are damaged, stopping the elevator and saving Diane. Richard flees to the rooftop, putting on the mask he had used to conceal himself while entering the building. Cornered by the police, he proceeds to jump to his death; a devastated Derek weeps over his body.

The injured Diane is loaded into an ambulance, but Derek is revealed to be the driver, having taken his dead father's mask and murdered the real driver. The ambulance drives off, leaving Diane's fate unknown, as a radio announcer in [[Honolulu]] is heard announcing the stroke of midnight in the [[Hawaii–Aleutian Time Zone]].

==Production==
The Cannon Group had a waiver to make the film during the [[1980 actors strike]]. [[Kip Niven]] was cast in the role of Richard Sullivan, of which he commented: "I've played bad guys before—like one of the three vigilante motorcycle cops in [[Clint Eastwood]]'s ''[[Magnum Force]]''—but this movie gave me my first leading part and the first sustained villain role that lets me pull the stops out...&nbsp;I could work while many others couldn't."<ref name=":2">{{Cite news |date=January 2, 1981 |title=Villainous role belies his clean-cut image|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-kansas-city-star-villainous-role-bel/160909307/ |work=[[The Kansas City Star]]|last=Butler|first=Robert R.|via=Newspapers.com|page=1B}}</ref>

For many cast and crew members, ''New Year's Evil'' was their first feature film: Niven,<ref name=":2" /> Chris Wallace,<ref name="Ed Kelleher">{{cite news |first=Ed|last= Kelleher |date=December 26, 1980 |title=Stage Center |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-delaware-gazette-stage-center-by-ed/160936363/ |work=[[The Delaware Gazette]]|via=Newspapers.com|page=5}}</ref> [[Grant Cramer]],<ref>{{cite news |date=December 28, 1980 |title=Suspense melodrama substandard|first= Robert C.|last= Trussell |url=https://www.newspapers.com/image/678127472/?clipping_id=160936272&fcfToken=eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJmcmVlLXZpZXctaWQiOjY3ODEyNzQ3MiwiaWF0IjoxNzM0NTI0NDkxLCJleHAiOjE3MzQ2MTA4OTF9.NLu1f-YCdgeJNYQhVhstILPNi2mLBeRiQO-bY2G_GDk |via=Newspapers.com |work=[[The Kansas City Star]] |page=33}}</ref> [[Thomas E. Ackerman]],<ref name=":4" /> [[Teri Copley]],<ref name=":3" /> [[Taaffe O'Connell]], and Julie Purcell.

Shadow, a long-haired touring rock band from [[Kennewick, Washington]] heard about a [[casting call]] for a [[new wave music|new wave]] punk band to play live in a film, cut their hair and auditioned, landing the role of the featured band, out of fifty bands.<ref name="Hassenger"/> 

===Filming===
[[Principal photography]] mainly took place in and around [[Los Angeles|Los Angeles, California]]. The shoot began in Los Angeles, on October 15, 1980 and was completed in 18 days, on time for the film to release two months later for the New Year's holiday. According to [[Thomas E. Ackerman]], [[Director of Photography]], they were "using one of the last [[Mobile cinema|Cinemobile]] production vans in which all camera, lighting, and grip equipment was compartmentalized on a single vehicle, with an on-board generator...one Brute [[Arc lamp|arc]]...my only 'big gun' for night exteriors, along with a couple of Maxi-brutes and four big-eye 10ks...In fact, it is amazing what you can accomplish with very little."<ref name=":4">{{Cite book |last=Rogers |first=Pauline B. |url=https://www.google.com/books/edition/Contemporary_Cinematographers_on_Their_A/86vMCgAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&dq=thomas+e.+ackerman&pg=PT15&printsec=frontcover |title=Contemporary Cinematographers on Their Art |year=1998|publisher=CRC Press |isbn=978-1-136-04577-6 |language=en}}</ref>

The end credits give "Special Thanks to [[Gazzarri's|Bill 'Godfather of Rock and Roll' Gazzarri]]."<ref name=afi/>

==Themes==
The motivation for the villain in ''New Year's Evil'', similar to other slasher films, is revenge.<ref>{{cite book|title=Slasher Films: An International Filmography, 1960 Through 2001|author=Armstrong, Kent Byron|year=2003|publisher=McFarland|isbn=978-0-786-41462-8|page=}}</ref> Ebert said the film "has music as its gimmick." Chuck Blystone for The Pantagraph contends "If the movie does have a message, it is that violence begets violence".<ref name=":5" />

Film scholar [[John Kenneth Muir]] notes Sullivan is "squarely in the [[Norman Bates]] mold; someone driven to violence by his family relationships," citing the killer's incantation, "You've castrated me. And that is not nice."{{sfn|Muir|2011|page=117}}

==Reception==

=== Contemporary ===
Bill Cosford for the ''[[Miami Herald]]'' described it as among the "knife-in-the-night genre" using a rock-concert backdrop featuring high-decibel "new-wave" bands saying "this music and its strange milieu is used to nice effect, punching up a wheezy plot."<ref>{{Cite news |date=December 30, 1980 |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-miami-herald-new-years-evil/136489625/ |last=Cosford|first=Bill|title=Few scares left in 'Evil'|work=[[Miami Herald]]|via=Newspapers.com|page=4C}}</ref>

On the [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], ''New Year's Evil'' has an approval rating of 25% based on 8 reviews, with an average rating of 3.5/10.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/new_years_evil|title=New Year's Evil (1981)|work=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]|publisher=[[Fandango Media]]|access-date=April 4, 2020}}</ref> On [[Metacritic]] the film has a [[weighted mean|weighted average score]] of 33 out of 100, based on 4 critics, indicating "generally unfavorable reviews".<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/new-years-evil|title=New Year's Evil (1980) reviews|work=[[Metacritic]]|publisher=[[CBS Interactive]]|access-date= April 4, 2020}}</ref> [[Roger Ebert]] of the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'' gave the film a 1½ and wrote, "''New Year's Evil'' is an endangered species - a plain, old-fashioned, gory [[Thriller (genre)|thriller]]. It is not very good. It is sometimes unpleasantly bloody. The plot is dumb and the twist at the end has been borrowed from hundreds if not thousands of other movies. But as thrillers go these days, ''New Year's Evil'' is a throwback to an older and simpler tradition, one that flourished way back in the dimly remembered past, [[Halloween (1978 film)|before 1978]]".<ref name=ebert>{{cite web|url = https://rogerebert.com/reviews/new-years-evil-1980|archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20251112150152/https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/new-years-evil-1980|archive-date =November 12, 2025|title = ''New Year's Evil''|last = Ebert|first = Roger|author-link = Roger Ebert|date = December 31, 1980|via=[[RogerEbert.com]]|work = [[Chicago Sun-Times]]|access-date = November 10, 2019|url-status = live}}</ref> [[Gene Siskel]] gave the film zero stars out of four, calling it "a hideously ugly motion picture".<ref>{{cite news|last=Siskel|first=Gene|author-link=Gene Siskel|date=December 24, 1980|title=No matter whose fault, 'Formula' doesn't work|work=[[Chicago Tribune]]|page=4|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/chicago-tribune-gene-siskel-movie-review/48108080/|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' wrote: "The true horror of ''New Year's Evil'' is the endless musical numbers by punk rockers and shots of their dancing fans. Amongst that, the bloody killings seem a welcome relief".<ref>{{cite journal|title=Film Reviews: New Year's Evil|work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date= January 21, 1981|page=27}}</ref>

Chuck Blystone for [[The Pantagraph]] while commenting on the "offensive qualities" of "Slash-'em up films" contended, "While it is classier than the norm [it was] yet another attempt to break from that mold [that] generally maintains a higher standard [with] one marvelous shock scene...so well done it compares favorably to similar heartstoppers in ''Jaws''".<ref name=":5">{{Cite news |date=January 15, 1981 |title='New Year's Evil' review|first=Chuck|last= Blystone|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-pantagraph-new-years-evil-movie-r/160907896/|via=Newspapers.com |work=The Pantagraph |pages=B-5}}</ref>

=== Retrospective ===
Jerry Bokamper of ''[[The Dallas Morning News]]'' opined it "is of minor cultural curiosity on two points. Its style sense represents a queasy turn-of-the-'80s synthesis of disco and punk.  Safety-pinned cheeks and shocking orange blush coexist with feathered perms and turquoise Qiana knit dresses."<ref name=":3">{{Cite news |date=January 5, 2001 |title='80s fashions tell a real horror story|first=Jerry|last=Bokamper|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-idaho-statesman-80s-fashions-tell-a/160935095/|work=[[The Dallas Morning News]]|via=Newspapers.com |page=75}}</ref>

Jesse Hassenger of ''[[GQ]]'' magazine included it in his list of "Exactly 13 Great New Year's Eve Movies" calling it "an unusual hybrid of ridiculous-gimmick and serial-killer thriller slasher" declaring "1980's quickie oddity ''New Year's Evil...'' wins the token holiday-themed slasher spot" while describing the live footage and accompanying neon lighting as giving the movie an extra jolt of novelty and saying "it's not exactly terrifying, but it's entertaining perverse and has genuine holiday atmosphere, giving a [[Dick Clark Productions|Dick Clark-style TV broadcast]] a menacing, purgatorial feel that now doubles as a time capsule" adding "the theme song by Shadow rips."<ref name=Hassenger/>

Among retrospective reviews, [[Dread Central]]'s Matt Serafini concluded: "This isn't worth your time if you're looking for a horror film to deliver in scares or suspense, but as a late night horror fix, it's ideal. What ''New Year's Evil'' lacks in scares it makes up for in pure entertainment. And really, that's all you can ask for".<ref>{{cite web |last=Serafini |first=Matt |date=December 26, 2009 |title=Saturday Nightmares: ''New Year's Evil'' (1980) |url=https://dreadcentral.com/news/35116/saturday-nightmares-new-years-evil-1980#axzz37NoUpecE |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181205010249/https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/35116/fantasia-2012-a-look-at-one-beast-of-a-movie/|archive-date=December 5, 2018|website=[[Dread Central]]}}</ref>

December 2023, in a list of top holiday horror and slasher genre movies, Drew Tinnin of DreadCentral called it an "obvious attempt to cash in on the new slasher craze sweeping across the nation" and "a weirdly engaging madcap murder mystery seething with 80s punk rock revelry" saying "[[Terror Train]] is the clear contender as the most recognized NYE shocker. But...it's time to ring in the new year with Emmett Alston's 1980 New Wave slasher New Year's Evil."<ref>{{Cite web |last=Tinnin |first=Drew |date=2023-12-29 |title=Ring in 2024 with 'New Year's Evil' |url=https://www.dreadcentral.com/editorials/471183/ring-in-2024-with-new-years-evil/ |access-date=2024-12-28 |website=[[Dread Central]] |language=en-US}}</ref>

Jake Dee writing for The BLACK SHEEP column of [[Joblo.com]] debated: <blockquote>''extremely overlooked in the context of standalone slasher flicks of the early 80s" suffering ''the misfortune of getting lost in the ungodly spate of post-HALLOWEEN imitators and cheaply made slasher derivations that flooded the market at that time ''saying the film ''bucks convention in more ways than it clings to it ''with ''a kickass soundtrack full of late 70s shredders, a hilarious middle-section car-ride, and a drolly assorted death-stroke every dozen minutes or so ''making it ''harder to understand why NEW YEAR'S EVIL was so summarily panned to begin with ''and claiming the film &nbsp;''rises above many to most (meritorious)!<ref>{{Cite web |last=Dee |first=Jake |date=2019-01-04 |title=The F*cking Black Sheep: New Year's Evil (1980) |url=https://www.joblo.com/the-fcking-black-sheep-new-years-evil-1980/ |access-date=2024-12-28 |website=JoBlo |language=en-US}}</ref> </blockquote>

Peter Normanton in the ''The Mammoth Book of Slasher Movies'' writes, "Alston's low-budget film certainly entertained as it juxtaposed an eighties rock score with a series of contrived killings, played out to the darkest of unintentional comedy."{{sfn|Normanton|2012|pages=324–325}}

Roger Ebert explained "The movie takes place on New Year's Eve, during a national TV show obviously inspired by [[The Midnight Special (TV series)|"Midnight Special"]]. The hostess (Roz Kelly) is taking votes over the air for the new wave song of the year".<ref name="ebert" />

The New Year's Evil soundtrack comprised rock songs performed live by the real bands Shadow and Made in Japan and an electronic music score with [[Moog synthesizer]] composed by W. Michael Lewis and Laurin Rinder, who are credited with producing the live recording of the movie's main title song of the same name, which plays over the opening and closing credits and once during the film.<ref name=":7">{{Cite news|last=Jarvis|first=Elena|date=January 9, 1981|title=Shadow shows movie and music fans they're a class act|work=[[The Gazette (Colorado Springs)|The Gazette]]|url=https://newspaperarchive.com/tags/new-year's-evil/?pci=7&ndt=ex&pd=9&pm=1&py=1981&search=ymd/#:~:text=Colorado%20Springs-,Gazette,-09%20Jan%201981|via=NewspaperArchive.com|url-access=subscription}}</ref><ref name=FilmFracture/>

The song was written by [[Roxanne Seeman]] and [[Eddie del Barrio]], who responded to a spec call for an original title and theme song to be played live by a punk band being featured in the film. Requests for a song were sent to several writers by the [[music supervisor]], Rex Devereaux. Del Barrio and Seeman submitted their original song, "New Year's Evil". Del Barrio wrote chords and melody on a synthesizer and gave a rough mixdown to Seeman to write the lyrics. The final demo recording with Seeman's lyrics was sung by session singer Gary Falcone,<ref>{{Cite web |title=Gary Falcone {{!}} Credits |url=https://www.allmusic.com/artist/gary-falcone-mn0000740725/credits |access-date=October 8, 2019 |website=[[AllMusic]] |language=en-us}}</ref> recorded on del Barrio's [[4-track (multitrack)|4-track machine]]. === Background ===
Shadow was a [[Seattle]] rock band who had been touring across the US and British Columbia non-stop for six years when they were spotted by a film scout playing in a club in Los Angeles. Ray Leonard was approached for the audition.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Viagogo |url=https://www.viagogo.com/Concert-Tickets/Dance-and-Electronic-Music/Shadow-Band-Tickets |access-date=2026-06-30 |website=www.viagogo.com}}</ref>

The lineup of the band appearing in the film comprised Cliff White on guitar, Ray Leonard (who changed his name to Ray Mega for the movie release) on bass, David Kesterson vocals, Art Bennett on drums, and J.P Pakalenka, vocals and guitar.<ref name=":1" /><ref name=":6">{{Cite web |title=Shadow - Kennewick, WA (1973-1982) |url=http://pnwbands.com/shadow1.html |access-date=October 8, 2019 |website=pnwbands.com}}</ref><ref name=":7" />

Cliff White, guitar player explained "They were really searching for a punk-band but liked our original music and thought it would fit in the movie."<ref name=":1" /><ref name=":7" />

The filmmakers wanted the sound of a raw, live performance for the live event in the movie during which Shadow performed on-camera. Microphones were set up at Salty Dog Recording with W. Michael Lewis and Laurin Rinder producing.  The band set up their equipment, especially excited about having [[Rick Hart (sound engineer)|Rick Hart]], who had worked with [[Pink Floyd]], as their engineer, recording the songs as they were playing them. There was no [[overdubbing]] or effect. If someone made a mistake, the song was recorded over again, from scratch.<ref name=":1" /><ref name=":7" />

For the soundtrack album release, the band was promised they could re-record the songs with proper production:<ref name=":1" /><blockquote>"The release of the 45 was a complete surprise to us.  We were not informed that they were going to release it to radio stations all over the country. When our booking agency told us about the release, we were pissed off beyond belief…Later on, we were glad they released the 45, as Cannon Records soon went out of business and we would have ended up with nothing.” a.m. to 1 p.m., for three days."<ref name=":1"/></blockquote>The main title track "New Year's Evil" by Shadow, and "The Cooler" by Made in Japan were pressed as [[7" Vinyl]] [[45rpm]] promotional singles and sent to 500 radio stations throughout the US.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.discogs.com/Shadow-New-Years-Evil-Simon-Bar-Sinister/release/5273809|title=Shadow (75) - New Year's Evil / Simon Bar Sinister|website=Discogs|date=1980 |language=en|access-date=2019-10-08}}</ref>  A full soundtrack record was announced in the closing credits of the film and on the original poster, but remained unreleased. In March 2020, [[Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer|MGM]] released the main title song "New Year's Evil" on digital streaming platforms.<ref>{{cite web|title=New Year's Evil|url=https://open.spotify.com/track/301BIitLiBbpv43My9RwSI|language=en|access-date=August 2, 2026|work=[[Spotify]]}}</ref>

===Critical reception===
''New Year's Evil'' has become a cult horror film<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.filmsite.org/cultfilms.html|title=Cult Films|website=[[Filmsite]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241217065123/https://www.filmsite.org/cultfilms.html|archive-date=December 17, 2024|url-status=live}}</ref> with a preponderance of podcasts and reviews of the film, the song "New Year's Evil" and the music soundtrack:

''[[Dread Central]]'' wrote: "Much of the music comes from an actual band named Shadow. The odd thing about Shadow is that their music sounds far more like a Seventies metal band than the Eighties punk rock and new wave the film promotes. Ah, who cares? It's 1980 and the top act on a New Year's countdown show isn't [[Barry Manilow|Manilow]], [[The Gap Band]], or [[The Oak Ridge Boys]]. "New Year's Evil" by Shadow it is".<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.dreadcentral.com/b-sides/40449/b-sides-rockin-in-2013-with-some-new-year-s-evil/|title=B-Sides: Rockin' in 2013 with Some New Year's Evil|last=Barton|first=Steve|date=January 13, 2013|website=[[Dread Central]]|language=en|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260802194425/https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/40449/b-sides-rockin-in-2013-with-some-new-year-s-evil/|archive-date=August 2, 2026|url-status=live}}</ref>

[[The Daily Dot|''TheDailyDot'']] wrote: "First off, this movie features the best New Year's Eve song ever made. Like the opening to ''[[Friday the 13th Part III]]'', it's just one of those rare original recordings for low-budget horror film that's a complete gem, and it should be an American staple of New Year's Eve celebrations".{{sfn|Frank|2000|pages=55–56}} James Jay Edwards, writing for the website Film Fracture, commented: {{quote|Of course, no rock and roll horror movie is complete without the music. The rock in ''New Year's Evil'' is supplied by two very real bands – Made in Japan and Shadow. Neither could really be called new wave or punk bands (Made in Japan is more of a The Knack-y power pop band and Shadow borders on 70's heavy metal), but both are obviously from the time period in which the film takes place. The theme song, done by Shadow and appropriately called "New Year's Evil", is incredibly infectious and plays over both the opening and closing credits as well as once during the course of the film. As if the song wasn't catchy enough, hearing it three times in ninety minutes means that there is no way that this song will not get stuck in the viewer's head. The incidental and mood music is fairly typical Moog synthesizer suspense and stinger fare, but it works well in the context of the new wave slasher film.  Between the rock and roll soundtrack and the electronic music score, ''New Year's Evil'''s music is one of the more memorable elements of the film.<ref name=FilmFracture>{{Cite web|last=Edwards|first=James Jay|url=https://www.filmfracture.com/cinema-fearite-presents-new-years-evil-dir-emmett-alston-1980/|title=Cinema Fearité presents New Year's Evil (Dir. Emmett Alston 1980) |work=Film Fracture|date=December 29, 2011|language=en-US|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260122050152/https://www.filmfracture.com/cinema-fearite-presents-new-years-evil-dir-emmett-alston-1980/|archive-date=January 22, 2026|url-status=live}}</ref>}}
