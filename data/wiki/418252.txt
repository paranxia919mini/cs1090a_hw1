'''''One-Eyed Jacks''''' is a 1961 American [[Epic film|epic]] [[Western film]] directed by [[Marlon Brando]] in his sole directorial credit, and starring Brando, [[Karl Malden]], [[Katy Jurado]], [[Ben Johnson (actor)|Ben Johnson]] and [[Pina Pellicer]]. Brando portrays a bandit seeking revenge on his former mentor who betrayed him (Malden).<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |title=One-Eyed Jacks |url=https://catalog.afi.com/Film/23780-ONE-EYED-JACKS?cxt=filmography |access-date=2026-05-22 |website=[[AFI Catalog of Feature Films]] |archive-date=August 29, 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230829092235/https://catalog.afi.com/Film/23780-ONE-EYED-JACKS?cxt=filmography |url-status=live }}</ref>

Loosely based on the 1956 novel ''The Authentic Death of Hendry Jones'', ''One-Eyed Jacks'' had a difficult production marked by clashes between Brando and multiple screenwriters and directors (including [[Stanley Kubrick]]), eventually taking over as director himself. Principal photography lasted twice its planned three months, and ran over budget. This was the last film shot in the [[VistaVision]] format until ''[[The Brutalist]]'' in 2024 and the last film projected in the format until ''[[One Battle After Another]]'' in 2025.<ref>{{cite news |last1=Tangcay |first1=Jazz |last2=Saavedra |first2=Mitch |url=https://variety.com/2024/artisans/news/the-brutalist-marble-quarry-active-mine-scene-1236256188/ |title='The Brutalist' Cinematographer Lol Crawley Breaks Down Filming in VistaVision in an Active Marble Mine in Tuscany |work=Variety |date=December 20, 2024 |access-date=April 29, 2025 |archive-date=March 11, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250311071805/https://variety.com/2024/artisans/news/the-brutalist-marble-quarry-active-mine-scene-1236256188/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

''One-Eyed Jacks'' was released by [[Paramount Pictures]] on March 30, 1961. It received mixed reviews and was a commercial disappointment, though its reputation has grown over the ensuing decades. It was nominated for an [[Academy Award for Best Cinematography|Academy Award for Best Cinematography, Color]], and Brando was nominated for a [[Directors Guild of America Award for Outstanding Directorial Achievement in Theatrical Feature Film|Directors Guild of America Award]]. It also won the [[Golden Shell]] at the 1961 [[San Sebastián International Film Festival]], with Pina Pellicer winning [[Silver Shell for Best Actress|Best Actress]].

In 2018, the film was selected for preservation in the United States [[National Film Registry]] by the [[Library of Congress]].<ref name=":1">{{cite web|url=https://www.cbsnews.com/news/jurassic-park-the-shining-my-fair-lady-2018-additions-to-library-of-congress-national-film-registry/|title='"Jurassic Park," "The Shining," "My Fair Lady" among latest additions to National Film Registry|website=CBS News|date=December 12, 2018|access-date=December 12, 2018|last=Morgan|first=David|archive-date=December 12, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181212121528/https://www.cbsnews.com/news/jurassic-park-the-shining-my-fair-lady-2018-additions-to-library-of-congress-national-film-registry/|url-status=live}}</ref> 

==Plot==

In 1880 [[Sonora, Mexico]], a bandit named Rio, his mentor Dad Longworth, their partner Doc rob a bank of two saddlebags of gold. Mexican ''[[rurales]]'' catch them celebrating in a cantina and kill Doc. Dad and Rio escape, but Dad leaves Rio to be taken by the ''rurales''. Rio is arrested and imprisoned. 

Five years later, Rio escapes and travels to [[Monterey, California]], where Dad has become [[sheriff]]. Rio plans to kill Dad and rob the bank in Monterey with his new partners Chico Modesto, Harvey Johnson and Bob Emory.

Plans are sidetracked when Rio falls in love with Dad's beautiful stepdaughter, Louisa. Rio takes advantage of a festival to spend the night with her on the beach. Dad tries to punish Louisa for what happened, but backs down after intervention by his wife, Maria. Instead, he traps Rio, whips him in public, and smashes his gun hand to make sure Rio will never be able to beat him in a gunfight. While recovering from his wounds, Rio struggles with his conflicting desires to love the girl and to get revenge on her stepfather. He decides to forgo vengeance, fetch Louisa, and leave town.

Emory and Johnson kill Chico and pull off the bank job without Rio's knowledge. The heist goes wrong, and a young girl is killed. Dad accuses Rio of the crime. Dad has one last private talk with Rio, again attempting to absolve himself for all he has done. Rio replies, "You're a one-eyed jack around here, Dad, but I've seen the other side of your face". Rio tells him he had been imprisoned for the last five years, but Dad calls it a lie.

[[File:One-Eyed Jacks 1961 (1).jpg|thumb|Louisa (Pina Pellicer) visits Rio (Marlon Brando) in jail]]
Louisa visits Rio in jail to tell him she is going to have his baby. He is then beaten by sadistic deputy Lon Dedrick, who desired and has been denied Louisa's affection. Maria confronts Dad and insists on being told the truth about the relationship between him and Rio, stating she knew something was wrong since the moment Rio arrived. She says she knows Dad wants to hang him purely out of guilt. Dad tells her she has no appreciation for everything he has done for her.

Louisa attempts to smuggle a [[Derringer]] to Rio, but she is discovered by Dedrick, who carries her out of the jail, leaving the gun on a table. While they are out, Rio is able to get hold of the pistol. Pointing the unloaded gun at Dedrick when he returns, Rio bluffs his way out of jail in a tense confrontation. Rio takes Dedrick's revolver, beats him unconscious, and locks him in a cell. As Rio is making his escape, he is spotted by Dad, riding into town. Under fire, in the final showdown Rio shoots Dad dead.

Rio and Louisa ride out to the dunes and say a sentimental farewell. Now a hunted man, Rio tells Louisa that he might go to [[Oregon]], but will return for her in the spring.

==Production==

=== Development ===
{{more citations needed section|date=February 2014}}
[[Rod Serling]], creator of ''[[The Twilight Zone (1959 TV series)|The Twilight Zone]]'' television series, wrote an adaptation of the 1956 novel ''The Authentic Death of Hendry Jones'' by [[Charles Neider]], at the request of producer Frank P. Rosenberg.<ref>{{Cite web |title=10 Things I Learned: One-Eyed Jacks |url=https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/5604-10-things-i-learned-one-eyed-jacks |access-date=2026-05-22 |website=The Criterion Collection |language=en |archive-date=May 24, 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240524145110/https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/5604-10-things-i-learned-one-eyed-jacks |url-status=live }}</ref> The book was a fictional treatment of the familiar [[Billy the Kid]] story, relocated from [[New Mexico]] to the [[Monterey Peninsula]] in [[California]]. The adaptation was rejected.

Rosenberg next hired [[Sam Peckinpah]], who finished his first script on 11 November 1957.<ref name="farout">{{cite web|url=https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/when-marlon-brando-made-short-work-legendary-director/|title=When Marlon Brando made short work of a legendary director: "He fired me"|first=Michael|last=Gordon|website=Far Out|date=20 November 2024|access-date=16 December 2024|archive-date=December 16, 2024|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241216151011/https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/when-marlon-brando-made-short-work-legendary-director/|url-status=live}}</ref> Marlon Brando's Pennebacker Productions had paid $40,000 for the rights to ''Authentic Death'' and then signed a contract with [[Stanley Kubrick]] to direct for [[Paramount Pictures]]. Kubrick stepped down from directing the film in November 1958 just two weeks before starting production, reportedly to get ''[[Lolita (1962 film)|Lolita]]'' into production.<ref name="farout"/><ref name="1966 Kubrick Interview by Jeremy Bernstein">{{cite web |last1=Bernstein |first1=Jeremy |title=Stanley Kubrick 1966 Interview |url=https://youtube.com/OQOMNF27kLg?t=2432 |website=YouTube |access-date=25 January 2019 }}{{Dead link|date=August 2025 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref><ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Daily Variety]]|date=November 20, 1958|page=1|title=Kubrick Quits Brando 'Jacks'}}</ref> Karl Malden was offered the directorial role but declined and Brando volunteered to direct instead.<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Daily Variety]]|date=November 20, 1958|page=2|title=Just For Variety|last=Archerd|first=Army|author-link=Army Archerd}}</ref> Peckinpah handed in a revised screenplay on 6 May 1959. Brando later fired Peckinpah and hired [[Calder Willingham]] to further revise the film's script, but he too was eventually fired. [[Guy Trosper]] was brought on as a final replacement.<ref name="farout"/>

The movie ultimately bore little resemblance to the Neider novel. At various times, the two credited screenwriters and the uncredited Peckinpah have claimed (or had claimed for them) a majority of the responsibility for the film. When [[Karl Malden]] was asked who really wrote the story, he said: "There is one answer to your question—Marlon Brando, a genius in our time."<ref>{{cite web| url= http://www.towntopics.com/jul0809/book.php| title= Karl Malden and Marlon Brando in One-Eyed Jacks: "We Had the Very Best of Each Other"| first= Stuart| last= Mitchner| date= July 8, 2009| publisher= [[Town Topics (newspaper)|Town Topics]]| location= Princeton, New Jersey| access-date= 2014-02-16| archive-date= October 11, 2014| archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20141011055648/http://www.towntopics.com/jul0809/book.php| url-status= dead}}</ref>

=== Casting ===
[[File:Pina Pellicer and Marlon Brando publicity photo (1961).jpg|thumb|Pina Pellicer and Marlon Brando behind the scenes of the film]]
[[Spencer Tracy]] was Stanley Kubrick's preference for the role of Dad Longworth, but Marlon Brando had already given the role to his friend Karl Malden, who was on the payroll of  Pennebaker. 

=== Filming ===
Shooting took place in various locations across California and Mexico.<ref name=":0" />

The movie went vastly over its $2 million budget, which was blamed on Brando's perfectionism as a director. Scheduled for a three-month shoot, principal photography on ''One Eyed Jacks'' took six months at a cost $6 million, while Brando shot 1 million feet (304,800 meters) of film.<ref name="Criterion">{{cite web |last1=Hampton |first1=Howard |title=One-Eyed Jacks: Zen Nihilism |url=https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/4312-one-eyed-jacks-zen-nihilism |website=criterion.com |publisher=Criterion Collection |access-date=April 30, 2019 |archive-date=November 30, 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231130040841/https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/4312-one-eyed-jacks-zen-nihilism |url-status=live }}</ref> Shooting began in 1958, but the film was not released until 1961.

Brando had shot and printed a total of five hours of additional footage, which made the editing process long and laborious; some of the unused footage was later destroyed. Among the excised footage was a romantic subplot between Brando and a "niece of a Chinese fisherman", played by [[Lisa Lu]].<ref name=":0" /> 

Brando's initial director's cut ran 4 hours and 42 minutes, which Paramount demanded be reduced. Eventually, Brando left during post-production due to exhaustion, and the final 141 minute cut was assembled without his involvement.<ref name=":0" /><ref name="tcmart">{{cite web|url=https://www.tcm.com/this-month/article/88922 |title=One-Eyed Jacks |publisher=Turner Classic Movies |access-date=2014-02-16}}</ref> Brando did return to reshoot the film's ending – originally Louisa died in the climactic shootout, but Brando decided the film should have a more upbeat ending.<ref name=":0" /> He did not direct another film in his later years, but he did continue to act. In a 1975 ''[[Rolling Stone]]'' interview, Brando said of directing, "You work yourself to death. You're the first one up in the morning... I mean, we shot that thing on the run, you know, you make up the dialogue the scene before, improvising, and your brain is going crazy".<ref>{{cite book|last=Baxter|first=John|title=Stanley Kubrick: A Biography|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=e-h1QgAACAAJ|year=1997|publisher=HarperCollins|isbn=978-0-00-638445-8|page=122}}</ref>

The film was [[Paramount Pictures]]' last feature released in [[VistaVision]]. Cinematographer [[Charles Lang]] received an [[Academy Awards|Academy Award]] nomination in the Best Cinematography, Color category that year. Upon release, it made little money, leading to a string of unsuccessful films for Brando. It was filmed at Pebble Beach and Pfeiffer Beach.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://filmmonterey.org/film/one-eyed-jacks/#:~:text=Western%20drama%20about%20an%20outlaw,Pfeiffer%20Beach%20in%20Big%20Sur|title=ONE-EYED JACKS|publisher=Montery County Film Commission|access-date=2024-01-26|archive-date=January 27, 2024|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240127030022/https://filmmonterey.org/film/one-eyed-jacks/#:~:text=Western%20drama%20about%20an%20outlaw,Pfeiffer%20Beach%20in%20Big%20Sur|url-status=live}}</ref>

==Release==
[[File:Pina Pellicer and Marlon Brando publicity photo One-Eyed Jacks (1961).jpg|thumb|upright|Promotional photograph for the film featuring main stars, Pina Pellicer and Marlon Brando]]
The film was released on March 30, 1961, in New York City.<ref name="nytbosley"/> 

The film was selected for screening as part of the Cannes Classics section at the [[2016 Cannes Film Festival]].<ref name="Classics">{{cite web |url=http://www.festival-cannes.fr/en/article/62136.html |title=Cannes Classics 2016 |access-date=21 April 2016 |date=20 April 2016 |work=Cannes Film Festival |archive-date=February 10, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170210012746/http://www.festival-cannes.fr/en/article/62136.html |url-status=live }}</ref> The Cannes screening was that of a 4K restoration supervised by [[Martin Scorsese]], [[Steven Spielberg]], and [[The Film Foundation]].<ref>{{Cite press release|url=http://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/universal-pictures-and-the-film-foundation-restore-marlon-brandos-one-eyed-jacks-for-cannes-film-festival-world-premiere-300269211.html|title=Universal Pictures and The Film Foundation restore Marlon Brando's One-Eyed Jacks for Cannes Film Festival world premiere|last=Universal Pictures|website=Prnewswire.com|access-date=2016-09-08|archive-date=September 16, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160916235535/http://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/universal-pictures-and-the-film-foundation-restore-marlon-brandos-one-eyed-jacks-for-cannes-film-festival-world-premiere-300269211.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Home media and restoration ===
The film fell into the public domain and for years was only available via numerous low-quality budget reissues on [[VHS]] and [[DVD]], along with the occasional official release by Paramount Home Video. In 2016, work was completed on a "New [[4K resolution|4K]] digital restoration, undertaken by [[Universal Pictures]] in partnership with [[The Film Foundation]] and in consultation with filmmakers [[Martin Scorsese]] and [[Steven Spielberg]]".<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.criterion.com/films/28979-one-eyed-jacks|title=Criterion Collection: One-Eyed Jacks|work=criterion.com|access-date=25 Jan 2017|archive-date=February 2, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170202023406/https://www.criterion.com/films/28979-one-eyed-jacks|url-status=live}}</ref> This restoration was issued on [[Blu-ray]] and DVD in November 2016 by [[the Criterion Collection]] in the US, and in June 2017 by [[Arrow Video]] in the UK.

==Reception==

=== Critical response ===
''One-Eyed Jacks'' received mixed reviews from critics. On the review aggregator [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film has a 67% approval rating based on 18 reviews.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/one_eyed_jacks|title=One-Eyed Jacks - Rotten Tomatoes|access-date=April 30, 2026|publisher=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]|archive-date=January 18, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220118194029/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/one_eyed_jacks|url-status=live}}</ref>

[[Bosley Crowther]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'', favorably influenced by Brando's efforts, noted: "Directed and played with the kind of vicious style that Mr. Brando has put into so many of his skulking, scabrous roles. Realism is redolent in them, as it is in many details of the film. But, at the same time, it is curiously surrounded by elements of creamed-cliché romance and a kind of pictorial extravagance that you usually see in South Sea island films."<ref name="nytbosley">{{cite web |last=Crowther |first=Bosley |url=https://www.nytimes.com/movie/review?res=9D05E3DC1539EE32A25752C3A9659C946091D6CF |title=One Eyed Jacks - Screen: Brando Stars and Directs:'One Eyed Jacks' in Premiere at Capitol |work=The New York Times |date=March 31, 1961 |access-date=2014-02-16 |archive-date=March 9, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140309123709/http://www.nytimes.com/movie/review?res=9D05E3DC1539EE32A25752C3A9659C946091D6CF |url-status=live }}</ref>

''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'', on the other hand, wrote: "It is an oddity of this film that both its strength and its weakness lie in the area of characterization. Brando's concept calls, above all, for depth of character, for human figures endowed with overlapping good and bad sides to their nature."<ref>{{cite web| url= https://variety.com/1960/film/reviews/one-eyed-jacks-1200419927/| title= Review: 'One-Eyed Jacks'| publisher= Variety| year= 1961| access-date= 2014-02-16| archive-date= March 4, 2014| archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20140304222604/http://variety.com/1960/film/reviews/one-eyed-jacks-1200419927/| url-status= live}}</ref> [[Dave Kehr]] of ''[[The Chicago Reader]]'' wrote: "There is a strong Freudian pull to the situation (the partner's name is "Dad") that is more ritualized than dramatized: the most memorable scenes have a fierce masochistic intensity, as if Brando were taking the opportunity to punish himself for some unknown crime."<ref>{{cite web |last=Kehr |first=Dave |url=http://www.chicagoreader.com/chicago/one-eyed-jacks/Film?oid=1150404 |title=One-Eyed Jacks |date=25 October 1985 |location=Chicago |publisher=Chicago Reader |access-date=2014-02-16 |archive-date=February 25, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140225232400/http://www.chicagoreader.com/chicago/one-eyed-jacks/Film?oid=1150404 |url-status=dead }}</ref>

==In popular culture==
"One-Eyed Jacks" is the name of a brothel in the TV series ''[[Twin Peaks]]'' created by [[David Lynch]] and [[Mark Frost]]. The film is mentioned in dialogue between characters [[Donna Hayward]] and [[Audrey Horne]]. Horne asks Hayward if she has heard of "One-Eyed Jacks" and Hayward responds, "Isn't that that Western with Marlon Brando?".<ref>{{cite book| url= https://books.google.com/books?id=eHQd9M569k8C&q=%22Isn%27t+that+that+Western+with+Marlon+Brando%22&pg=PA162 | title= The 100 Greatest Western Movies of All Time | first= Philip |last=Armour | publisher= Globe Pequot | year=2011| isbn=978-0762769377| page= 162| access-date= 2014-02-16}}</ref>
