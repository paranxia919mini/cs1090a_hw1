'''''Love in the Afternoon''''' ({{langx|fr|'''L'Amour, l'après-midi'''}}; released in North America as '''''Chloe in the Afternoon''''') is a 1972 French [[Romance film|romantic]] [[comedy-drama]] film written and directed by [[Éric Rohmer]]. It is the sixth and final installment in Rohmer's ''[[Six Moral Tales]]'' series.

An English-language remake starring [[Chris Rock]], titled ''[[I Think I Love My Wife]]'', was released in 2007. Singer-songwriter [[St. Vincent (musician)|St. Vincent]] named the opening song of her 2011 album, ''[[Strange Mercy]]'', after the film.<ref>{{cite web|author=Ryan Dombal|date=13 September 2011|title=St. Vincent: Strange Mercy|url=https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/15813-strange-mercy/|website=Pitchfork}}</ref>

==Plot==
Frédéric, a young and successful partner in a Paris business firm, is happily married to Hélène, an English teacher, and father to one child with another on the way. Still, something eats away at him. While going through his day, Frédéric begins to ponder the times before he was married, when he was free to be with any woman he wanted and could feel the deep satisfaction of anticipation while he chased them. At one point, he has an elaborate fantasy in which he possesses a magical amulet that causes all women to bow to his will (the sequence features actresses from previous installments in Rohmer's "[[Six Moral Tales]]" series). These thoughts do not distress Frédéric, though, as he sees them as a reflection of how true his love for his wife is.

One day, Chloé, a woman from Frédéric's past, stops by his office. Chloé had once been the girlfriend of an old friend of Frédéric, and she caused this friend a great deal of grief. At first, Frédéric believes Chloé only wants cash and company, but over time, as she tries a series of jobs to attempt to find some stability in her life—to the increasing amusement of the secretaries in Frédéric's office—the two begin spending afternoons together, talking of many things Frédéric finds himself unable to talk about with Hélène. He enjoys the pleasures of having an attractive mistress without the guilt of adultery, while she has a man who will do whatever she wants without needing sex.

After the birth of Frédéric's second child, Chloé decides a child could give focus to her aimless existence, and tells Frédéric that he will be the father. She has no desire to be tied down by marriage, so she sees it as all-but-irrelevant that Frédéric already has a wife and family. Although Frédéric's attraction to Chloé continues to build, he is always able to stop himself before giving in and sleeping with her. Then, one afternoon, he arrives at her apartment to find her in the shower. Emerging, she asks him to towel her dry, which he does, and then she invites him to bed. He begins to get undressed, but stops when he sees his reflection in the mirror with his face sticking out of the neck of his [[Polo neck|turtleneck]], as it reminds him of a game he had played with his daughter. Leaving Chloé naked and waiting, Frédéric sneaks away without a word and goes home to Hélène, finding her alone. Husband and wife talk awkwardly for a bit, struggling to find the right words to express their feelings, and Hélène breaks down in tears. Frédéric comforts her, and they go to their bedroom.

==Reception==
On the [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], 87% of 23 critics' reviews of the film are positive; the site's "critics consensus" reads: "Eric Rohmer's ''Chloe in the Afternoon'' doesn't need sparkly cinematic dross to discover unspoken, universal truths about relationships and love through filmmaking".<ref>{{cite web |title=Chloe in the Afternoon |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |publisher=[[Fandango Media]] |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/chloe_in_the_afternoon  |access-date=27 September 2025}}</ref>

[[Roger Ebert]] of the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'' gave the film 4 out 4 stars, calling it the best of Rohmer's "[[Éric Rohmer#1962–1972: Six Moral Tales and television work|Six Moral Tales]]" series, and adding:<blockquote>It is also the most fully rounded, lacking the one-dimensional tone of some of his earlier tales. It's as if he were striking notes in the previous works, and is now bringing them all together into a chord; the final scene in ''Chloe'' is his last comment on the series, and Rohmer is telling us to, for god's sake, stop playing games and embrace each other with honesty.<ref>{{cite web |date=28 December 1972 |first=Roger |last=Ebert |author-link=Roger Ebert |title=Chloe in the Afternoon |website=[[Chicago Sun-Times]] |url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/chloe-in-the-afternoon-1972 |access-date=30 October 2020}}</ref></blockquote>

[[Vincent Canby]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' gave the film a positive review, writing: "because Mr. Rohmer keeps his focus short, clear and precise, one sees deeply into the lives of his characters without the sort of pretentious distortions of most movies that deal in metaphors".<ref>{{cite web |date=30 September 1972 |last=Canby |first=Vincent |author-link=Vincent Canby |title=Film Festival: Eric Rohmer's 'Chloe in the Afternoon' (Published 1972) |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1972/09/30/archives/film-festival-eric-rohmers-chloe-in-the-afternoon.html |website=[[The New York Times]]}}</ref>

In contrast, [[Pauline Kael]] wrote that the film "had evaporated a half hour after I saw it. It’s about as forgettable as a movie can be."<ref>{{Cite web |date=June 5, 2019 |title=15 of Pauline Kael's Greatest Takes |url=https://www.indiewire.com/gallery/pauline-kael-best-reviews/mpw-119822/ |website=IndieWire}}</ref>
