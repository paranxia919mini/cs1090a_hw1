'''''All the President's Men''''' is a 1976 American [[Biographical film|biographical]] [[neo-noir]] [[political thriller]] film directed by [[Alan J. Pakula]], with a screenplay by [[William Goldman]], about the [[Watergate scandal]] that brought down the [[Presidency of Richard Nixon|presidency]] of [[Richard Nixon]]. Based on the [[All the President's Men|1974 non-fiction book]] by journalists [[Carl Bernstein]] and [[Bob Woodward]], it stars [[Robert Redford]] and [[Dustin Hoffman]] as Woodward and Bernstein, respectively, as they investigate the scandal for ''[[The Washington Post]]''. It was produced by [[Walter Coblenz]] for Redford's [[Wildwood Enterprises, Inc|Wildwood Enterprises]].

The film garnered critical acclaim upon its release, and was nominated in multiple [[Academy Awards|Oscar]], [[Golden Globe Awards|Golden Globe]] and [[British Academy Film Awards|BAFTA]] categories; [[Jason Robards]] won the [[Academy Award for Best Supporting Actor]] for his portrayal of [[Ben Bradlee]]. In 2010, it was selected for preservation in the United States [[National Film Registry]] by the [[Library of Congress]] as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".<ref>{{Cite web|title=Hollywood Blockbusters, Independent Films and Shorts Selected for 2010 National Film Registry|url=https://www.loc.gov/item/prn-10-273/2010-national-film-registry-announced/2010-12-28/|website=Library of Congress|access-date=2020-05-18|archive-date=2017-02-28|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170228035318/https://www.loc.gov/item/prn-10-273/2010-national-film-registry-announced/2010-12-28/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|title=Complete National Film Registry Listing |url=https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-film-preservation-board/film-registry/complete-national-film-registry-listing/|website=Library of Congress|access-date=2020-05-18|archive-date=2016-10-31|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161031213743/https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-film-preservation-board/film-registry/complete-national-film-registry-listing/|url-status=live}}</ref>

== Plot ==
On June 17, 1972, [[Frank Wills (security guard)|Frank Wills]], a security guard at the [[Watergate complex]] finds a door's bolt taped over to prevent it from locking. He calls the police, who find and arrest five burglars in the [[Democratic National Committee]] headquarters within the complex. The next morning, ''[[The Washington Post]]'' assigns new reporter [[Bob Woodward]] to the local courthouse to cover the story, which is considered of minor importance.

Woodward learns that the [[White House Plumbers|five men]]—[[James W. McCord Jr.]] and four Cuban-Americans from Miami—possessed electronic bugging equipment, and are represented by a high-priced "country club" attorney. At the arraignment, McCord identifies himself in court as having recently left the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] (CIA), and the others are also revealed to have CIA ties. Woodward connects the burglars to [[E. Howard Hunt]], an employee of [[President of the United States|President]] [[Richard Nixon]]'s [[White House Counsel]] [[Charles Colson]], and formerly of the CIA.

[[Carl Bernstein]], another ''Post'' reporter, is assigned to cover the Watergate story with Woodward. The two young men are reluctant partners but work well together. Executive editor [[Ben Bradlee|Benjamin Bradlee]] believes that their work lacks reliable sources and is not worthy of the ''Post'''s front page, but he encourages further investigation.

Woodward contacts a senior government official, an anonymous source he has used before and refers to as "[[Deep Throat (Watergate)|Deep Throat]]". Communicating secretly, using a flag placed in a balcony flowerpot to signal meetings, they meet at night in an underground [[multistorey car park|parking garage]]. Deep Throat speaks vaguely and with metaphors, avoiding substantial facts about the Watergate break-in, but promises to keep Woodward on the right path to the truth, advising Woodward to "[[follow the money]]".

Woodward and Bernstein connect the five burglars to corrupt activities involving campaign contributions to Nixon's [[Committee for the Re-Election of the President|Committee to Re-elect the President]] (CRP or CREEP). This includes a check for $25,000 paid by [[Kenneth H. Dahlberg]], who Miami authorities identified when investigating the Miami-based burglars. However, Bradlee and others at the ''Post'' still doubt the investigation and its dependence on sources such as Deep Throat, wondering why the Nixon administration should break the law when the president is almost certain to defeat his opponent, [[Democratic Party (United States)|Democratic]] nominee [[George McGovern]].

Through former CREEP treasurer [[Hugh W. Sloan Jr.]], Woodward and Bernstein connect a [[slush fund]] of hundreds of thousands of dollars to White House chief of staff [[H. R. Haldeman]]—"the second most important man in this country"—and to former attorney general [[John N. Mitchell]], now head of CREEP. They learn that CREEP was financing a "[[ratfucking]]" campaign to sabotage Democratic presidential candidates a year before the Watergate burglary, when Nixon was lagging [[Edmund Muskie]] in the polls.

While Bradlee's demand for thoroughness compels the reporters to obtain other sources to confirm the Haldeman connection, the White House issues a [[non-denial denial]] of the ''Post'''s above-the-fold story. Bradlee continues to encourage investigation.

Woodward again meets secretly with Deep Throat and demands that he be less evasive. Very reluctantly, Deep Throat reveals that Haldeman masterminded the Watergate break-in and [[cover-up]]. He also states the cover-up was not only intended to camouflage the CREEP involvement, but also to hide "covert operations" involving "the entire U.S. intelligence community", including the CIA and [[Federal Bureau of Investigation]] (FBI). He warns Woodward and Bernstein that their lives, and those of others, are in danger. When the two relay this information to Bradlee and tell him of the depth of the conspiracy, Bradlee realizes that a constitutional crisis is coming, but tells them to move forward with the story.

During the [[second inauguration of Richard Nixon]] on January 20, 1973, Bernstein and Woodward type the full story, while a television in the newsroom shows Nixon taking the oath of office for his second term as president. A montage of Watergate-related teletype headlines from the following years is shown, ending with the report of [[Nixon's resignation]] and the [[inauguration of Gerald Ford]] on August 9, 1974.

== Differences from the book ==
Unlike the book, the film covers only the first seven months of the Watergate scandal, from the time of the break-in to [[Second inauguration of Richard Nixon|Nixon's second inauguration]] on January 20, 1973.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://thatwasnotinthebook.com/diff/all-the-president%27s-men|title=Differences between All the President's Men Book vs Movie|website=thatwasnotinthebook.com|access-date=2018-12-25|archive-date=2018-12-25|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181225080901/http://thatwasnotinthebook.com/diff/all-the-president%27s-men|url-status=live}}</ref> The film introduced the catchphrase "[[follow the money]]" in relation to the case, which did not appear in the book or in any Watergate documentation.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://freakonomics.com/2011/09/23/follow-the-money/|title=Follow the Money|last=Shapiro|first=Fred|date=2011-09-23|website=Freakonomics|language=en|access-date=2018-12-25|archive-date=2018-12-25|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181225125942/http://freakonomics.com/2011/09/23/follow-the-money/|url-status=live}}</ref>

== Production ==
{{Watergate}}

Redford began asking about the Watergate break-in while promoting [[The Candidate (1972 film)|''The Candidate'']], then read Woodward and Bernstein's Watergate stories in ''[[The Washington Post]]'', while waiting to start filming ''[[The Way We Were]].'' Redford first spoke with Woodward in November 1972, after the reporters' well-publicized mistake about Hugh Sloan implicating Haldeman in his testimony to the Watergate grand jury.<ref name="Legacy">[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UVrGZSxpBEA ''The Legacy of "All the President's Men"'' with Robert Redford, Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein (April 21, 2011)] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190812032051/https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UVrGZSxpBEA |date=2019-08-12 }} [[LBJ Presidential Library]] on YouTube</ref>

Redford bought the rights to Woodward and Bernstein's book in 1974 for $450,000, with the idea to adapt it into a film with a budget of $5&nbsp;million.<ref name= "shales">{{cite news | last1 = Shales | first1 = Tom | first2 = Tom | last2 = Zito | first3 = Jeannette | last3 = Smyth | title = When Worlds Collide: Lights! Camera! Egos! | newspaper = [[The Washington Post]] | date = April 11, 1975 | url = https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/longterm/movies/features/dcmovies/postinfilm.htm | access-date = 2008-11-12| archive-date = 2008-07-24| archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20080724134651/http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/longterm/movies/features/dcmovies/postinfilm.htm | url-status = live }}</ref> [[Ben Bradlee]], executive editor of ''The Washington Post,'' realized that the film was going to be produced regardless of his approval, and believed it made "more sense to try to influence it factually".<ref name= "shales"/> He hoped the film would show that newspapers "strive very hard for responsibility".<ref name="shales"/>

Redford hired [[William Goldman]] to write the script in 1974. Goldman has said that Woodward was extremely helpful to him but that Bernstein was not. Goldman wrote that his crucial decision regarding the screenplay's structure was to discard the second half of the book.{{sfn|Goldman|1989|p=235}} After he delivered his first draft in August 1974, [[Warner Bros. Pictures|Warner Bros.]] agreed to finance the film.

Redford was not happy with Goldman's first draft.<ref name= "shales"/> Woodward and Bernstein read it and also did not like it. Redford asked for their suggestions, but Bernstein and his then-girlfriend, writer [[Nora Ephron]], wrote their own draft. Redford showed this draft to Goldman, suggesting that it might contain some material he could integrate, but Goldman later called Redford's acceptance of the Bernstein-Ephron draft a "gutless betrayal".{{sfn|Goldman|1989|p=240}} Redford later expressed dissatisfaction with the Bernstein-Ephron draft, saying, "A lot of it was sophomoric and way off the beat."<ref name= "shales"/> According to Goldman, "In what they wrote, Bernstein was sure catnip to the ladies."{{sfn|Goldman|1989|p=240}} He also said that one of Bernstein and Ephron's scenes was included in the final film; a bit in which Bernstein deceives a secretary in an attempt to see someone, which was not factually true.

[[Alan J. Pakula]] was hired to direct and requested rewrites from Goldman. In a 2011 biography, Redford claimed that he and Pakula held all-day sessions working on the script.{{Citation needed|date=August 2024}} Pakula spent hours interviewing editors and reporters, taking notes of their comments.

In 2011, Richard Stayton, ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' drama critic<ref name="latimes/1990-08-09-vl-274">{{cite news |last1=Everett |first1=Todd |title=BACKSTAGE: PLAYWRIGHT : A Role Reversal : Richard Stayton quit his job as a newspaper drama critic to stage plays of his own creation. |url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-08-09-vl-274-story.html |access-date=5 August 2025 |work=[[Los Angeles Times]] |date=9 August 1990}}</ref> and playwright,<ref name="latimes/1990-08-09-vl-274"/> wrote an article for the [[Writers Guild of America West]] magazine, ''[[Writers Guild of America West|Written By]]''<ref>
*{{cite web|title=Written By|url=http://bluetoad.com/publication/?i=67460|work=magazine publication of|publisher=[[Writers Guild of America West#Magazine|WGAw]]|access-date=2011-11-06|archive-date=2011-09-06|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110906010215/http://bluetoad.com/publication/?i=67460|url-status=live}}
*[https://web.archive.org/web/20200801102851/https://cdn.coverstand.com/4973/67460/67460.1.pdf PDF]
</ref> as its editor, following his comparison of many drafts of the script, including the final production draft. He concluded that Redford's and Pakula's contributions were not significant, that Goldman was properly credited as the writer, and that the final draft had "William Goldman's distinct signature on each page".<ref>{{cite web|last=Stayton|first=Richard|title=Fade In – From the Vault, an archival reprint Feb/March 2018|url=https://www.mydigitalpublication.com/publication/?m=&l=1&i=473620&p=56|publisher=[[Writers Guild of America West#Magazine|Written By]] magazine (– original publish date, April/May 2011)|access-date=2021-04-11|archive-date=2021-04-11|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210411094114/https://www.mydigitalpublication.com/publication/?m=&l=1&i=473620&p=56|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Casting ===
Redford first selected [[Al Pacino]] to play Bernstein, but later decided that [[Dustin Hoffman]] was a better fit for the role.<ref>{{cite magazine|first=Michael J.|last=Gaynor|magazine=Washingtonian|title=All the President's Men: An Oral History|date=3 April 2016|access-date=22 September 2025|url=https://washingtonian.com/2016/04/03/all-the-presidents-men-movie-oral-history/}}</ref>

[[Jason Robards]] was always Redford's choice to play [[Ben Bradlee]]. However, Bradlee had recommended [[George C. Scott]] for the role, and he was somewhat unimpressed when Robards visited the ''Post'' offices to develop a feel for the newsroom. In advance of the shoot, Bradlee told Robards, "Just don't make me look like an asshole." At first, Pakula was worried that Robards could not carry Bradlee's easy elegance and command authority. [[Karl Malden]], [[Hal Holbrook]] (who would ultimately play Deep Throat), [[John Forsythe]], [[Leslie Nielsen]], [[Henry Fonda]], [[Richard Widmark]], [[Christopher Plummer]], [[Anthony Quinn]], [[Gene Hackman]], [[Burt Lancaster]], [[Robert Stack]], [[Robert Mitchum]] and [[Telly Savalas]] were also considered for the role.<ref>{{cite book | first =Jeff| last = Himmelman | title =Yours in Truth: A Personal Portrait of Ben Bradlee, Legendary Editor of The Washington Post | publisher = Random House Publishing Group | year= 2012 | url = https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0812980565/ | isbn=978-0-679-60364-1 }}</ref>

Character actor [[Martin Balsam]] played managing editor [[Howard Simons]]. According to Bradlee, Simons felt that his involvement with the real story was greatly diminished in the script.

Bradlee teased ''Post'' publisher [[Katharine Graham]] about who would play her in the film. "Names like [[Katharine Hepburn]], [[Lauren Bacall]] and [[Patricia Neal]] were tossed out — by us — to make her feel good," Bradlee said. "And names like [[Edna May Oliver]] or [[Marie Dressler]], if it felt like teasing time. And then her role was dropped from the final script, half to her relief."<ref>{{cite web|url=http://liveswelivedanhagen.blogspot.com/2014/10/ben-bradlee-iconic-editor.html|title=Ben Bradlee: Iconic Editor|access-date=2018-09-12|archive-date=2018-09-09|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180909215255/http://liveswelivedanhagen.blogspot.com/2014/10/ben-bradlee-iconic-editor.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

Redford and Hoffman divided [[Billing (performing arts)|top billing]], with Redford billed above Hoffman in the posters and trailers, and Hoffman billed above Redford in the film itself (in precisely the same manner in which [[James Stewart]] and [[John Wayne]] had divided top billing for [[John Ford]]'s ''[[The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance]]'' in 1962).

=== Filming ===
Hoffman and Redford visited ''The Washington Post''{{'s}} offices for months, attending news conferences and conducting research for their roles.<ref name= "shales"/> Because the ''Post'' denied the production permission to shoot in its newsroom, set designers took measurements of the newspaper's offices and took many photographs. Boxes of trash were gathered and transported to sets recreating the newsroom on two soundstages in [[Hollywood, Los Angeles|Hollywood]]'s [[The Burbank Studios|Burbank Studios]] for $200,000. The filmmakers went to great lengths for accuracy and authenticity, including making replicas of outdated phone books.<ref name= "shales"/> Nearly 200 desks were purchased for $500 each from the same firm that had sold desks to the ''Post'' in 1971. The desks were painted the same color as those in the newsroom. The production was supplied with a brick from the main lobby of the ''Post'' so that it could be duplicated in fiberglass for the set. Principal photography began May 12, 1975, in Washington, D.C.<ref name= "shales"/>

== Release ==
''All the President's Men'' was initially assigned an [[Motion Picture Association film rating system#Ratings|R rating]] by the [[Motion Picture Association|Motion Picture Association of America]] (MPAA) due to multiple uses of the word "[[fuck]]" and its derivatives.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Vaughn |first=Stephen |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=B7MkaxKGTGAC&pg=PA48 |title=Freedom and Entertainment: Rating the Movies in an Age of New Media |date=2006 |publisher=Cambridge University Press |isbn=978-0-521-85258-6 |pages=47–51 |language=en |access-date=2023-10-26}}</ref> [[Warner Bros. Pictures]] and [[Robert Redford]] appealed the decision, arguing that the film's subject matter and historical importance warranted a lower rating.<ref>{{cite news |date=1976-03-26 |title='President's Men' Wins Rating Appeal |newspaper=[[The Tampa Tribune]] |page=42}}</ref> The MPAA Appeals Board, requiring a two-thirds majority to overturn a rating, agreed and reclassified the film as PG (the PG-13 rating did not exist at the time, as it was created in 1984).<ref>{{cite news |date=1976-03-21 |title=Film Ratings: Out Of The Closet |newspaper=[[Arizona Daily Star]] |page=63}}</ref> MPAA president [[Jack Valenti]] later stated that the film would have received a G rating if not for the profanity. According to MPAA official Richard Heffner, the Appeals Board considered the language to be contextually appropriate given the film's journalistic and educational significance. The decision exemplified the MPAA's ability to exercise discretion in its ratings process, particularly for films regarded as culturally or historically important.

== Reception ==

=== Box office ===
''All the President's Men'' grossed $7,016,001 from 604 theaters in its first week, placing it [[List of 1976 box office number-one films in the United States|atop the U.S. box office]].<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|title=First Week In Only 604 Theatres (advertisement)|date=April 21, 1976|page=8}}</ref><ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|title=50 Top-Grossing Films|date=April 21, 1976|page=11}}</ref> It eventually grossed $70.6&nbsp;million at the box office.<ref name="mojo" />

=== Critical reception ===
''All the President's Men'' has received near-universal acclaim from many film critics, both on its release and in the years immediately following it. On the [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film holds an approval rating of 95% based on 76 reviews. The website's critics consensus reads, "A taut, solidly acted paean to the benefits of a free press and the dangers of unchecked power, made all the more effective by its origins in real-life events."<ref>{{cite web|website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |url=http://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/all_the_presidents_men|title=All the President's Men|access-date=August 7, 2026}}</ref> On [[Metacritic]], which assigns a [[weighted average]] score out of 100 to reviews from mainstream critics, the film received an average score of 84, based on reviews from 18 critics, indicating "universal acclaim".<ref>{{Metacritic film|title=All the President's Men}}</ref>

==== Initial reviews ====
At the time of the film's release, [[Roger Ebert]] of the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'' awarded the film 3½ stars out of 4, writing: "It provides the most observant study of working journalists we're ever likely to see in a feature film. And it succeeds brilliantly in suggesting the mixture of exhilaration, paranoia, self-doubt, and courage that permeated ''The Washington Post'' as its two young reporters went after a presidency."<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/all-the-presidents-men-1976 | title=All the President's Men | first=Roger | last=Ebert | author-link=Roger Ebert | website=RogerEbert.com | date=January 1, 1976 | access-date=January 15, 2019 | archive-date=November 6, 2018 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181106005005/https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/all-the-presidents-men-1976 | url-status=live }}</ref> [[Variety (magazine)|''Variety'']] praised the "ingenious direction [...] and scripting" that overcame the difficult lack of drama that a story about reporters running down a story might otherwise have.<ref>{{cite magazine | url=https://variety.com/1975/film/reviews/all-the-president-s-men-1200423767/ | title=All the President's Men | magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] | date=December 31, 1975 | access-date=January 15, 2019 | archive-date=November 6, 2018 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181106005003/https://variety.com/1975/film/reviews/all-the-president-s-men-1200423767/ | url-status=live }}</ref> [[Gene Siskel]] gave the film four stars. In his ''[[Chicago Tribune]]'' review, he wrote, "Director Alan J. Pakula duplicates the surprise and suspense of his ''[[Klute]]'' while avoiding the overstatement and pandering paranoia of his more recent political thriller, ''[[The Parallax View]]''. Pakula's greatest achievement is the way he unobtrusively weaves outside events into narrative of the reporters' story."<ref>{{cite web | url=https://chicagotribune.newspapers.com/article/chicago-tribune-gene-siskel-movie-review/53071992/ | title='All the President's Men: Excitement from pillar to Post | first=Gene | last=Siskel | author-link=Gene Siskel | work=Chicago Tribune | date=April 9, 1976 | access-date=2023-11-15}}</ref> Siskel also named it the best film of 1976 on his year-end list.

[[Vincent Canby]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' wrote, "Newspapers and newspapermen have long been favorite subjects for movie makers—a surprising number of whom are former newspapermen, yet not until ''All The President's Men'', the riveting screen adaptation of the Watergate book by Carl Bernstein and Bob Woodward, has any film come remotely close to being an accurate picture of American journalism at its best."<ref>{{cite web |url=https://archive.org/details/TheNewYorkTimes1976USAEnglish/Apr%2008%201976%2C%20The%20New%20York%20Times%2C%20%2343174%2C%20USA%20%28en%29/page/n41/mode/2up |first=Vincent |last=Canby |author-link=Vincent Canby |title=All Hail to 'All the President's Men' |work=[[The New York Times]] |date=April 8, 1976 |access-date=2023-11-15}}</ref> [[Rex Reed]] wrote, "Just to think about Watergate (and there are those, I'm told, who prefer not to) is to chatter the brain with a million details, telephone conversations, notebook jottings, investigations and technical problems that could be very dull indeed on film. The result is a movie that literally keeps the audience glued to the edge of the seat with nail-biting tension and excitement."<ref>{{cite web | url=https://nydailynews.newspapers.com/article/daily-news/135273063/ | first=Rex | last=Reed | author-link=Rex Reed |title=How the big fish was caught | work=[[New York Daily News]] | date=April 4, 1976 | access-date=2023-11-15}}</ref> Kathleen Carroll of the ''[[New York Daily News]]'' gave the film a full four-star rating and wrote that it "offers a rousing argument for the preservation of freedom of the press that precious right that allows reporters like Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein to ferret out the truth."<ref>{{cite web |url=https://nydailynews.newspapers.com/article/daily-news/135270191/ |first=Kathleen |last=Carroll |title=The toppling of a President | work=[[New York Daily News]] | date=April 8, 1976 | access-date=2023-11-15}}</ref> [[Charles Champlin]] of the ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' called it "quite beyond anything else, an engrossing mystery movie, with atmosphere, suspense, surprise, conflict, danger, secret messages, clandestine meetings, heroes, villains and a cast of leading and supporting characters that might have emerged from an unlikely collaboration of, let us say, [[Gore Vidal]] and [[Raymond Chandler]]."<ref>{{cite news | title=History Re-Created as High Drama | first=Charles | last=Champlin | author-link=Charles Champlin | work=[[Los Angeles Times]] | date=April 4, 1976 }}</ref>

==== Retrospective reviews ====
The film continued to receive acclaim long after its release. Chris Nashawaty of ''[[Entertainment Weekly]]'', reviewing [[Blu-ray]] releases of both this film and [[Network (1976 film)|''Network'']] in 2011, called it "a victory lap for American journalism – the triumphant flip side to ''Network''{{'s}} self-loathing take on the media. It also anticipated our current [[WikiLeaks]] era, with Robert Redford (Woodward) and Dustin Hoffman (Bernstein) milking anonymous sources and burning shoe leather to speak truth to power. These films are timeless and essential, raising thorny questions we're still struggling to answer 35 years later. That must have been heavy stuff to be hit with at the multiplex in 1976. == ''"All the President's Men" Revisited'' ==
[[Sundance Institute|Sundance Productions]], which Redford owned, produced a two-hour documentary titled ''"All the President's Men" Revisited''.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Stelter |first=Brian |author-link=Brian Stelter |date=April 2, 2012 |title=Watergate Reporting, the Second Draft |language=en-US |work=The New York Times |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2012/04/03/arts/television/robert-redford-to-produce-a-documentary-about-watergate.html |access-date=April 9, 2023 |issn=0362-4331}}</ref> Broadcast on [[Discovery Channel|Discovery Channel Worldwide]] April 21, 2013, the documentary focuses on the Watergate case and the subsequent film adaptation. It simultaneously recounts how ''The Washington Post'' broke Watergate and how the scandal unfolded, going behind the scenes of the film. It explores how the Watergate scandal would be covered in the present day, whether such a scandal could happen again and who Richard Nixon was as a man. [[W. Mark Felt]], deputy director of the FBI during the early 1970s, revealed his identity as Deep Throat in 2005, and this is also covered in the documentary.

Footage from the film is included, as are interviews with Redford and Hoffman, as well as with real-life central characters, including Woodward, Bernstein, Bradlee, [[John Dean]], [[Alexander Butterfield]] and [[Fred Thompson]], who served as minority counsel to the [[United States Senate Watergate Committee|Senate Watergate Committee]], in his first major public appearance. Contemporary media figures, such as [[Tom Brokaw]] (who was [[NBC News]]' White House correspondent during the scandal), [[Jill Abramson]], [[Rachel Maddow]] and [[Jon Stewart]] are also featured in the documentary, which earned a 2013 [[Emmy Awards|Emmy]] nomination for Outstanding Documentary or Nonfiction Special.<ref>{{cite web|last=Bauder|first=David|title='All the President's Men Revisited' Documentary To Air On Discovery|url=https://www.huffingtonpost.com/2013/04/20/all-the-presidents-men-revisited-documentary-discovery_n_3122655.html|work=[[The Huffington Post]]|publisher=[[AOL]]|access-date=October 17, 2013|date=March 20, 2013|archive-date=October 26, 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131026113500/http://www.huffingtonpost.com/2013/04/20/all-the-presidents-men-revisited-documentary-discovery_n_3122655.html|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>[http://www.emmys.com/shows/all-presidents-men-revisited The Primetime Emmys  – ''All The President's Men Revisted''] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140529235128/http://www.emmys.com/shows/all-presidents-men-revisited |date=2014-05-29 }} The Emmys</ref>
