'''''The Little Shop of Horrors''''' is a 1960 American [[Comedy horror|horror comedy]] film directed and produced by [[Roger Corman]], from a screenplay by [[Charles B. Griffith]]. The film is a [[farce]] about a florist's assistant who cultivates a plant that feeds on human blood. It stars [[Jonathan Haze]], [[Jackie Joseph]], [[Mel Welles]], and [[Dick Miller]], who had all worked for Corman on previous films. Produced under the title ''The Passionate People Eater'',<ref name="Corman">{{cite book |last=Corman |first=Roger |author-link=Roger Corman |author2=Jerome, Jim |title=How I Made a Hundred Movies in Hollywood and Never Lost a Dime |url=https://archive.org/details/howimadehundredm00corm/page/61 |publisher=Da Capo Press |isbn=0-306-80874-9 |pages=[https://archive.org/details/howimadehundredm00corm/page/61 61–62; 67–70] |url-access=registration |date=1998-08-22}}</ref><ref name="Gray">{{cite book |last=Gray |first=Beverly |title=Roger Corman: Blood-Sucking Vampires, Flesh-Eating Cockroaches, and Driller Killers |year=2004 |publisher=Thunder's Mouth Press |isbn=1-56025-555-2 |pages=62–65, 67–69}}</ref> the film employs an original style of humor, combining [[Black comedy|dark comedy]] with farce<ref name="Graham">{{cite web |url=https://www.sensesofcinema.com/2005/conversations-with-filmmakers/charles_b_griffith/ |title=Little Shop of Genres: An interview with Charles B. Griffith |access-date=2007-10-24 |author=Graham, Aaron W. |date=5 November 2000 |publisher=Senses of Cinema |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071016064240/http://www.sensesofcinema.com/contents/05/35/charles_b_griffith.html |archive-date=October 16, 2007}}</ref> and incorporating [[Jewish humor]] and elements of [[parody film|spoof]].<ref name="Weaver-Tamborini">{{cite book |editor1=Weaver, James B. |editor2=Tamborini, Ronald C. |title=Horror Films: Current Research on Audience Preferences and Reactions |year=1996 |publisher=Lawrence Erlbaum Associates |pages=59}}</ref> ''The Little Shop of Horrors'' was shot on a budget of $28,000 ({{Inflation|US|28000|1960|fmt=eq|r=-3}}). Interiors were shot in two days, by utilizing sets that had been left standing from ''[[A Bucket of Blood]]''.<ref name="Ray">{{cite book |last=Ray |first=Fred Olen |title=The New Poverty Row: Independent Filmmakers As Distributors |year=1991 |publisher=McFarland & Company |isbn=0-89950-628-3 |pages=28–30 }}</ref><ref name="BucketFunFacts">{{cite AV media notes |title=A Bucket of Blood |year=2000 |chapter=Fun Facts |publisher=[[MGM Home Entertainment]] |id=UPC:027616852847}}</ref><ref name="Peary">{{cite book |last=Peary |first=Danny |title=Cult Movies |url=https://archive.org/details/cultmoviesclassi0000pear |url-access=registration |year=1981 |publisher=Delacorte Press |location=New York |isbn=0-440-01626-6 |pages=[https://archive.org/details/cultmoviesclassi0000pear/page/203 203–205]}}</ref><ref name="Corman2">{{cite web |url=http://www.mjsimpson.co.uk/interviews/rogercorman.html |title=Interview with Roger Corman |access-date=2007-10-24 |last=Simpson |first=MJ |date=September 23, 1995 |quote=I shot Little Shop of Horrors in two days and a night for about $30,000, and the picture has lasted all these years. |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100104204843/http://www.mjsimpson.co.uk/interviews/rogercorman.html |archive-date=January 4, 2010}}</ref>

The film was released in October 1960 by Filmgroup as a [[double feature]] with Filmgroup's ''[[Last Woman on Earth]]'' (1960).<ref>Bill Warren, Keep Watching The Skies! American Science Fiction Movies of the 1950s, Vol. 2, 1958-1962 (New York: McFarland & Co, 1986), pg. 752-753.</ref> The film slowly gained a [[Cult film|cult following]] through word of mouth when it was later distributed as the [[B movie]] in a [[double feature]] with [[Mario Bava]]'s ''[[Black Sunday (1960 film)|Black Sunday]]'' (1960).<ref name="Ray"/><ref name="Weaver"/>

The film's popularity increased with local television broadcasts,<ref name="Hogan">{{cite book |last=Hogan |first=David J. |title=Dark Romance: Sexuality in the Horror Film |year=1997 |publisher=McFarland & Company |isbn=0-7864-0474-4 |pages=224}}</ref> and the presence of a young [[Jack Nicholson]], whose small role in the film has been prominently promoted on its [[home video]] releases.<ref name="Pearce">{{cite web |url=https://www.dvdverdict.com/reviews/littleshophorrors.php |title=Review of ''The Little Shop of Horrors'' |access-date=2007-10-24 |last=Pearce |first=Joel |date=June 16, 2006 |publisher=DVD Verdict |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080119043253/http://www.dvdverdict.com/reviews/littleshophorrors.php |archive-date=January 19, 2008}}</ref> The film was the basis for an [[Off-Broadway]] musical, ''[[Little Shop of Horrors (musical)|Little Shop of Horrors]]'', which in turn was adapted into a [[Little Shop of Horrors (1986 film)|1986 feature film]]. The musical enjoyed a 2003 [[Broadway theatre|Broadway]] debut and a 2019 off-Broadway revival, among other productions.

==Plot==
[[File:The Little Shop of Horrors (1960) by Roger Corman.webm|thumb|The full film]]
Florist shop owner Gravis Mushnick has two employees, Audrey Fulquard and Seymour Krelborn. Located on [[skid row]], Mushnick's rundown shop gets little business. When Seymour fouls up a floral arrangement for dentist Dr. Farb, Mushnick fires him. Hoping to change his mind, Seymour talks about a plant he has grown from seeds he got from a "Japanese gardener over on Central Avenue". Seymour names the plant "Audrey Jr.",<!-- the plant is only named "Audrey II" in the musical versions. --> which delights Audrey.

However, when finally shown the plant, Mushnick is unimpressed. Seymour suggests that Audrey Jr.'s uniqueness might attract people to see it, and Mushnick gives him one week to revive the plant. The usual plant food does not nourish it, but when Seymour accidentally pricks his finger, he discovers that the plant craves blood. Fed on Seymour's blood, Audrey Jr. begins to grow. The shop's revenues increase when customers are lured in to see the plant. Mushnick tells Seymour to refer to him as "Dad" and calls Seymour his son in front of a customer.

The plant develops the ability to speak and demands that Seymour feed it. Now [[Anemia|anemic]], Seymour walks along the railroad track. Throwing a rock to vent his frustration, he inadvertently knocks out a drunken man who falls on the track and is run over by a train. He tries to get rid of the body by burying it in a yard but is nearly caught each time. Guilt-ridden, Seymour decides to feed the mutilated body parts to Audrey Jr. Meanwhile, Mushnick returns to the shop to get cash and secretly observes Seymour feeding the plant. Mushnick considers telling the police but hesitates after seeing the line of customers at his shop the next day.

Seymour eventually arrives too, suffering from a toothache. Mushnick confronts him about Audrey Jr.'s eating habits without explicitly revealing what he knows about the plant. Seymour grows increasingly distressed as he realizes that Mushnick knows the truth. After finishing his rant, Mushnick sends Seymour to Farb, who wants to kill him and get even for his ruined flowers. Defending himself, Seymour kills Farb. Although horrified, Seymour feeds Farb's body to Audrey Jr. The disappearances of the two men attract the attention of Sergeant Joe Fink and his assistant Officer Frank Stoolie.

Audrey Jr. grows several feet tall and is budding. A representative of the Society of Silent Flower Observers of Southern California announces that Seymour will receive a trophy and that she will return to the shop when the plant's buds open. While Seymour and Audrey go on a date, Mushnick stays at the shop to see that Audrey Jr. harms no one else.

While tending to his shop, Mushnick finds himself at the mercy of Kloy Haddock, a robber who pretended to be a customer earlier that day. Haddock believes that the huge crowds he observed at the shop indicate the presence of a lot of money. Mushnick tricks Haddock into thinking that the money is where Audrey Jr. is kept. The plant eats Haddock after Mushnick maneuvers him next to it.

When forced to damage his relationship with Audrey to keep her from discovering Audrey Jr.'s nature, Seymour confronts the plant, planning to no longer do its bidding. The plant then hypnotizes Seymour and commands him to bring it more food. He wanders the night streets and knocks out a prostitute, whom he takes to Audrey Jr.

Lacking clues about the disappearances, Fink and Stoolie attend a sunset celebration at the shop during which Seymour is to be presented with the trophy and Audrey Jr.'s buds are expected to bloom. As the attendees watch, four buds open; inside each flower is the face of one of Audrey Jr.'s victims. Fink and Stoolie realize that Seymour is the murderer.

Seymour flees from the shop with the officers in pursuit. He manages to lose them and make his way back to the now-empty shop, where he blames Audrey Jr. for ruining his life. The plant instead asks to be fed. Seymour grabs a kitchen knife and climbs into Audrey Jr.'s maw with the intention of killing it. Later that evening, Audrey Jr. begins to wither and die. One final bloom opens to reveal Seymour's face, who shouts "I didn't mean it!" before wilting.

==Development==
''The Little Shop of Horrors'' was developed when director [[Roger Corman]] was given temporary access to sets that had been left standing from his previous film, ''[[A Bucket of Blood]]''. Corman decided to use the sets in a film made in the last two days before the sets were torn down.<ref name="Corman"/><ref name="Gray"/><ref name="Ray"/><ref name=BucketFunFacts/><ref name="Peary"/>

Corman initially planned to develop a story involving a [[private investigator]]. In the story's initial version, the character that eventually became Audrey would have been referred to as "Oriole Plove." Actress [[Nancy Kulp]] was a leading candidate for the part.<ref name="Ray"/> The characters that eventually became Seymour and Winifred Krelborn were named "Irish Eye" and "Iris Eye".<ref name="Ray"/> Actor [[Mel Welles]] was scheduled to play a character named "Draco Cardala," [[Jonathan Haze]] was scheduled to play "Archie Aroma," and [[Jack Nicholson]] would have played a character named "Jocko".<ref name="Ray"/>

[[Charles B. Griffith]] wanted to write a [[horror film|horror]]-themed [[comedy film]]. According to Mel Welles, Corman was not impressed by the box office performance of ''A Bucket of Blood'', and had to be persuaded to direct another comedy.<ref name="Gray"/> However, Corman later claimed he was interested because of ''A Bucket of Blood'' and said the development process was similar to that of the earlier film, when he and Griffith were inspired by visiting various coffee houses:

<blockquote>We tried a similar approach for ''The Little Shop of Horrors'', dropping in and out of various downtown dives. We ended up at a place where [[Sally Kellerman]] (before she became a star) was working as a waitress, and as Chuck and I vied with each other, trying to top each other's sardonic or subversive ideas, appealing to Sally as a referee, she sat down at the table with us, and the three of us worked out the rest of the story together.<ref name="roger">{{Cite news |last=Corman |first=Roger |date=October 17, 2007 |title=Wild Imagination |url=http://www.laweekly.com/2007-10-18/film-tv/wild-imagination/ |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140421083947/http://www.laweekly.com/2007-10-18/film-tv/wild-imagination/ |archive-date=2014-04-21 |access-date=2025-10-11 |work=LA Weekly |language=en |url-status=live }}</ref></blockquote>

The first screenplay Griffith wrote was ''Cardula'', a [[Dracula]]-themed story involving a [[vampire]] music critic.<ref name="Weaver"/> After Corman rejected the idea, Griffith says he wrote a screenplay titled ''Gluttony'',<ref name="Weaver"/> in which the protagonist was "a salad chef in a restaurant who would wind up cooking customers and stuff like that, you know? We couldn't do that though because of the [[Hays Code|code]] at the time. So I said, 'How about a man-eating plant?' and Roger said, 'Okay.' By that time, we were both drunk."<ref name="Graham"/>

[[Jackie Joseph]] later recalled "at first they told me it was a detective movie; then, while I was flying back [to make the movie], I think they wrote a whole new movie, more in the horror genre. I think over a weekend they rewrote it."<ref name="Jackie">[https://bmonster.com/horror15.html Tom Weaveer, Jackie Joseph interview, ''B Monster''] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20061113164052/http://www.bmonster.com/horror15.html |date=2006-11-13 }} accessed 18 April 2014</ref>

The screenplay was written under the title ''The Passionate People Eater''.<ref name="Corman"/><ref name="Gray"/><ref name="Ray"/> Welles stated, "The reason that ''The Little Shop of Horrors'' worked is because it was a love project. It was ''our'' love project."<ref name="Gray"/> The film's concept may have been inspired by "Green Thoughts", a 1932 story by [[John Collier (fiction writer)|John Collier]] about a man-eating plant.<ref>Fowler, Christopher. [https://www.independent.co.uk/arts-entertainment/books/features/forgotten-authors-no-34-john-collier-1689028.html "Forgotten authors No. 34: John Collier"] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121109213232/http://www.independent.co.uk/arts-entertainment/books/features/forgotten-authors-no-34-john-collier-1689028.html|date=2012-11-09}}. ''The Independent'', May 24, 2009. Retrieved November 15, 2010</ref> Hollywood writer [[Dennis McDougal]] suggests that Griffith may have been influenced by [[Arthur C. Clarke]]'s 1956 science fiction short story "[[The Reluctant Orchid]]"<ref>{{cite book |last=McDougal |first=Dennis |url=https://archive.org/details/isbn_9780470422823 |title=Five Easy Decades: How Jack Nicholson Became the Biggest Movie Star in Modern Times |publisher=[[John Wiley & Sons]] |year=2008 |isbn=978-0-471-72246-5 |pages=[https://archive.org/details/isbn_9780470422823/page/39 39] |quote=Arthur C. Clarke. |url-access=registration}}</ref> (which was in turn inspired by the 1894 [[H. G. Wells]] story "The Flowering of the Strange Orchid").

Officers Fink and Stoolie were inspired by ''[[Dragnet (TV series)|Dragnet]]'' characters [[Joe Friday]] and Frank Smith.<ref name="Peary"/> The film opens up in a similar format to a Dragnet episode.

==Production==
[[File:The Little Shop of Horrors robber cropped.png|right|thumb|Screenwriter [[Charles B. Griffith]], left, in a [[cameo appearance|cameo role]] as Kloy Haddock, a robber]]
The film was partially cast with stock actors that Corman had used in previous films.<ref name=Hallencom>{{cite book |title=Comedy-Horror Films: A Chronological History, 1914-2008 |url=https://archive.org/details/comedyhorrorfilm00hall|url-access=limited |last=Hallenbeck |first=Bruce G.|publisher=[[McFarland & Company]]|year=2009 |isbn=9780786453788|pages=64–65}}</ref> Writer Charles B. Griffith portrays several small roles. Griffith's father appears as a dental patient, and his grandmother, [[Myrtle Vail]], appears as Seymour's hypochondriac mother.<ref name="Corman"/><ref name="Weaver"/> [[Dick Miller]], who had starred as the protagonist of ''A Bucket of Blood'' was offered the role of Seymour, but turned it down, instead taking the smaller role of Burson Fouch.<ref name="Gray"/><ref name="Ray"/> Production at the ''Bucket of Blood'' sets was compressed into three days of cast rehearsals, immediately followed by two days and one night of [[principal photography]].<ref name="YouTube Interview">{{cite web|url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aNL7bb6CE4U |title=Roger Corman talks about making The Little Shop of Horrors (1960) in 2 only days|date=10 January 2015 |access-date=2017-09-22|publisher=[[YouTube]]|archive-date=2021-04-25|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210425021144/https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aNL7bb6CE4U|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="CNN">{{cite news |first=Porter |last=Anderson |title=Roger Corman: Attack of the independent filmmaker |url=http://archives.cnn.com/2001/CAREER/trends/01/04/corman/ |publisher=CNN |date=January 4, 2001 |access-date=2007-10-24 |archive-date=2007-10-14 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071014200323/http://archives.cnn.com/2001/CAREER/trends/01/04/corman/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

It had been rumored that the film's shooting schedule was based on a bet that Corman could not complete a film within that time. However, this claim has been denied by Mel Welles.<ref name="Weaver"/> According to Joseph, Corman shot the film quickly in order to beat changing industry rules that would have prevented producers from "buying out" an actor's performance in perpetuity. On January 1, 1960, new rules were to go into effect requiring producers to pay all actors [[residual (entertainment industry)|residuals]] for all future releases of their work. This meant that Corman's B-movie business model would be permanently changed and he would not be able to produce low-budget films in the same way. Before these rules went into effect, Corman decided to shoot one last film and scheduled it for the last week in December 1959.<ref name="Joseph">{{cite web |url=https://bmonster.com/horror15.html |title=Interview with Jackie Joseph |access-date=2007-10-24 |author=Weaver, Tom |publisher=The Astounding B Monster |archive-date=2007-10-14 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071014053103/http://www.bmonster.com/horror15.html |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name=Hallencom/>

Interiors were shot with three cameras in wide, lingering master shots in single takes.<ref name="Corman"/><ref name="Ray"/> Welles states that Corman "had two camera crews on the set—that's why the picture, from a cinematic standpoint, is really not very well done. The two camera crews were pointed in opposite directions so that we got both angles, and then other shots were 'picked up' to use in between, to make it flow. It was a pretty fixed set and it was done sort of like a sitcom is done today, so it wasn't very difficult."<ref name="Weaver"/>

At the time of shooting, [[Jack Nicholson]] had appeared in two films and worked with Roger Corman as the lead in ''[[The Cry Baby Killer]]''. According to Nicholson, "I went in to the shoot knowing I had to be very quirky because Roger originally hadn't wanted me. In other words, I couldn't play it straight. So I just did a lot of weird shit that I thought would make it funny."<ref name="Corman"/> According to Dick Miller, all of the dialogue between his character and Mel Welles was [[ad-libbed]].<ref name="Weaver"/> During a scene in which writer Charles B. Griffith played a robber, Griffith remembers that "When [Welles] and I forgot my lines, I improvised a little, but then I was the writer. I was allowed to."<ref name="Corman"/> However, Welles states that "Absolutely none of it was ad-libbed [...] every word in ''Little Shop'' was written by Chuck Griffith, and I did ninety-eight pages of dialogue in two days."<ref name="Weaver"/>

According to Nicholson, "we never did shoot the end of the scene. This movie was pre-lit. You'd go in, plug in the lights, roll the camera, and shoot. We did the take outside the office and went inside the office, plugged in, lit and rolled. Jonathan Haze was up on my chest pulling my teeth out. And in the take, he leaned back and hit the rented dental machinery with the back of his leg and it started to tip over. Roger didn't even call cut. He leapt onto the set, grabbed the tilting machine, and said 'Next set, that's a wrap.'"<ref name="Corman"/> By 9&nbsp;a.m. of the first day, Corman was informed by the production manager that he was behind schedule.<ref name="Ray"/>

Exteriors were later directed by Griffith and Welles over two successive weekends, with $279 worth of rented equipment.<ref name="Gray"/><ref name="Ray"/> Griffith and Welles paid a group of children five cents apiece to run out of a subway tunnel.<ref name="Weaver"/> They were also able to persuade homeless alcoholics to appear as extras for ten cents apiece.<ref name="Gray"/><ref name="Weaver"/> "The [[wikt:wino|winos]] would get together, two or three of them, and buy pints of wine for themselves! We also had a couple of the winos act as ramrods—sort of like production assistants—and put them in charge of the other wino extras."<ref name="Weaver"/> Griffith and Welles also persuaded a funeral home to donate a hearse and coffin—with a real corpse inside—for the film shoot.<ref name="Weaver"/> Griffith and Welles were able to use the nearby [[Southern Pacific Transportation Company]] yard for an entire evening using two bottles of scotch as persuasion.<ref name="Gray"/> The scene in which a character portrayed by Robert Coogan is run over by a train was accomplished by persuading the railroad crew to back the locomotive away from the actor. The shot was later printed in reverse.<ref name="Gray"/> Griffith and Welles spent a total of $1,100 on fifteen minutes' worth of exteriors.<ref name="Gray"/><ref name="Weaver"/>

The film's musical score, written by cellist [[Fred Katz (cellist)|Fred Katz]], was originally written for ''A Bucket of Blood''. According to Mark Thomas McGee, author of ''Roger Corman: The Best of the Cheap Acts'', each time Katz was called upon to write music for Corman, Katz sold the same score as if it were new music.<ref>{{cite book |last=Ray |first=Fred Olen |title=The New Poverty Row: Independent Filmmakers As Distributors |year=1991 |publisher=McFarland & Company |isbn=0-89950-628-3 |page=40 }}</ref> Katz explained that his music for the film was created by a music editor piecing together selections from other soundtracks that he had produced for Corman.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Lawson |first=R.D. |title=Fred Katz |url=https://www.runmovies.eu/?p=543 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141123150356/https://www.runmovies.eu/?p=543 |archive-date=2014-11-23 |access-date=2025-10-11 |work=Soundtrack |language=en-US}}</ref>

Howard R. Cohen learned from Charles B. Griffith that when the film was being edited, "there was a point where two scenes would not cut together. It was just a visual jolt, and it didn't work. And they needed something to bridge that moment. They found in the editing room a nice shot of the moon, and they cut it in, and it worked. Twenty years go by. I'm at the studio one day. Chuck comes running up to me, says, 'You've got to see this!' It was a magazine article—eight pages on the symbolism of the moon in ''Little Shop of Horrors''."<ref name="Gray"/> According to Corman, the total budget for the production was $30,000.<ref name="Corman2"/> Other sources estimate the budget to be between $22,000 and $100,000.<ref name="Gray"/><ref name="Ray"/><ref name="Peary"/>

==Release and reception==
[[File:The Little Shop of Horrors trailer.png|thumb|The film's trailer emphasized its comedic content]]

===Release history===
Corman had initial trouble finding distribution for the film, as some distributors, including [[American International Pictures]] (AIP), felt that the film would be interpreted as [[anti-Semitic]], citing the characters of Gravis Mushnick and Siddie Shiva.<ref name="Gray"/><ref name="Ray"/><ref name="Weaver">{{cite book |last=Weaver |first=Tom |title=Return of the B Science Fiction and Horror Movie Makers: Writers, Producers, Directors, Actors, Moguls and Makeup |year=1999 |publisher=McFarland & Company |pages=387–390 }}</ref><ref name="Halligan">{{cite book |last=Halligan |first=Benjamin |title=Michael Reeves |year=2003 |publisher=Manchester University Press |isbn=0-7190-6351-5 |pages=45 }}</ref> Welles, who was [[Jewish]], stated that he gave his character a Turkish Jewish accent and mannerisms, and that he saw the humor of the film as playful, and felt there was no intent to defame any ethnic group.<ref name="Gray"/> The film was finally released by its production company, [[The Filmgroup]], nine months after it had been completed.<ref name="Weaver"/>

''The Little Shop of Horrors'' was screened out of competition at the [[1960 Cannes Film Festival]] on the strength of its reviews in trade papers. Mel Welles had sent these to the festival, which asked for a print of the film.<ref name="Corman"/><ref name="Peary"/><ref>{{cite magazine|url=https://archive.org/details/variety-1961-05/page/n201/mode/1up?|magazine=Variety|title=From trade paper reviews Roger Corman cheapie film rates Cannes bid|page=17|date=17 May 1961}}</ref>

A year later, AIP distributed the film as the [[B movie]] for its release of [[Mario Bava]]'s ''[[Black Sunday (1960 film)|Black Sunday]]''. Despite being barely mentioned in advertising (it was only occasionally referred to as an "Added Attraction" to Bava's film), ''Black Sunday'''s critical and commercial success resulted in positive [[word of mouth]] responses to ''The Little Shop of Horrors''.<ref name="Weaver"/> The film was re-released again the following year in a [[double feature]] with ''[[Last Woman on Earth]]''.<ref name="Ray"/>

Because Corman did not believe that ''The Little Shop of Horrors'' had much financial prospect after its initial theatrical run, he did not bother to copyright it, resulting in the film entering the [[public domain]].<ref name="Ray"/><ref>{{citation |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=4WKTNLRtUAsC |title=The Public Domain: How to Find & Use Copyright-Free Writings, Music, Art & More |author=Fishman, Stephen |year=2010 |edition=5th |access-date=2010-10-31 |isbn=978-1-4133-1205-8 |archive-date=2011-05-11 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110511081742/http://books.google.com/books?id=4WKTNLRtUAsC |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last=David |first=Pierce |date=June 2007 |title=Forgotten Faces: Why Some of Our Cinema Heritage Is Part of the Public Domain |journal=Film History: An International Journal |volume=19 |issue=2 |pages=125–43 |oclc=15122313 |jstor=25165419 |doi=10.2979/FIL.2007.19.2.125 |s2cid=191633078 }}</ref> Because of this, the film is widely available in copies of varying quality. The film was originally screened theatrically in the widescreen [[Aspect ratio (image)|aspect ratio]] of 1.85:1, but has largely only been seen in [[open matte]] at an aspect ratio of 1.33:1 since its original theatrical release.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Dante |first=Joe |date=2011 |title=Notes from Joe: Aspect Ratios in THE LITTLE SHOP OF HORROR |url=http://trailersfromhell.com/blog/2011/07/06/notes-from-joe-aspect-ratios-in-the-little-shop-of-horror/ |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121018025620/http://trailersfromhell.com/blog/2011/07/06/notes-from-joe-aspect-ratios-in-the-little-shop-of-horror/ |archive-date=2012-10-18 |access-date=2025-10-11 |website=Trailers From Hell |language=en-US}}</ref>

===Critical and audience reception===
The film's critical reception was largely favorable. On review aggregation website [[Rotten Tomatoes]] it has an approval rating of 92% based on reviews from 12 critics.<ref>{{cite web |title=''The Little Shop of Horrors'' reviews |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/1012514-little_shop_of_horrors/ |access-date=September 22, 2019 |archive-date=September 25, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200925105552/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/1012514-little_shop_of_horrors |url-status=live }}</ref> ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' wrote, "The acting is pleasantly preposterous. [...] Horticulturalists and vegetarians will love it."<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.variety.com/review/VE1117792654 |title=The Little Shop of Horrors |author=Staff |date=December 31, 1960 |work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] |access-date=February 10, 2012 |archive-date=April 25, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210425020859/https://variety.com/1960/film/reviews/the-little-shop-of-horrors-1200419866/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

[[Jack Nicholson]], recounting the reaction to a screening of the film, states that the audience "laughed so hard I could barely hear the dialogue. I didn't quite register it right. It was as if I had forgotten it was a comedy since the shoot. I got all embarrassed because I'd never really had such a positive response before."<ref name="Corman"/>

In his book ''Comedy-Horror Films: A Chronological History, 1914-2008'', Bruce G. Hallenbeck called the film "one of Corman's gems, an idea that was born on 'a night out on the town' that's every bit as loony as it sounds." He cited the hilarious performances delivered by the ensemble cast and Corman's strong results while working under the self-imposed pressures of a cheap budget and a fast shooting schedule.<ref name=Hallencom/>

===Legacy===
The film's popularity slowly grew with local television broadcasts throughout the 1960s and 1970s.<ref name="Hogan"/> Interest in the film was rekindled when a stage musical adaptation called ''[[Little Shop of Horrors (musical)|Little Shop of Horrors]]'' was produced in 1982.<ref name="Gray"/> It was based on the original film and was itself adapted to cinema as ''[[Little Shop of Horrors (1986 film)|Little Shop of Horrors]]'' in 1986.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-features/chris-evans-talks-star-little-shop-horrors-1280781/ | title=Chris Evans in Talks to Star in Greg Berlanti's 'Little Shop of Horrors' | first=Borys | last=Kit | date=February 24, 2020 | access-date=February 24, 2020 | work=[[The Hollywood Reporter]] | archive-date=February 24, 2020 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200224233800/https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/heat-vision/chris-evans-talks-star-little-shop-horrors-1280781 | url-status=live }}</ref> A short-lived animated television series, ''[[Little Shop]]'', about a teenage boy and his giant talking plant, was adapted from the 1960 film, but incorporated elements from the musical.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Perlmutter |first1=David |title=The Encyclopedia of American Animated Television Shows |date=2018 |publisher=Rowman & Littlefield |isbn=978-1538103739 |page=367}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last1=Erickson |first1=Hal |title=Television Cartoon Shows: An Illustrated Encyclopedia, 1949 Through 2003 |date=2005 |edition=2nd |publisher=McFarland & Co |isbn=978-1476665993 |pages=511–512}}</ref> By 2024, [[Joe Dante]] and [[Roger Corman]] were working on a spinoff film to be titled ''Little Shop of Halloween Horrors.'' Dante was set to direct with [[Charles S. Haas]] writing the script. Corman, who died in May of that year, would have produced the film with [[Brad Krevoy]].<ref>{{cite news|url=https://deadline.com/2024/03/little-shop-of-halloween-horrors-joe-dante-roger-corman-1235859850/|title=Joe Dante, Roger Corman & Brad Krevoy Team On 'Little Shop Of Halloween Horrors'|last=Groba|first=Matt|date=15 March 2024|work=[[Deadline Hollywood|Deadline]]|accessdate=18 March 2024}}</ref> In 2025, Dante announced that the film had been shelved.<ref name="Little2">{{cite web|last=Felt|first=Kevin|url=https://thedirect.com/article/little-shop-of-horrors-reboot-movie-exclusive|title=Little Shop of Horrors Reboot Movie Gets Shelved Indefinitely (Exclusive)|website=The Direct|date=April 8, 2025|access-date=April 8, 2025}}</ref>
