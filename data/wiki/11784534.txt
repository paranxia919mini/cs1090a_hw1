'''''Journey to Italy''''', also known as '''''Voyage to Italy''''',<ref name="Brunette1996" /> is a 1954 [[drama film]] directed by [[Roberto Rossellini]]. [[Ingrid Bergman]] and [[George Sanders]] play Katherine and Alex Joyce, a childless English married couple on a trip to Italy whose marriage is on the point of collapse until they are miraculously reconciled.

The film was written by Rossellini and [[Vitaliano Brancati]], but is loosely based on the 1934 novel ''[[Duo (novel)|Duo]]'' by [[Colette]]. Although the film was an Italian production, its dialogue was in English. The film’s first theatrical release occurred in Italy under the title '''''Viaggio in Italia''''', with the dialogue dubbed into Italian.

''Journey to Italy'' is considered by many to be Rossellini's masterpiece,<ref name="Lehman1990">{{cite book |editor1-last=Lehman |editor1-first=Peter |last=Brunette |first=Peter |chapter=Visual Motifs in Rosselini's ''Voyage to Italy''  |title=Close Viewings: An Anthology of New Film Criticism |chapter-url=https://books.google.com/books?id=ju1GnaR180YC&pg=PA39 |date=1 January 1990 |publisher=University Press of Florida|isbn=978-0-8130-0967-4|page=39}}</ref><ref name=Kehr>{{cite news |last=Kehr |first=Dave |author-link=Dave Kehr |title=Voyage to Italy |work=Chicago Reader |url=http://www.chicagoreader.com/chicago/voyage-to-italy/Film?oid=1062861 |quote=Roberto Rossellini's finest fiction film (1953), and unmistakably one of the great achievements of the art. }}</ref><ref name=Schwartz>{{cite web |title='Voyage in Italy' (Viaggio in Italia) |last=Schwartz |first=Dennis |author-link=Dennis Schwartz |work=Ozus' World Cinema Reviews |url=http://homepages.sover.net/~ozus/voyageinitaly.htm |date=February 9, 2006 |quote=A magical love story that is beautifully told without one false note. It makes the best of its dead time, more so than any other film of this high quality has ever done before. Its passionate conclusion is still moving even at this date some fifty years after its release. This is Roberto Rossellini's finest film (his others with Ingrid Bergman as his wife include ''Joan of Arc at the Stake''-1954 and ''Fear''-1954). |access-date=September 12, 2016 |archive-date=February 4, 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180204170809/http://homepages.sover.net/~ozus/voyageinitaly.htm |url-status=dead }}</ref> as well as a seminal work of [[Modernism|modernist]] cinema due to its loose storytelling. In 2012, it was listed by ''Sight & Sound'' magazine as one of the fifty greatest films ever made.<ref name="bfi" />

==Plot==
[[File:Bergman & Sanders Journey to Italy.jpg|thumb|upright=1.2|Ingrid Bergman and George Sanders in ''Journey to Italy'']]
Alex and Katherine Joyce are a couple from [[England]] who have traveled by car to [[Italy]] to sell a villa near [[Naples]] that they have recently inherited from Alex's deceased Uncle Homer. The trip is intended as a vacation for Alex, a workaholic businessman given to brusqueness and sarcasm. Katherine is more sensitive, and the journey has evoked poignant memories of a poet friend, Charles Lewington, now deceased.

Conversing as they drive through the Italian countryside, Alex and Katherine arrive in Naples and run into Judy, an old friend, and her party. They join and have drinks and dinner. The next day they are given a lengthy room-by-room tour of Uncle Homer's villa by its caretakers, Tony Burton, a former British soldier, and Natalia Burton, the Italian wife Tony married after the war.

Within days of their arrival, the couple's relationship becomes strained amid mutual misunderstandings, buried anger and jealousy on both sides. The two begin to spend their days separately. Alex takes a side trip to the island of Capri. His attempts to have a nice evening all fail, one with a woman who misses her husband, and one with a morose prostitute.

Katherine tours Naples. On the third day of her visit, she tours the large, ancient statues at the [[Naples National Archaeological Museum|Naples Museum]]. On the sixth day, she visits the [[Phlegraean Fields]] with their volcanic curiosities. On another day, she accompanies Natalie to the [[Fontanelle cemetery]], with its stacks of unidentified disinterred human skulls that are adopted and honored by local people.<ref name="Mulvey">{{cite book |last=Mulvey |first=Laura |author-link=Laura Mulvey |title=Death 24X a second: Stillness and the Moving Image |chapter=Roberto Rossellini's ''Journey to Italy''/''Viaggio in Italia'' (1953) |pages=104–122 |publisher=Reaktion Books |date=2006 |oclc=61529345 |isbn=9781861892638 |quote=it was dismissed on release, with only a few critics understanding that it was carefully constructed to undermine conventions of event-driven narrative and open out space and time for thought. The places included in the film were carefully chosen for their resonances and associations, from which Rossellini creates an implicit, idiosyncratic, commentary on the cinema, its reality, its indexical quality, as well as its uncanny ability to preserve light.}} Mulvey identifies several of the locales near Naples that were used in filming.</ref>

On the last day, Alex and Katherine impetuously agree to divorce following an explosive argument. They are interrupted by Tony, who insists that they go with him to [[Pompeii]] for an extraordinary opportunity. There, the three witness the discovery of another couple who had been buried in ashes during the eruption of [[Mount Vesuvius]] nearly 2000 years earlier, leaving Katherine profoundly disturbed.<ref>Giuseppe Forelli discovered that one could make plaster castings of the cavities left by the bodies of victims; the castings preserve the form of the body and its bones. See {{cite news |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/history/ancient/romans/pompeii_rediscovery_01.shtml#two |title=Pompeii: Its Discovery and Preservation |first=Salvatore Ciro |last=Nappo |date=February 17, 2011 |access-date=September 19, 2016 |publisher=BBC}}</ref> She and Alex depart Pompeii only to be caught up in the procession for [[Januarius|Saint Gennaro]] in Naples.<ref name="Gallagher" />  Katherine is swept away in the crowd, and Alex goes after her and retrieves her. They embrace, and Katherine says, "Tell me that you love me!" He responds, "Well, if I do, will you promise not to take advantage of me?" The film concludes with a crane shot showing the couple embracing passionately amid the continuing religious procession.

==Production==
The film originally was intended as an adaptation of the French writer [[Colette]]'s novel ''[[Duo (novel)|Duo]]''; Rossellini was, however, unable to get the rights to the novel and so was forced to draft a screenplay that differed sufficiently from the novel.<ref name="Brunette1996">{{cite book |last=Brunette |first=Peter |title=Roberto Rossellini |chapter=''Voyage to Italy'' (1953) |chapter-url=http://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?docId=ft709nb48d&chunk.id=d0e4630&toc.depth=1&toc.id=d0e3357&brand=ucpress |year=1996 |publisher=University of California Press |isbn=978-0-520-20053-1 |pages=155–176}} Brunette's chapter incorporates a lengthy, close reading of the film.</ref> Rossellini and his co-author, Vitaliano Brancati, also apparently drew on a script entitled ''New Vine'', by [[Antonio Pietrangeli]], which described the argumentative relationship of an English couple touring Naples in a Jaguar automobile.<ref name=Gallagher>{{cite book |title=The Adventures of Roberto Rossellini |first=Tag |last=Gallagher |chapter=''Voyage in Italy'' |publisher=Da Capo Press |date=1998 |isbn=9780306808739 |page=[https://archive.org/details/adventuresofrobe00gall/page/397 397] |chapter-url=https://books.google.com/books?id=xLfqxYTKhwQC&pg=PA397 |url=https://archive.org/details/adventuresofrobe00gall/page/397 }} See also a synopsis of Gallagher's chapter: {{cite news |title=Voyage to Italy |first=Wheeler Winston |last=Dixon |date=July 2009 |work=Senses of Cinema |issue=51 |url=http://sensesofcinema.com/2009/cteq/voyage-to-italy/}}</ref> The film's storyline about Charles Lewington, the deceased poet who'd been in love with Katherine Joyce, is considered to be an allusion to the short story "[[The Dead (Joyce short story)|The Dead]]" by [[James Joyce]].<ref name="Brunette1996" /><ref name=Mulvey /><ref name=Gronstad>{{cite journal |title=The Gaze of Tiresias: Joyce, Rossellini and the Iconology of "The Dead" |last=Grønstad |first= Asbjørn |url=http://ojs.ub.gu.se/ojs/index.php/njes/article/view/198/196 |journal=Nordic Journal of English Studies |volume=1 |issue=2 |date=2002 |pages=233–248 |quote=Rossellini's film uses "The Dead" as a point of departure for a creative contemplation of the ideas encountered in the short story.|doi=10.35360/njes.115 |doi-access=free }}</ref>

Rossellini's directorial style was very unusual. The actors did not receive their lines until shortly before filming of a particular scene, which left them little if any chance to prepare or rehearse.<ref name="Brunette1996" /> George Sanders' autobiography ''Memoirs of a Professional Cad'' (1960) tellingly describes Rossellini's methods of direction and their effects on the actors and production team.<ref>{{cite book |title=Memoirs of a Professional Cad |last=Sanders |first=George |date=1960 |publisher=Putnam |location=New York |oclc=475650}}</ref>

==Theatrical releases==
The film was completed in 1953, but it took 18 months to arrange for distribution of the film in Italy.<ref name=Gallagher /> It was released in 1954 with the title ''Viaggio in Italia'', with a running time of 105 minutes.<ref>{{cite book |title=Dictionary of Films |first1=Georges |last1=Sadoul |author1-link=Georges Sadoul |first2=Peter |last2=Morris |others=Peter Morris (translation and editing) |publisher=University of California Press |year=1972 |isbn=9780520021525 |oclc=490700 |page=402 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=_CL5zCKR2PgC&pg=PA402}} This book is a translation of {{cite book |title=Dictionnaire des Film |language=fr |date=1965 |publisher=[[Éditions du Seuil]] |oclc=1934530}}</ref> The receipts and critical reception were poor. The film had been dubbed into Italian, and now is used as an example of "monstrous" difficulties with dubbing.<ref>{{cite book |title=Translating Popular Film |first=C. |last=O'Sullivan |publisher=Springer |date=2011 |isbn=9780230317543 |page=184 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=gP2HDAAAQBAJ&pg=PA184 }}</ref> In April 1955, an 88-minute version of the film, in English, was released in France as ''L'Amour est le plus fort''.<ref>{{cite web |title=Viaggio in Italia - Fiche Film |work={{Interlanguage link|Bibliothèque du film|fr}} (BiFi) |language=fr |url=http://cinema.encyclopedie.films.bifi.fr/index.php?pk=42673 |access-date=2016-09-10|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160303225209/http://cinema.encyclopedie.films.bifi.fr/index.php?pk=42673|archive-date=3 March 2016|url-status=unfit}}</ref> There was little interest in the film in the U.S. and Britain despite the fact that the film had been made in English with noted actors in the leads. An American version, with an 80-minute running time, had a limited release in 1955 with the title ''Strangers''.<ref>{{cite web |title=Index to Motion Picture Credits - 'Strangers' |publisher=Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences |access-date=2016-09-22 |url=http://wwwdb.oscars.org:8100/servlet/impc.DisplayCredits?vetted=T&primekey_in=2011072513:48:16994046853}} Los Angeles release August 31, 1955 (80 minutes).{{Dead link|date=March 2026}}</ref> In Britain, a cut version (70 minutes) was released in 1958 under the title ''The Lonely Woman''.<ref>{{cite book |title=World Cinema's 'Dialogues' With Hollywood |last= Nowell-Smith |first=Geoffrey |chapter=The American Dream in Postwar Italy |editor1-first=P. |editor1-last=Cooke |publisher=Springer |date=2007 |isbn=9780230223189 |page=129 |chapter-url=https://books.google.com/books?id=0CpaCwAAQBAJ&pg=PA129}}</ref>

==Reception and significance==
''Journey to Italy'' performed badly at the box office and was largely a critical failure.<ref>{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=vb_1ma9DKH0C&pg=PA438|title=An Introduction to Film Studies|page=438|editor-last=Nelmes|editor-first=Jill|publisher=Psychology Press|date=2003|isbn=0415262682}}</ref> It had a profound influence, however, on [[French New Wave|New Wave]] filmmakers working in the 1950s and 1960s. As described six decades later by film critic John Patterson: "French critics at the ''[[Cahiers du Cinéma]]'' – the likes of [[Jean-Luc Godard]], [[Jacques Rivette]], [[François Truffaut]] and [[Claude Chabrol]] – all saw it as the moment when poetic cinema grew up and became indisputably modern. ''Journey to Italy'' is thus one wellspring of the French New Wave. A film convulsed by themes of sterility, petrification, pregnancy and eternity, it finds its echo in such death-haunted ''Nouvelle Vague'' masterpieces as Chabrol's ''[[Le Boucher]]'' and Truffaut's ''[[The Green Room (film)|La Chambre Verte]]''."<ref name=Patterson>{{cite news |title=Journey To Italy: the Italian film that kickstarted the French New Wave |last=Patterson |first=John |work=The Guardian |date= May 5, 2013 |url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2013/may/06/journey-to-italy-roberto-rossellini }}</ref> Filmmaker [[Martin Scorsese]] talks about the film and his impressions of it in his own film ''[[My Voyage to Italy]]'' (1999).<ref name="Brunette1996" />

Today, ''Journey to Italy'' generally is regarded as a landmark film. Critic Geoff Andrew referred to it as "a key stepping stone on the path to modern cinema" in its shift away from [[Italian neorealism|neorealism]],<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/realer-realism-journey-italy|title=Realer than realism: Journey to Italy|last=Andrew|first=Geoff|date=8 May 2013 |publisher=British Film Institute|access-date=March 3, 2017}}</ref> and [[A.O. Scott]] notes Rossellini's "way of dissolving narrative into atmosphere, of locating drama in the unspoken inner lives of his characters";<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2013/05/01/movies/rossellinis-voyage-to-italy-with-ingrid-bergman.html|title=Revisiting a Rossellini Classic to Find Resonances of Today|last=Scott|first=A. O.|newspaper=The New York Times|date=April 30, 2013|access-date=March 3, 2017}}</ref> because Alex and Katherine are not developed through a conventional plot but instead spend lengthy amounts of time in boredom and dejection, the film frequently is cited as a major influence on the dramas of [[Michelangelo Antonioni]] and later works about modern malaise.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.bfi.org.uk/films-tv-people/4ce2b6b9d14d8|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160211222352/http://www.bfi.org.uk/films-tv-people/4ce2b6b9d14d8|url-status=dead|archive-date=February 11, 2016|title=Journey to Italy (1954)|publisher=British Film Institute|access-date=March 3, 2017}}</ref> The film is ranked 41st in the [[The Sight & Sound Top 50 Greatest Films of All Time|2012 survey of film critics]] conducted by ''[[Sight & Sound]]'' magazine, under the auspices of the [[British Film Institute]].<ref name="bfi">{{cite journal | url= http://www.bfi.org.uk/news/50-greatest-films-all-time | archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20120802102928/http://www.bfi.org.uk/news/50-greatest-films-all-time | url-status= dead | archive-date= August 2, 2012 | title= The Top 50 Greatest Films of All Time |issue= September 2012 | date=1 August 2012 | journal= [[Sight & Sound]] |publisher= [[British Film Institute]] |access-date=6 June 2013 |editor-link= Ian Christie (film scholar) |editor-first= Ian |editor-last=Christie}}</ref> It is ranked 71st in an overall aggregation of several "greatest films" surveys.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.theyshootpictures.com/gf1000_all1000films_table.php |title=1,000 Greatest Films (Full List) |access-date=January 27, 2016 |editor1-last=Georgaris |editor1-first=Bill}} "77. Voyage in Italy (Rossellini, 1953)"</ref> On Rotten Tomatoes, the average score from 26 critics is 8.6 out of 10 with an approval rating of 96%.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/journey-to-italy-viaggio-in-italia |title=Voyage to Italy (1953) |publisher=Rotten Tomatoes|accessdate=June 17, 2023}}</ref> On Metacritic, based on an average of 4 reviews from critics, the film received a perfect score of 100, meaning "universal acclaim."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/voyage-to-italy-re-release |title=Voyage to Italy (re-release) 2013 |publisher=Metacritic |accessdate=June 17, 2023}}</ref>

The Japanese filmmaker [[Akira Kurosawa]] cited this movie as one of his 100 favorite films.<ref name="farout">{{cite web |last1=Thomas-Mason |first1=Lee |title=From Stanley Kubrick to Martin Scorsese: Akira Kurosawa once named his top 100 favourite films of all time |url=https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/akira-kurosawa-100-favourite-films-list/ |website=Far Out Magazine |date=12 January 2021 |access-date=23 January 2023}}</ref>

==Home media and restoration==
There have been several releases of ''Journey to Italy'' for home video. In 2013, the Criterion Collection released a newly restored version as a region 1 DVD.<ref>{{cite AV media |title=Journey to Italy |publisher=The Criterion Collection |date=2013 |type=DVD (region 1) |oclc=857774031 |isbn=9781604657494 }} The feature film runs 85 minutes. Also released as a Blu-ray disk.</ref> This version is based on restoration work at [[Cineteca di Bologna]] and {{Interlanguage link|Cinecittà Luce|it|3=Istituto Luce Cinecittà}}, which was reviewed very favorably by [[Glenn Erickson]].<ref>{{cite web |title=3 Films By Roberto Rossellini starring Ingrid Bergman - Savant Blu-ray Review |last=Erickson |first=Glenn |url=https://www.dvdtalk.com/dvdsavant/s4168ross.html |work=DVD Savant |date=September 25, 2013}}</ref> An earlier DVD version was released in 2003 as a region 2 DVD by the British Film Institute.<ref>{{cite AV media |title=Journey to Italy |publisher=The British Film Institute (BFI) |date=2003 |type=DVD (region 2) |oclc=747191196 }} The feature film runs 80 minutes.</ref> It was reviewed then by Gary Tooze.<ref>{{cite web |title=Voyage to Italy |last=Tooze |first=Gary |date=2003 |work=DVD Beaver |url=http://www.dvdbeaver.com/film2/DVDReviews34/voyage_to_italy.htm}}</ref> A VHS tape version was released in 1992.<ref>{{cite AV media |title=Voyage in Italy |publisher=Connoisseur/Meridian |date=1992 |type=VHS tape (NTSC) |oclc=26441934 }} The feature film runs 80 minutes.</ref>
