'''''Black Christmas'''''{{efn-lr|The film was initially re-titled as ''Silent Night, Evil Night'' in the United States by its American distributor, [[Warner Bros. Pictures|Warner Bros.]], though its original title was restored for later screenings. It was retitled again as ''Stranger in the House'' for its initial television broadcasts in North America.}} is a 1974 Canadian [[slasher film]] produced and directed by [[Bob Clark]], and written by Roy Moore. It stars [[Olivia Hussey]], [[Keir Dullea]], [[Margot Kidder]], and [[John Saxon]]. The story follows a group of sorority sisters who receive threatening phone calls and are eventually stalked and murdered by a killer during the [[Christmas]] season.

Inspired by the urban legend "[[the babysitter and the man upstairs]]" and a series of murders that took place in the [[Westmount, Quebec|Westmount neighbourhood]] of [[Montreal]], [[Quebec]], Moore wrote the screenplay under the title ''Stop Me''. The filmmakers made numerous alterations to the script, primarily the shifting to a university setting with young adult characters. It was shot in [[Toronto]] in 1974 on an estimated budget of $686,000. 

The film was released in Canada by Ambassador Film Distributors on October 11, 1974. [[Warner Bros. Pictures|Warner Bros.]] released the film in the United States in May 1975 under the alternate title ''Silent Night, Evil Night'', though this title was retracted and the film was reissued under its original title beginning August 6, 1975. The film went on to gross $4,053,000 worldwide.<ref>Kerswell, J.A. (2012). The Slasher Movie Book. Chicago, Ill.: Chicago Review Press. ISBN 978-1556520105. OCLC 761851819</ref>

Upon its release, ''Black Christmas'' received mixed reviews, but it has since received critical re-appraisal, with film historians noting it for being one of the earliest slasher films.{{sfn|Paszylk|2009|pages=135–136}} It is also praised for its influence on [[John Carpenter]]'s ''[[Halloween (1978 film)|Halloween]]'' (1978). Aside from it earning a [[cult following]]{{sfn|Jenkins|2008|p=146}} since its release, a novelization written by Lee Hays was published in 1976. It is the first film in the [[Black Christmas (film series)|''Black Christmas'' series]], being followed by two remakes in [[Black Christmas (2006 film)|2006]] and  [[Black Christmas (2019 film)|2019]].

==Plot==
[[Billy (Black Christmas)|An unseen man]] climbs the exterior of a [[sorority house]] during a Christmas party and enters the [[attic]]. The house phone rings, and sorority sister [[Jess Bradford]] answers, discovering an [[Obscene phone call|obscene call]] from a previously known caller nicknamed "The Moaner". The caller rants in strange voices about someone named "Billy" and speaks vulgarities before threatening to kill them. Upset, a younger student, Clare Harrison, goes upstairs to pack, where she is attacked and asphyxiated by the intruder.

The next morning, Clare's father, Mr. Harrison, arrives, concerned she missed a train. [[Housemaster|Housemother]] Mrs. MacHenry offers to help search for her. Jess tells her boyfriend Peter Smythe she is pregnant and plans to have an abortion, angering him. Meanwhile, Mr. Harrison and sorority sisters Barb Coard and Phyl Carlson try to report Clare's disappearance to Sergeant Nash, who brushes them off until Clare's boyfriend, Chris Hayden, storms in and demands action.

That night, a search party gathers to find Clare as well as Janice, a young girl who has gone missing nearby. Volunteers locate Janice's dead body in a nearby park. Meanwhile, Mrs. MacHenry discovers Clare's body in the attic but is killed by the intruder with a crane hook. Jess receives another obscene call and decides to involve the police. Lieutenant Fuller arrives with a lineman to [[Wiretapping|trace the call]].

After Fuller leaves, an inebriated Barb suffers an [[asthma|asthma attack]] and retires for the night. [[Carol (music)|Christmas carolers]] distract Jess while the killer enters Barb's room and murders her with a glass [[unicorn]] figurine. Another call from the intruder echoes parts of Jess's argument with Peter. Fuller reports the trace failed, and suspicion briefly falls on Peter. Jess and Phyl lock the house, but Phyl is killed after finding Barb's body.

A subsequent call is successfully traced, and Nash instructs Jess to leave immediately, realizing the calls are originating from within the house. Jess finds Barb and Phyl dead and glimpses the killer through a door crack. She flees to the basement and locks herself in. Peter breaks in, and in a panic, Jess kills him with a fire poker. Police arrive to find her barely conscious beside his body, assuming he was the killer.

Believing the danger over, police leave Jess sleeping in her bedroom with an officer outside. From the attic, the killer's voice is heard, and the hatch opens, while Clare and Mrs. MacHenry's bodies remain undiscovered. The house phone rings once more.

==Production==
===Development===
''Black Christmas'' was initially developed by Canadian screenwriter Roy Moore, who wrote the screenplay under the title ''Stop Me''.<ref name=onscreen/> Inspirations for the film came from the [[urban legend]] known as "[[the babysitter and the man upstairs]]",<ref name="TCM">{{Cite news |title=Black Christmas (1974) |work=[[Turner Classic Movies]] |url=https://www.tcm.com/this-month/article/410638 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200727000712/http://www.tcm.com/this-month/article/410638%7C0/Black-Christmas.html |archive-date=July 27, 2020}}</ref> which had become widespread during the 1970s.{{sfn|Brunvand|2012|pages=46–47}}{{sfn|Koven|2008|p=129}} Moore claimed to have been inspired by a series of murders that occurred during the holiday season in the [[Westmount, Quebec|Westmount]] area of [[Montreal]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.cbc.ca/arts/homegrown-horror-5-canadian-scary-movies-you-need-to-watch-this-halloween-1.3825829|work=[[Canadian Broadcasting Company]]|title=Homegrown horror: 5 Canadian scary movies you need to watch this Halloween|date=October 28, 2016|author=Dupuis, Chris|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241130203715/http://www.cbc.ca/arts/homegrown-horror-5-canadian-scary-movies-you-need-to-watch-this-halloween-1.3825829|archive-date=November 30, 2024|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="duffin2006">{{cite av media|people=Duffin, Dan (director)|title=The 12 Days of 'Black Christmas'|medium=DVD documentary short|location=Canada|publisher=Critical Mass Releasing|date=2006}}</ref>

As noted in an article for ''[[The Daily Telegraph|The Telegraph]]'', the murders, which occurred in 1943, were perpetrated by a fourteen-year-old boy who bludgeoned several of his family members to death.<ref name="Power2019"/>
Film producers Harvey Sherman and Richard Schouten had [[Timothy Bond]] rewrite the script to give it a university setting.<ref name="TCM"/><ref name=legacy>{{cite AV media|title=Black Christmas Legacy|work=Black Christmas|medium=[[Blu-ray]] documentary short|publisher=[[Shout! Factory|Scream Factory]]|year=2016|oclc=1080289043}}</ref> Director and producer [[Bob Clark]], who had felt the original script was too much of a straightforward slasher film, made several alterations in dialogue,<ref name="TCM"/> and also incorporated humorous elements into the film, particularly the drunkenness of Barb, and Mrs. Mac, the latter of whom Clark based on his aunt.<ref name=onscreen/>

Clark felt that college and high school students had not been depicted with "any sense of reality" in American film, and that he intended to capture the "astuteness" of young adults: "College students — even in 1974 — are astute people. They're not fools. It's not all 'bikinis, beach blankets, [and] bingo'".<ref name=onscreen/>

===Casting===
[[Olivia Hussey]], who had previously garnered international fame for her role as [[Juliet]] in ''[[Romeo and Juliet (1968 film)|Romeo and Juliet]]'' (1968), signed on to appear in the film after being told by a [[psychic]] that she would "make a film in Canada that would earn a great deal of money".<ref name=onscreen/> Clark had also considered [[Jessica Harper]] and [[Cassandra Peterson]] for the role.{{sfn|DuPée|2022|p=121}} Clark sought [[Keir Dullea]] to play the role of Peter based on his performance as Dave Bowman in ''[[2001: A Space Odyssey (film)|2001: A Space Odyssey]]'' (1968).<ref name=onscreen/> The role of Mrs. Mac was offered to [[Bette Davis]],<ref name="TCM"/> who declined the part.<ref name=onscreen/> [[Margot Kidder]] was cast in the role of Barb, and said she had been attracted to the character "because she was wild and out of control", and not a "conventional leading" part.<ref name=onscreen/> For the role of Clare Harrison, whose murder jump-starts the film's plot, Toronto native [[Lynne Griffin]] was cast after her mother, who was also her [[casting agent]] at the time, got her an audition. Griffin would later go on to star in ''[[Curtains (1983 film)|Curtains]]'' (1983), and in the acclaimed television series ''[[Wind at My Back]]'' (1996–2001).<ref name="griffininterview11">{{cite web |title=Six for Her Scythe: An Interview with Lynne Griffin: Part I |url=http://www.terrortrap.com/interviews/lynnegriffin/ |website=The Terror Trap  |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250316220122/http://www.terrortrap.com/interviews/lynnegriffin/|archive-date=March 16, 2025|url-status=live |date=July 2011}}</ref>

[[Gilda Radner]] was offered the role of Phyllis Carlson. She accepted the part, but dropped out one month before filming began owing to ''[[Saturday Night Live]]'' commitments, and was replaced by fellow [[The Second City|Second City]] comedy troupe performer [[Andrea Martin]].<ref name="TCM"/><ref name=onscreen/> The role of Lieutenant Fuller was originally given to [[Edmond O'Brien]].<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/north-bay-nugget/186843442/|work=[[North Bay Nugget]]|date=April 26, 1974|p=28|title=Saxon accustomed to snap offers|last=King|first=Paul|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> Upon his arrival to the set, however, the producers realized he would be unable to fulfill the duties required of the part due to his failing health (stemming from [[Alzheimer's disease]]).<ref name="Chiller" /> John Saxon, who had read the script prior, was called by the producers who offered him the role. He accepted, and had to arrive in Toronto from New York City within two days to begin shooting.<ref name=onscreen/> Saxon had previously appeared in the first ''[[giallo]]'' film, ''[[The Girl Who Knew Too Much (1963 film)|The Girl Who Knew Too Much]]'' (1963).<ref name="filmink">{{cite magazine|magazine=[[FilmInk]]|first=Stephen|last=Vagg|title=The Top Twelve Stages of Saxon|date=July 29, 2020|url-status=live|archive-url=https://archive.today/20251218065458/https://www.filmink.com.au/the-top-twelve-stages-of-saxon/|archive-date=December 18, 2025|url=https://www.filmink.com.au/the-top-twelve-stages-of-saxon/}}</ref> Clark always intended to cast Saxon from the beginning as he was his first choice for the role, but miscommunications between Clark and Saxon's agent resulted in O’Brien being cast before Saxon officially came aboard the film. 

For the role of the film's antagonist, Italian-Canadian actor [[Nick Mancuso]] was cast as one of the main voices in the phone call sequences. When [[audition]]ing for the role, director Clark had Mancuso sit in a chair facing away from him, so as not to see the actor's face. Clark then had Mancuso experiment with different voices in order to come up with one that was right for the character, with Clark later offering him the part.<ref name="Power2019"/>

===Filming===
[[File:Hart House, University of Toronto, by night (December 2008).jpg|thumb|upright=1|right|[[Soldiers' Tower]] at the [[University of Toronto]] is featured in the film.]]
The film was shot from March 25 to May 11, 1974, at a cost of $686,000 ({{Inflation|CA|686,000|1974|fmt=eq}}), with $200,000 coming from the [[Telefilm Canada|Canadian Film Development Corporation]].{{sfn|Turner|1987|p=180}} The 20-room house<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-globe-and-mail/186843268/|work=[[The Globe and Mail]]|date=April 27, 1974|p=27|title=Black Christmas Hussey's bright hope|via=Newspapers.com|last=Lee|first=Betty}}</ref> featured in the film (located at 6 Clarendon Crescent in [[South Hill, Toronto|South Hill]]) had been discovered by Clark while scouting for locations, and its owners agreed to lease the home for the production.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/420505/is-the-house-in-black-christmas-the-best-character-in-the-movie-horror-reel-estate/|title=Is the House in 'Black Christmas' the Best Character in the Movie? [Horror Reel Estate]|first=Drew|last=Tinnin|date=December 24, 2021|website=[[Dread Central]]|archive-url=https://archive.today/20251215002058/https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/420505/is-the-house-in-black-christmas-the-best-character-in-the-movie-horror-reel-estate/|archive-date=December 15, 2025|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name=onscreen/> Additional photography was completed on the [[University of Toronto]] campus.<ref name=onscreen/> According to John Saxon, Clark had meticulously drawn out storyboards with key shots, which he brought to the film set each day: "I could understand exactly what I thought he needed, and the scene needed".<ref name=onscreen/> 

Scenes in the film involving [[POV shot]]s of Billy scaling the house were accomplished through the use of a rig designed by [[camera operator]] Bert Dunk, which was attached to Dunk's head as he climbed up the side of the house.<ref name="iconsoffright05" /> Griffin's death scene, which was shot with a handheld camera in a real closet, was accomplished in only a couple of takes. According to Griffin, her character's surprise as the killer lunges from the closet was genuine as the actress later recalled: "It was a total shock because I didn't really know when to expect him to jump out!" Shots of Clare's corpse in the rocking chair required the actress to wear an actual plastic bag over her head for extended periods of time. Griffin would also state that these scenes came relatively easy for her: "I was actually, and still am, a fairly good swimmer so I could hold my breath for a long time. And I could also keep my eyes open for a long time without blinking".<ref name="griffininterview11" />

Kidder remembered shooting the film as being "fun. I really bonded with Andrea Martin, filming in Toronto and Ontario. Olivia Hussey was a bit of an odd one. She was obsessed with the idea of falling in love with [[Paul McCartney]] through her psychic. We were a little hard on her for things like that".<ref>{{cite web|title=Random Roles: Margot Kidder|url=https://avclub.com/article/random-roles-margot-kidder-24554|work=[[The A.V. Club]]|date=March 3, 2009|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191115051911/https://www.avclub.com/random-roles-margot-kidder-1798215851|archive-date=November 15, 2019|last=Rabin|first=Nathan}}</ref>

===Post-production===
The composer of the film's score, Carl Zittrer, stated in an interview that he created the film's mysterious music by [[Prepared piano|tying forks, combs, and knives onto the strings of the piano]] to warp the sound of the keys.<ref name="Chiller"/> Zittrer also stated that he would distort the sound further by recording its sound onto an audiotape and make the sound slower.<ref name="Chiller"/> The audio for the disturbing phone calls was performed by multiple actors including Mancuso<ref name="TCM"/><ref name="Power2019"/> and director Bob Clark.<ref name="Chiller"/> Mancuso stated in an interview that he stood on his head during the recording sessions to compress his [[thorax]] and make his voice sound more demented.<ref name="filmreel"/> Mancuso spent only three days recording dialogue for the character, later recalling the experience as being very "[[avant-garde]]", with Clark encouraging him to improvise with the character's voice.<ref name="Power2019"/>

During preparation in 1975 for the film's American release, [[Warner Bros. Pictures|Warner Bros.]] studio executives asked Clark to change the concluding scene to show Clare's boyfriend, Chris, appear in front of Jess and say, "Agnes, don't tell them what we did" before killing her. However, Clark insisted on keeping the ending ambiguous. The original title of the film was initially planned to be ''Stop Me''.<ref name="TCM"/> Clark has stated in an interview that he came up with the film's official title, saying that he enjoyed the irony of a dark event occurring during a festive holiday. According to Clark as well, Warner Bros. changed the title to ''Silent Night, Evil Night'' for the United States theatrical release.<ref name="Chiller"/>

==Release==
===Theatrical run===

In the United States, the film was released in May 1975 by [[Warner Bros. Pictures|Warner Bros.]], who initially issued it under the [[alternative title|alternate title]] ''Silent Night, Evil Night'' due to fears that the original title would mislead audiences into believing the film was a [[blaxploitation]] picture.{{sfn|Thrower|2017|p=8}} Warner Bros. retracted the title after the initial release, restoring it to ''Black Christmas'' for subsequent screenings.<ref name=onscreen/> The film was re-released in select theaters in [[Los Angeles]] under its original title on August 6, 1975.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-los-angeles-times/99919410/|title=Black Christmas: Starts Wednesday|date=August 3, 1975|work=[[Los Angeles Times]]|p=4|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> The released expanded to other major U.S. cities through October 1975, including [[Austin, Texas|Austin]],<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/austin-american-statesman/186847775/|work=[[Austin American-Statesman]]|date=October 24, 1975|p=31|title=Black Christmas|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> [[Chicago]],<ref>{{cite news|work=[[Chicago Tribune]]|date=October 25, 1975|title=2 Shock Features|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/chicago-tribune/186847863/|via=Newspapers.com|p=13}}</ref> [[Miami]],<ref name=VonMaurer>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-miami-news/186847928/|work=[[Miami News]]|last=Von Maurer|first=Bill|title='Tis the season for obscene phone calls|p=6A|via=Newspapers.com|date=October 11, 1975}}</ref> New York City,<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/newsday-nassau-edition/186847714/|work=[[Newsday]]|title=Black Christmas|date=October 17, 1975|p=40A|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> and [[Portland, Oregon]].<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-oregonian/186847668/|work=[[The Oregonian]]|title=Black Christmas|date=October 29, 1975|p=D6|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref>

In the United Kingdom, the film was released in London on September 25, 1975.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/evening-standard/186848053/|work=[[Evening Standard]]|title=Black Christmas (Cert. X; ABC 2 Shaftesbury Avenue)|via=Newspapers.com|p=23}}</ref>

To commemorate the film's 50th anniversary, a theatrical re-release for the film was held from December 7–22, 2024 in select cinemas across the United States, in a restored 4K format for the first time from the original camera negative.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Dunn |first=Jack |date=November 21, 2024|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250802234059/https://variety.com/2024/film/news/american-genre-film-archive-black-christmas-50th-anniversary-4k-rerelease-1236214645/|archive-date=August 2, 2025|url-status=live |title=American Genre Film Archive to Give 'Black Christmas' 50th Anniversary 4K Re-release (EXCLUSIVE) |url=https://variety.com/2024/film/news/american-genre-film-archive-black-christmas-50th-anniversary-4k-rerelease-1236214645/|website=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] |language=en-US}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |date=November 21, 2024|last=Boccella|first=Maggie |title=Black Christmas Returns To Theaters In 4K Next Month |url=https://www.fangoria.com/black-christmas-4k-theatrical-re-release/ |url-status=live|archive-url=https://archive.today/20241220161140/https://www.fangoria.com/black-christmas-4k-theatrical-re-release/|archive-date=December 20, 2024|website=[[Fangoria]] |language=en-US}}</ref> 

===Marketing===
For its second U.S. theatrical run, Warner Bros. devised a marketing gimmick in which individuals could call a publicized telephone number which responded with an automated, sinister message promoting the film.{{sfn|Thrower|2017|p=9}}<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-los-angeles-times/99771740/|work=[[Los Angeles Times]]|date=October 1, 1975|title=Black Christmas Rocks Los Angeles!|p=10|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> The phone number received over 10,000 calls in the New York City area within 24 hours of its publication, tying up the studio's phone line and resulting in the New York Telephone Company requesting them to retract the promotional materials advertising the number.{{sfn|Thrower|2017|p=9}} Warner Bros. complied, and also cancelled a planned promotional event in which two women dressed in [[Santa suit]]s were to hand out cards with the number on the streets of Manhattan.{{sfn|Thrower|2017|p=9}}

===Television programming===
The film, under the title ''Stranger in the House'', was set to make its network television premiere on Saturday night, January 28, 1978, on [[NBC]]'s weekly "Saturday Night at the Movies".{{sfn|Thrower|2017|p=22}} Two weeks prior to its premiere, the [[Chi Omega]] sorority house on the campus of [[Florida State University]] in [[Tallahassee, Florida|Tallahassee]] was the scene of a double murder in which two Chi Omega sisters, asleep in their beds, were bludgeoned to death. The killer then went to a nearby room in the sorority house and violently attacked two more sleeping students, who survived. The killer was later identified as [[Ted Bundy]], who was executed for this and other homicides on January 24, 1989.<ref name="ap">{{Cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/image/134823910/?terms=%22Stranger%2Bin%2Bthe%2BHouse%22|title=Network Offers TV Alternative for Terror Film|agency=Associated Press|date=January 25, 1978|work=[[The Palm Beach Post]]|page=61|access-date=July 14, 2016|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref>

A few days before the film was set to premiere on network television Florida's then-Governor [[Reubin Askew]] contacted NBC President Robert Mullholland to request the movie not be shown due to its all-too-similar theme as the murders of sorority sisters by an unknown madman at the Chi Omega Sorority House. On Tuesday, January 24, NBC-TV gave several of its affiliates in Florida, Georgia, and Alabama, the option of showing an alternate film, ''[[Doc Savage: The Man of Bronze]]'', in place of ''Stranger in the House''.<ref name="ap"/> The report revealed that "the network said in a statement issued yesterday in New York City that it was responding to concern voiced by the affiliates because of the murder of two coeds this month in a sorority house at Florida State University in Tallahassee".<ref name="ap"/>

==Reception==
===Box office===
The film grossed $143,000 from nine theatres in Toronto in its first two weeks and earned $1.3 million during its theatrical run in Canada.{{sfn|Nowell|2011|p=76}} The film went on to become the third-highest-grossing Canadian film of all time in Canada with a gross of $2 million, behind ''[[The Apprenticeship of Duddy Kravitz (film)|The Apprenticeship of Duddy Kravitz]]'' (1974) and the French language ''Deux femmes en or'' (1970), directed by [[Claude Fournier (filmmaker)|Claude Fournier]].<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=24 November 1976|title=Canadian Films Grosses|page=32}}</ref><ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=November 21, 1979|title=Canada-Only B.O. Figures|page=24}}</ref>

Warner Bros., who had acquired distribution rights in the United States, estimated that the film would earn at least $7 million there.{{sfn|Nowell|2011|p=75}} The film was released in the United States on December 20, 1974, under the alternate title ''Silent Night, Evil Night'', but only earned $284,345 during its theatrical run due to competition from ''[[The Godfather Part II]]'' and ''[[The Man with the Golden Gun (film)|The Man with the Golden Gun]]''.{{sfn|Nowell|2011|pages=75-76}}

Warner Bros. re-released the film in Los Angeles in August 1975 under its original title, and it earned $86,340 in one week.{{sfn|Nowell|2011|pages=76–77}} Its theatrical run was extended to nineteen theatres.{{sfn|Nowell|2011|p=77}} The film's successful run in Los Angeles and Chicago resulted in Warner Bros. expanding the film to seventy theatres for [[Halloween]].{{sfn|Nowell|2011|p=77}} However, the film only made $354,990 from those theatres, averaging $700 per theatre per day, causing fifty-eight of the locations to cancel their bookings.{{sfn|Nowell|2011|p=77}} The film ended its U.S. theatrical run in December 1975 after making less than $1 million that year.{{sfn|Nowell|2011|pages=76-77}}

===Critical response===
====Contemporary====
''Black Christmas'' garnered largely unfavorable reviews in its home country of Canada.{{sfn|Thrower|2017|pages=8–10}} ''[[The Globe and Mail]]''{{'}}s Martin Knelman gave the film an unflattering review, observing that director Clark's "klutzy inventiveness is boundless...&nbsp;Clark may not have much talent, but he certainly must have salesmanship. It's amazing how many familiar performers have been talked into risking their reputation by playing this material as if they believed it."<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-globe-and-mail/186842785/|work=[[The Globe and Mail]]|last=Knelman|first=Martin|date=October 16, 1974|title=Black Christmas: film industry survival?|p=12|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> Jamie Portman, writing for the ''[[Calgary Herald]]'', lambasted the film as "trash", deeming the screenplay "inept, cliche-ridden and juvenile", summarizing the film as a "senseless piece of mayhem".<ref name=Portman/> Natalie Edwards of ''[[Cinema Canada]]'' offered some praise for the film, particularly for Kidder and Martin's performances, as well as the cinematography and use of locations, but was ambivalent of the film's themes, noting:{{quote|The satisfactions of a film in which upper middle-class females, sharp and sexy, are terrorized and brutally or gruesomely destroyed can be easily seen to appeal to all misogynists, insecure and frustrated men, and a thwarted and denied [[working class]] who resent college kids, liberated women, intellectual and particularly artistic males, and, quite possibly, the expense of Christmas.<ref>{{cite journal|url=https://cinemacanada.athabascau.ca/index.php/cinema/article/download/1976/2031.pdf|work=[[Cinema Canada]]|title=Black Christmas|last=Edwards|first=Natalie|p=78|date=December 1974|issue=17|issn=0009-7071|via=[[Athabasca University]]|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251218062814/https://cinemacanada.athabascau.ca/index.php/cinema/article/download/1976/2031.pdf|archive-date=December 18, 2025}}</ref> }}

[[A. H. Weiler]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' called it "a whodunit that raises the question as to why was it made".<ref>{{Cite news |date= October 20, 1975|last=Weiler|first=A. H.|author-link=A. H. Weiler|archive-url=https://archive.today/20240417235144/https://www.nytimes.com/2018/12/11/movies/black-christmas-review.html|archive-date=April 17, 2024|url-status=live |title=Screen: Murky Whodunit: 'Black Christmas' Is at Local Theaters |newspaper=[[The New York Times]] |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2018/12/11/movies/black-christmas-review.html |page=45}}</ref> ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' called the film "a bloody, senseless kill-for-kicks feature, [that] exploits unnecessary violence in a university sorority house operated by an implausibly alcoholic ex-hoofer. Its slow-paced, murky tale involves an obscene telephone caller who apparently delights in killing the girls off one by one, even the hapless house-mother".<ref>{{cite web|work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|title=Black Christmas|date=December 31, 1974|access-date=November 18, 2017|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131231231035/https://variety.com/1973/film/reviews/black-christmas-1200423278/|archive-date=December 31, 2013|url=https://variety.com/1973/film/reviews/black-christmas-1200423278/}}</ref> [[Gene Siskel]] of the ''[[Chicago Tribune]]'' gave the film 1.5 stars out of 4 and called it a "routine shocker" that "is notable only for indicating the kind of junk roles that talented actresses are forced to play in the movies".<ref>{{cite news|author=Siskel, Gene|author-link=Gene Siskel|date=October 6, 1975|title='Master Gunfighter' a whopping misfire"|work=[[Chicago Tribune]]|p=6|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/chicago-tribune-the-master-gunfighter/133496167/|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> 

[[Kevin Thomas (film critic)|Kevin Thomas]] of the ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' was more favorable, noting: "Before it maddeningly overreaches in a gratuitously evasive ending, ''Black Christmas''...&nbsp;is a smart, stylish Canadian-made little horror picture that is completely diverting...&nbsp;It may well be that its makers simply couldn't figure out how to end it".<ref>{{cite news|last=Thomas|first=Kevin|author-link=Kevin Thomas (film critic)|date=August 6, 1975|title=Gothic Tale of a 'Black Christmas'|work=[[Los Angeles Times]]|p=12|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-los-angeles-times/186843132/|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref>

Heidi Martinuzzi of ''[[Film Threat]]'' called the film "innovative" and praised the leading actresses, Olivia Hussey and Margot Kidder.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://filmthreat.com/reviews/6782/ |title=Black Christmas |work=[[Film Threat]] |date= December 24, 2004|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120918040523/https://filmthreat.com/reviews/6782/|archive-date=September 18, 2012}}</ref> ''[[TV Guide]]'' awarded the film three out of four stars, writing: "Although strictly standard fare, the material is elevated somewhat through Clark's skillful handling of such plot devices as obscene phone calls from the killer to the girls via the upstairs phone and a nicely handled twist ending, which provides a genuine shock".<ref>{{cite web|work=[[TV Guide]]|title=Black Christmas (1974) Review|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171201034558/http://www.tvguide.com/movies/black-christmas/review/108583/|archive-date=December 1, 2017|url=http://www.tvguide.com/movies/black-christmas/review/108583/}}</ref>
Author and film critic [[Leonard Maltin]] gave the film two and a half out of four stars calling it "bizarre" but also praised Kidder's performance as a standout.{{sfn|Maltin|2013|p=137}} The ''[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out]]'' film guide noted that the film "manages a good slice of old-fashioned suspense".<ref>{{cite web|url=https://timeout.com/film/reviews/67908/black_christmas.html|work=[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out]]|publisher=[[Time Inc.]]|location=London|title=Black Christmas|access-date=November 9, 2017|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121107083055/https://timeout.com/film/reviews/67908/black_christmas.html|archive-date=November 7, 2012}}</ref>

== Legacy ==
''Black Christmas'' gained a [[Cult film|cult following]] in the years after its original release, and is widely regarded by critics and film historians as one of the earliest [[slasher film]]s.<ref name=Lindsey/>{{sfn|Markusen|2021|p=209}}{{sfn|Muir|2011|p=315}}{{sfn|Paszylk|2009|pages=136–137}}{{sfn|Cullen|Patterson|Allon|2000|p=80}} It brought together various tropes, like using [[Point-of-view shot|point-of-view shots]] from the killer's perspective (common in earlier Italian [[Giallo|giallo]] films), and so formulated a template for subsequent American slasher films.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.slashfilm.com/729216/how-black-christmas-changed-slasher-movies-forever/|work=[[/Film]]|title=How Black Christmas Changed Slasher Movies Forever|last=Colangelo|first=BJ|date=January 10, 2022|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241223105135/https://www.slashfilm.com/729216/how-black-christmas-changed-slasher-movies-forever/|archive-date=December 23, 2024}}</ref> In particular, [[John Carpenter]]'s ''[[Halloween (1978 film)|Halloween]]'' (1978), which was apparently inspired by Clark suggesting what a ''Black Christmas'' sequel would be like.<ref>{{cite web|last=Squires|first=John|title=How 'Halloween' Was Basically an Unofficial 'Black Christmas' Sequel|url=https://bloody-disgusting.com/editorials/3413469/halloween-basically-unofficial-black-christmas-sequel|website=[[Bloody Disgusting]]|date=November 11, 2016|archive-url=https://archive.today/20251215002146/https://bloody-disgusting.com/editorials/3413469/halloween-basically-unofficial-black-christmas-sequel/|archive-date=December 15, 2025|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name=onscreen>{{cite episode|people=Orchard, Tristan (dir.); Clark, Bob; Kidder, Margot; Dullea, Keir|display-authors=etal|series=On Screen!|publisher=Canadian Television Fund|title= Black Christmas|airdate=July 22, 2005|url=https://www.imdb.com/title/tt1074369/}}</ref>{{sfn|Muir|2011|p=315}}

9, 2024}}</ref> It was ranked at No. 67 in ''[[IndieWire]]''{{'}}s ''The 100 Best Horror Movies of All Time'', its entry stating that "the plot sounds formulaic, but ''Black Christmas'' remains timeless thanks to its terrifying and elusive killer, 'Billy', whose backstory is never revealed, as well as a foreboding ending that doesn't offer much hope for the film's Final Girl".<ref While director Clark maintained he did not intend for the film to have political leanings, critics have noted ''Black Christmas'' is nonetheless a [[Feminism|feminist]] film for its treatment of female characters—particularly Jess having agency and making the choice to have an abortion—and its portrayal of casual misogyny (as when the police initially fail to take the sorority's concerns about the phone calls and Clare's absence seriously).<ref>{{Cite web |last=Monagle |first=Matthew |date=December 13, 2019 |title=The Politics of Slasher Black Christmas |url=https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/2019-12-13/the-politics-of-slasher-black-christmas/ |archive-url=https://archive.today/20251215050755/https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/the-politics-of-slasher-black-christmas-11776392/|archive-date=December 15, 2025|url-status=live |website=[[The Austin Chronicle]] |language=en-US}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Romano |first=Aja |date=19 December 2017 |title=Skip Die Hard this year. Hussey initially thought he was referring to ''Romeo and Juliet'', but was surprised when Martin said it was ''Black Christmas'' and that he had seen the film 27 times.<ref>{{cite web|last=Stitzel|first=Kelly|title=Horror Movie Marathon: Part The Last|url=http://popdose.com/horror-movie-marathon-part-the-last/|work=Popdose|date=October 31, 2012|archive-url=https://archive.today/20251215050344/https://popdose.com/horror-movie-marathon-part-the-last/|archive-date=December 15, 2025|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Novelization===
A [[novelization]] of the film written by Lee Hays was published in 1976 by Popular Library.<ref>{{cite book|author=Hays, Lee|title=Black Christmas|year=1976|publisher=Popular Library|location=New York City, New York|oclc=17395520}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.thrillist.com/entertainment/nation/black-christmas-review-first-slasher-film-genre|title=The Low-Budget Canadian Film That Inspired 'Halloween' and Launched an Entire Horror Genre|last=Searles|first=Jourdain|date=October 30, 2018|website=[[Thrillist]]|archive-url=https://archive.today/20251215050904/https://www.thrillist.com/entertainment/nation/black-christmas-review-first-slasher-film-genre|archive-date=December 15, 2025|url-status=live}}</ref> The book follows roughly the same plot, but sticks with Roy Moore's original script. Because of this, the novel ends up fleshing out the characters more, adding scenes and lines of dialogue that were initially cut from the film's final script, and giving the Pi Kappa Sigma sorority more backstory.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://bloody-disgusting.com/books/3645326/slasher-classic-print-unwrapping-black-christmas-novelization/ | title=A Slasher Classic in Print: Unwrapping the 'Black Christmas' Novelization | date=December 17, 2020|work=[[Bloody Disgusting]]|archive-url=https://archive.today/20251215032732/https://bloody-disgusting.com/books/3645326/slasher-classic-print-unwrapping-black-christmas-novelization/|archive-date=December 15, 2025|url-status=live|author= Lê, Paul }}</ref>

===Remakes===
{{Main|Black Christmas (2006 film){{!}}Black Christmas (2006 film)|Black Christmas (2019 film){{!}}Black Christmas (2019 film)}}

''Black Christmas'' has been remade on two separate occasions, with the films differing significantly from the original.

The [[Black Christmas (2006 film)|first remake]] was written and directed by [[Glen Morgan]] and was released on December 25, 2006, by [[Dimension Films]] and [[MGM Distribution Co.]] It is loosely based on the original film, containing more graphic content and a focus into the past of Billy. [[Andrea Martin]] was the only original cast member to appear in the film, and Clark served as an executive producer.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://variety.com/2007/film/markets-festivals/bob-clark-67-director-1117962526/|title=Bob Clark, 67, director|last=Garrett|first=Diane|work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=April 4, 2007|archive-url=https://archive.today/20251215032950/https://variety.com/2007/film/markets-festivals/bob-clark-67-director-1117962526/|archive-date=December 15, 2025|url-status=live}}</ref>

The [[Black Christmas (2019 film)|second remake]] was directed and co-written by [[Sophia Takal]], and starring [[Imogen Poots]] and [[Cary Elwes]].<ref name=Sneider/> The film was released on December 13, 2019, by [[Universal Pictures]].<ref name=Sneider>{{cite web|url=https://collider.com/blumhouse-black-christmas-remake-sophia-takal-imogen-poots/|title=Blumhouse to Remake 'Black Christmas' with Director Sophia Takal|last=Sneider|first=Jeff|website=[[Collider (website)|Collider]]|date=June 13, 2019|access-date=June 14, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190614002737/https://collider.com/blumhouse-black-christmas-remake-sophia-takal-imogen-poots/|archive-date=June 14, 2019}}</ref>

===Fan films===
A short fan film funded through an [[Indiegogo]] campaign was released on YouTube and Vimeo with the title ''It's Me, Billy'', in 2021. It is billed as an "unofficial sequel" to the original film and is set fifty years after the original, following the granddaughter of Jess Bradford.
