'''''My Own Private Idaho''''' is a 1991 American [[independent film|independent]] [[adventure film|adventure]] [[drama (film and television)|drama]] film written and directed by [[Gus Van Sant]], loosely based on [[William Shakespeare]]'s ''[[Henry IV, Part 1]]''; ''[[Henry IV, Part 2]]''; and ''[[Henry V (play)|Henry V]]''. The story follows two friends, [[Mikey Waters|Mike Waters]] and Scott Favor, played by [[River Phoenix]] and [[Keanu Reeves]], respectively, as they embark on a journey of personal discovery that takes them from [[Portland, Oregon]], to Mike's hometown in [[Idaho]], and then to [[Rome]] in search of Mike's mother.

''My Own Private Idaho'' had its premiere at the [[48th Venice International Film Festival]], and received largely positive reviews from critics, including [[Roger Ebert]] and those of ''[[The New York Times]]'' and ''[[Entertainment Weekly]]''. The film was a moderate financial success, grossing over $8 million, above its estimated budget of $2.5 million. Phoenix received several accolades for his performance in the film, including the [[Volpi Cup for Best Actor]] at the 1991 Venice Film Festival, [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Male Lead|Best Male Lead]] from the [[Independent Spirit Awards]], and [[National Society of Film Critics Award for Best Actor|Best Actor]] from the [[National Society of Film Critics]].

''My Own Private Idaho'' is considered a landmark film in [[new queer cinema]], an early 1990s movement in [[queer]]-themed independent filmmaking.<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Smith |first=Nathan |title=Twenty-five years of New Queer Cinema |magazine=[[Times Higher Education]] |date=February 5, 2015 |url=https://www.timeshighereducation.com/features/culture/twenty-five-years-of-new-queer-cinema/2018251.article |access-date=April 4, 2018}}</ref> Since its release, it has grown in popularity and been deemed a [[cult film|cult classic]], especially among LGBT audiences. The film is notable for its then-taboo subject matter and [[avant-garde]] style.<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Portwood |first=Jerry |title=The Enduring Power of ''My Own Private Idaho'' |magazine=[[Out (magazine)|Out]] |date=October 25, 2015 |url=https://www.out.com/movies/2015/10/25/enduring-power-my-own-private-idaho |access-date=April 4, 2018}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |last=Collins |first=K. Austin |title='My Own Private Idaho' Is a Queer Masterpiece |website=[[The Ringer (website)|The Ringer]] |date=September 29, 2016 |url=https://www.theringer.com/2016/9/29/16043752/my-own-private-idaho-gus-van-sant-520b847ed1f |access-date=April 4, 2018}}</ref>

In 2024, the film was selected for preservation in the United States [[National Film Registry]] by the [[Library of Congress]] as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".<ref name="2024NFR">{{cite web|title=25 Films Added to National Film Registry for Preservation|url= https://newsroom.loc.gov/news/25-films-named-to-national-film-registry-for-preservation/s/55d5285d-916f-4105-b7d4-7fc3ba8664e3|date=December 17, 2024|access-date=December 17, 2024}}</ref>

==Plot==
Mike, a [[Male prostitution|street hustler]], stands alone on a deserted stretch of highway. He starts talking to himself and notices that the road looks "like someone's face, like a fucked-up face". He then experiences a [[Narcolepsy|narcoleptic]] episode and dreams of his mother comforting him as home movies of his childhood play in his mind.

Later, after receiving [[fellatio]] from a client in Seattle, Mike returns to his favorite spots to pick up clients. He is picked up by a wealthy older woman who takes him to her mansion, where he finds two fellow hustlers she has also hired. One of them is Scott Favor, Mike's best friend, and the other is Gary. While preparing to have sex with the woman, Mike has another narcoleptic episode and awakens the next day with Scott in Portland, Oregon.

Mike and Scott are soon reunited with Bob Pigeon, a middle-aged mentor to a gang of street kids and hustlers who live in an abandoned apartment building. Scott, the son of the mayor of Portland, confides in Bob that when he turns 21, he will inherit his father's fortune and retire from street hustling. Meanwhile, Mike yearns to find his mother, so he and Scott leave for Idaho to visit Mike's older brother, Richard.

Along this journey, Mike confesses to Scott that he is in love with him, and Scott gently reminds Mike he only sleeps with men for money. Richard tells a story of a man he claims is Mike's father, but Mike insists that he knows it is Richard. Richard informs Mike that their mother works as a hotel maid; when Mike and Scott visit her workplace, they learn she went to Italy in search of her own family. At the hotel, they meet Hans, the man who drove them to Portland, and prostitute themselves to him.

With the money they receive from Hans, Mike and Scott travel to Italy. They find the country farmhouse where Mike's mother worked as a maid and English tutor. Carmela, a young woman who lives there, tells Mike that his mother returned to the United States months earlier. Carmela and Scott fall in love and return to the US, leaving a brokenhearted Mike to return on his own. Scott's father dies, and Scott inherits his fortune.

Back in Portland, Bob and his gang confront a reformed Scott at an upscale restaurant, but he rejects them. That night, Bob has a fatal heart attack. The next day, the hustlers hold a rowdy funeral for Bob, while in the same cemetery, within shouting distance, Scott attends a solemn funeral for his father.

Finally, Mike is back on the deserted stretch of Idaho highway. After he falls into another narcoleptic stupor, two strangers pull up in a truck, take his backpack and shoes, and drive away. Moments later, an unidentified figure pulls up in a car, picks the unconscious Mike up, places him in the vehicle, and drives away.

==Development==
''My Own Private Idaho'' originated from [[John Rechy]]'s 1963 novel ''[[City of Night]]'', which featured street hustlers who did not admit to being gay.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxi}} Van Sant's original screenplay was written in the 1970s, when he was living in [[Hollywood, Los Angeles|Hollywood]].{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxiii}} After reading Rechy's book, Van Sant felt it was considerably better than what he was writing, and shelved the script for years. In 1988, while editing ''[[Mala Noche]]'', Van Sant met street kid Michael Parker, who inspired the character of Mike in ''My Own Private Idaho''.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxiii}} Parker also had a friend named Scott, a street kid like himself. In the script, Van Sant made the Scott character a rich kid, also fashioned after street hustlers Van Sant had met in Portland.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxiii}}

Early drafts of the screenplay were set on [[Hollywood Boulevard]], not Portland, with working titles such as ''Blue Funk'' and ''Minions of the Moon''.<ref name="Elder, Sean">{{cite magazine|last=Elder|first=Sean|title=Young Actors Go Wild with Gus Van Sant|magazine=[[Elle (magazine)|Elle]]|date=October 1991}}</ref> Rechy's novel inspired Van Sant to change the setting to Portland.<ref name="Ehrenstein, David">{{cite magazine|last=Ehrenstein|first=David|title=Back to Idaho|magazine=[[The Advocate (magazine)|The Advocate]]|date=April 12, 2005}}</ref> The script originally consisted of two separate scenarios: the first, titled ''Modern Days'', recounted Mike's story while the second updated the ''[[Henry IV (Shakespeare play)|Henry IV]]'' plays with Scott's story.<ref name="Lyons, Donald">{{cite book|last=Lyons|first=Donald|url=https://archive.org/details/independentvisio0000lyon/page/232/mode/2up|title=Independent Visions: A Critical Introduction to Recent Independent American Film|location=New York|publisher=[[Ballantine Books]]|year=1994|pages=232–3|isbn=978-0-345-38249-8|url-access=registration}}</ref> Van Sant realized he could blend the two stories together in the manner of [[William S. Burroughs]]' "cut up" technique.<ref name="Lyons, Donald"/> In essence, this method involves various story fragments and ideas mixed and matched together to form a unique story. The idea to combine the two scenarios occurred to Van Sant after seeing [[Orson Welles]]'s ''[[Chimes at Midnight]]''.<ref name="Lyons, Donald"/> He has said, "I thought that the ''Henry IV'' plays were really a street story. I also knew this fat guy named Bob, who had always reminded me of [[John Falstaff|Falstaff]] and who was crazy about hustler boys". Van Sant realized that [[Prince Hal]] in the plays resembled Scott and the sidekick was Mike. His script ended up as a restructuring of the ''Henry IV'' plays.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxv}} Van Sant got the idea for Mike's narcolepsy from a man who was a guide of sorts when the director was gathering material for the film and who always looked like he was about to fall asleep.<ref name="Bowen, Peter">{{cite magazine|last=Bowen|first=Peter|title=His Own Private Idaho|magazine=Off-Hollywood Report|volume=6|issue=3|date=Fall 1991}}</ref> The film's title is derived from the song "[[Private Idaho (song)|Private Idaho]]" by [[the B-52's]], which Van Sant heard while visiting the state in the early 1980s.<ref name="Greenberg, Harvey">{{cite journal|last=Greenberg|first=Harvey|title=''My Own Private Idaho''|journal=[[Film Quarterly]]|date=Fall 1992}}</ref>

Van Sant showed the script to a [[20th Century Fox]] executive who liked Shakespeare.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxv}} Eventually, he toned down the Shakespearean elements and modernized the language. Van Sant was also working on a "My Own Private Idaho" short story that he intended to film. Twenty-five pages long, it concerned two Latino characters on the Portland streets who go in search of their parents and travel to a town in Spain. One falls in love with a girl and leaves the other behind.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxv}} Van Sant had another script, ''The Boys of Storytown'', containing the Mike and Scott characters, as well as Hans and Bob; Van Sant wanted to make the film but felt the script was unfinished.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxvi}} Ultimately, while editing ''[[Drugstore Cowboy]]'', he combined the scripts for ''Modern Days'' and ''Storytown'' with the "Idaho" short story.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxvi}}

Initially, no studio would finance the film because of its controversial and offbeat subject matter. After ''Drugstore Cowboy'' received favorable critical raves and awards, studios started to show some interest,<ref name="Greenberg, Harvey"/> but they all wanted revisions. Frustrated, Van Sant attempted to make the film on a shoestring budget with a cast of actual street kids, including Michael Parker and actor [[Rodney Harvey]], who was going to play Scott.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxvi}}

When casting the two central roles, Van Sant sent the script to [[Keanu Reeves]] and [[River Phoenix]], assuming it would be rejected by their agents.<ref name="Robb, Brian">{{cite book|last=Robb|first=Brian J.|url=https://archive.org/details/riverphoenixshor0000robb|title=River Phoenix: A Short Life|location=New York|publisher=[[Harper Perennial]]|year=1995|isbn=978-0-06-095132-0|url-access=registration}}</ref> Reeves' agent was amenable to the project, but Phoenix's would not even show it to him.<ref name="Greenberg, Harvey" /> Van Sant had the idea to get Reeves to personally deliver the film treatment to Phoenix at his home in Florida.<ref name="Robb, Brian" /> Reeves did so during the winter holiday season, riding his 1974 [[Norton Commando]] motorcycle from his family home in Canada to the Phoenix family ranch in [[Micanopy, Florida]].<ref name="Robb, Brian" /> Reeves had worked previously with Phoenix on [[Lawrence Kasdan]]'s 1990 film ''[[I Love You to Death]]'' and with his brother [[Joaquin Phoenix]] and girlfriend [[Martha Plimpton]] on [[Ron Howard]]'s 1989 ''[[Parenthood (film)|Parenthood]]''. After reading the treatment, Phoenix agreed to play Scott, but since Van Sant had already cast Reeves in the role, they had to convince River to take the edgier role of Mike. Van Sant promised not to make either actor do anything embarrassing.<ref name="Block, Adam">{{cite magazine|last=Block|first=Adam|title=Inside Outsider Gus Van Sant|magazine=[[The Advocate (magazine)|The Advocate]]|date=September 24, 1991}}</ref> He got an offer of $2 million from an outside investor, but when he delayed production for nine months so that Phoenix could make ''[[Dogfight (film)|Dogfight]]'' (1991), the investor pulled out.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxxvi}} Producer Laurie Parker shopped the script around; at the time, [[New Line Cinema]] was in the process of branching out into producing [[arthouse]] films and decided to back Van Sant's vision with a $2.5 million budget.<ref name="Greenberg, Harvey" /> In a 2012 interview, [[Kiefer Sutherland]] said that he declined Van Sant's offer to star in the lead role because he wanted to go skiing, a decision he has said he regrets.<ref>{{Cite web |date=9 March 2012 |title=Sutherland turned down My Own Private Idaho to go skiing |url=https://www.express.co.uk/celebrity-news/307136/Sutherland-turned-down-My-Own-Private-Idaho-to-go-skiing |access-date=2024-12-17 |website=[[Daily Express|Express]] |language=en}}</ref>

===Filming===
Principal photography took place in November and December 1990, primarily in [[Portland, Oregon|Portland]], [[Seattle]], and [[Rome]].<ref>{{cite web|title=My Own Private Idaho (1991)|website=[[AFI Catalog of Feature Films]]|url=https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/MovieDetails/58952|access-date=4 April 2018}}</ref> Scenes of the Idaho road depicted in the film were shot near [[Maupin, Oregon]], on [[Oregon Route 216]]. Phoenix arrived in Portland two weeks before principal photography to begin research, with Van Sant remembering, "He seemed to be changing into this character".<ref name="PremiereMag">{{cite magazine|title=A Brief Life|magazine=[[Première (magazine)|Première]]|date=March 1994}}</ref> One of the film's directors of photography, Eric Alan Edwards, recalled that Phoenix "looked like a street kid", and "in a very raw way he wore that role. I've never seen anybody so intent on living his role".<ref name="PremiereMag" /> Several cast and crew members, including Michael Parker, Phoenix, Reeves, and [[Flea (musician)|Flea]], lived together in a house in Portland during filming. A couple of times a week, they would play music together. Due to the low budget, a typical day of shooting started at 6 am and ended at 11 pm.<ref name="PremiereMag" />

The film was not [[storyboard]]ed and was made without a shot list.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxxiv}} The campfire scene, originally just three pages,{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxxiv}} was expanded by Phoenix into an eight-page scene in which Mike professes his love for Scott, thereby making the character's homosexuality obvious, as opposed to Van Sant's more ambiguous original version. Phoenix described his process as his "own stream-of-consciousness, and this just happened to be one that was more than actor notes. Then Keanu and I refined it, worked on it... but it was all done quickly. It was something I wrote down a night, two nights, before, and then I showed it to Keanu and Gus... And Gus kept the whole thing. He didn't pare it down. It's a long scene." Phoenix said that neither he nor Reeves felt uncomfortable filming ''Idaho''{{'}}s gay scenes, and noted that their existing friendship helped add to the chemistry between their characters.<ref>{{cite news |last=Rea |first=Steven |url=http://www.whoaisnotme.net/articles/1991_1013_ane.htm |title=An Eye on the Private River Phoenix |newspaper=[[The Philadelphia Inquirer]] |date=October 13, 1991 |url-access= |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120917075543/http://www.whoaisnotme.net/articles/1991_1013_ane.htm |archive-date=September 17, 2012 |url-status=dead |via=Whoa Is (Not) Me}}</ref> When asked if he was worried that playing a gay prostitute might hurt his public image, Reeves said, "Hurt my image? Who am I—a politician? [laughs softly] No. I'm an actor. That wasn't a problem."<ref>{{cite magazine |last1=Powell |first1=Paige |last2=Sikes |first2=Gini |url=https://www.interviewmagazine.com/film/my-own-private-idaho |title=My Own Private Idaho |magazine=[[Interview (magazine)|Interview]] |date=November 1991}}</ref>

Eric Edwards shot the [[time-lapse photography]] shots on his own.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxxix}} They were not in the script and the film's producer was worried that he was using too much film. Van Sant originally had the screen go black when Mike passed out, but was not satisfied with this approach. He used Edwards's footage to present Mike's perspective of "an altered sense of time".{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxxix}} Some New Line executives disliked the Shakespeare scenes and wanted Van Sant to cut them, but foreign distributors wanted as much Shakespeare in the film as possible.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=xxxviii}}

''My Own Private Idaho'' premiered at the [[48th Venice International Film Festival]] on September 4, 1991.{{sfn|Van Sant|1993|page=105}} It also received screenings at the 17th [[Deauville American Film Festival]], [[1991 Toronto International Film Festival|16th Toronto International Film Festival]]<ref name="MacInnis">{{cite news|last=MacInni|first=Craig|title=Festival of Festivals fills in its open spaces|newspaper=[[Toronto Star]]|date=August 21, 1991}}</ref> and the 29th [[New York Film Festival]].<ref name="Holden">{{cite news|last=Holden|first=Stephent|author-link=Stephen Holden|title=Film Festival: The Spirit Is the Focus|newspaper=[[The New York Times]]|date=September 20, 1991}}</ref> The film was released in [[limited release|select theaters]] in the United States on September 29, 1991. It grossed $6.4 million in the United States and Canada and $1.7 million in the United Kingdom.<ref name="boxoffice">{{cite web|title=''My Own Private Idaho'' (1991)|website=[[Box Office Mojo]]|url=https://www.boxofficemojo.com/title/tt0102494/|access-date=April 3, 2024}}</ref><ref name=2ndrd>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Screen International]]|date=September 3, 1993|page=16|title=Bard influence}}</ref>

===Critical response===
{{Anchor|Reviews|Critics}}
The film achieved critical acclaim. Film critic [[Roger Ebert]] wrote, "The achievement of this film is that it wants to evoke that state of drifting need, and it does. There is no mechanical plot that has to grind to a Hollywood conclusion, and no contrived test for the heroes to pass."
<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/my-own-private-idaho-1991|title=My Own Private Idaho|last=Ebert|first=Roger|author-link=Roger Ebert|date=October 18, 1991|newspaper=[[Chicago Sun-Times]]|access-date=September 1, 2023|via=[[RogerEbert.com]]}}</ref>
In his review for ''[[Rolling Stone]]'' magazine, [[Peter Travers]] wrote, "Van Sant's clear-eyed, unsentimental approach to a plot that pivots on betrayal and death is reflected in magnetic performances from Reeves and Phoenix."<ref name="Travers">{{cite magazine|last=Travers |first=Peter |author-link=Peter Travers |title=''My Own Private Idaho'' |magazine=[[Rolling Stone]] |date=October 17, 1991 |url=https://www.rollingstone.com/reviews/movie/5948985/review/5948986/my_own_private_idaho |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070329000126/http://www.rollingstone.com/reviews/movie/5948985/review/5948986/my_own_private_idaho |url-status=dead |archive-date=March 29, 2007 |access-date=2008-02-21}}</ref> [[Vincent Canby]], in his ''[[The New York Times|New York Times]]'' review, wrote, "The performances, especially by the two young stars, are as surprising as they are sure. Mr. Phoenix (''[[Dogfight (film)|Dogfight]]'') and Mr. Reeves (of the two ''Bill and Ted'' comedies) are very fine in what may be the two best roles they'll find in years. Roles of this density, for young actors, do not come by that often".<ref name="Canby">{{cite news |last=Canby |first=Vincent |author-link=Vincent Canby |title=A Road Movie About Male Hustlers |newspaper=[[The New York Times]] |date=September 27, 1991 |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1991/09/27/movies/reviews-film-festival-a-road-movie-about-male-hustlers.html |access-date=April 3, 2024}}</ref> In his review for ''[[Newsweek]]'', [[David Ansen]] praised Phoenix's performance: "The campfire scene in which Mike awkwardly declares his unrequited love for Scott is a marvel of delicacy. In this, and every scene, Phoenix immerses himself so deeply inside his character you almost forget you've seen him before: it's a stunningly sensitive performance, poignant and comic at once."<ref name="Ansen">{{cite magazine |last=Ansen |first=David |author-link=David Ansen |title=Turning Shakespearean Tricks |magazine=[[Newsweek]] |date=October 7, 1991}}</ref> [[Owen Gleiberman]] of ''[[Entertainment Weekly]]'' gave the film an "A−" rating and wrote, "When Van Sant shows us speeded-up images of clouds rolling past wheat fields, the familiar device transcends cliché, because it's tied to the way that Mike, in his benumbed isolation, experiences his own life—as a running piece of surrealism. The sheer, expressive beauty of those images haunted me for days."<ref name="Gleiberman">{{cite magazine |last=Gleiberman |first=Owen |author-link=Owen Gleiberman |title=''My Own Private Idaho'' |magazine=[[Entertainment Weekly]] |date=October 11, 1991 |url=http://www.ew.com/ew/article/0,,315771,00.html |access-date=2009-02-19 |archive-date=2014-08-08 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140808052639/http://www.ew.com/ew/article/0,,315771,00.html}}</ref> [[J. Hoberman]], in his review for ''[[The Village Voice]]'', wrote, "While Phoenix vanishes with reckless triumph into his role, Reeves stands, or occasionally struts, uneasily beside his, unable to project even the self-mocking wit of [[Matt Dillon]]'s star turn in ''[[Drugstore Cowboy]]''."<ref name="Hoberman">{{cite news |last=Hoberman |first=J. |author-link=J. Hoberman |title=''My Own Private Idaho'' |newspaper=[[The Village Voice]] |date=October 1, 1991}}</ref> [[Hal Hinson]] of ''[[The Washington Post]]'' wrote, "Gus Van Sant's sensibility is wholly original, wholly fresh. ''My Own Private Idaho'' adds a new ingredient: a kind of boho sweetness. I loved it."<ref>{{cite news |last=Hinson |first=Hal |author-link=Hal Hinson |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/longterm/movies/videos/myownprivateidahorhinson_a0a711.htm |title='My Own Private Idaho' (R) |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |date=October 18, 1991 |access-date=April 3, 2024}}</ref>

Conversely, [[Mike Clark (critic)|Mike Clark]] of ''[[USA Today]]'' gave ''My Own Private Idaho'' two and half stars out of four, calling it "nothing but set pieces; tossed into a mix whose meaning is almost certainly private".<ref name="Clark">{{cite news |last=Clark |first=Mike |author-link=Mike Clark (critic) |title=Half-baked plot mires ''Idaho'' studs |newspaper=[[USA Today]] |date=September 27, 1991}}</ref>
''[[Time (magazine)|Time]]'' magazine's [[Richard Schickel]] wrote, "What plot it has is borrowed, improbably, from ''Henry IV'', and whenever anyone manages to speak an entire paragraph, it is usually a Shakespearean paraphrase. But this is a desperate imposition on an essentially inert film."<ref name="Schickel">{{cite magazine|last=Schickel |first=Richard |author-link=Richard Schickel |title=Cinema |magazine=[[Time (magazine)|Time]] |date=October 28, 1991 |access-date=2009-02-19 |url=http://www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,974132,00.html |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080408193800/http://www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,974132,00.html |archive-date=April 8, 2008}}</ref>
In his review for ''[[The New Yorker]]'', [[Terrence Rafferty]] wrote, "Van Sant has stranded the actor in a movie full of flat characters and bad ideas, but Phoenix walks through the picture, down the road after road after road, as if he were surrounded by glorious phantoms."<ref name="Rafferty">{{cite magazine|last=Rafferty |first=Terrence |author-link=Terrence Rafferty |title=Street Theatre |magazine=[[The New Yorker]] |date=October 7, 1991}}</ref>

On the [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film holds an approval rating of 79% based on 63 reviews. The website's critics consensus reads, "A tantalizing glimpse of a talented director and his stars all at the top of their respective games, Gus Van Sant's loose reworking of Henry IV is smart, sad and audacious."<ref name="Rotten Tomatoes">{{cite web |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/my_own_private_idaho |title=My Own Private Idaho |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |access-date=March 18, 2026}}</ref> [[Metacritic]], which uses a [[Weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average]], assigned the film a score of 77 out of 100, based on 18 critics, indicating "generally favorable" reviews.<ref name="Metacritic">{{cite web |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/my-own-private-idaho/ |title=My Own Private Idaho |website=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=April 27, 2013}}</ref>

=== Home media ===
The film was originally released on [[VHS]] and [[LaserDisc]] in 1992.

{{Anchor|DVD|Video}}
In 2005, the film was remastered by [[The Criterion Collection]] and released on a two-disc [[DVD]] set.<ref name="CCEssay">{{cite web| url= http://www.criterion.com/current/posts/596-my-own-private-idaho-private-places |title= My Own Private Idaho: Private Places| first= Amy|last= Taubin |author-link=Amy Taubin|publisher= [[The Criterion Collection]]| access-date= 2015-08-10}}</ref> The second disc features new interviews, outtakes and more information about the movie. This DVD set is accompanied by an illustrated 64-page-booklet featuring previously published articles, interviews with cast and crew, new essays by [[JT LeRoy]] and [[Amy Taubin]], a 1991 article by [[Lance Loud]], and reprinted interviews with Gus Van Sant, River Phoenix and Keanu Reeves.<ref name="Ross">{{cite magazine|last=Ross|first=Dalton|title=''My Own Private Idaho''|magazine=[[Entertainment Weekly]]|date=February 28, 2005|url=http://www.ew.com/ew/article/0,,1031366,00.html|access-date=2009-07-16|archive-date=2009-04-21|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090421031355/http://www.ew.com/ew/article/0,,1031366,00.html|url-status=dead}}</ref> ''Entertainment Weekly'' gave the DVD a "B+" rating and wrote, "While you may enjoy watching ''My Own Private Idaho'', whether you choose to view this two-disc Criterion edition in its entirety depends on how much you enjoy watching people talking about ''My Own Private Idaho''", and concluded, "But with all the various interpretations and influences, this is definitely a film worth talking about".<ref name="Ross" />

In 2015, [[The Criterion Collection]] released the film on Blu-ray, based on a restored 4K digital transfer.<ref>{{cite web|title=My Own Private Idaho (1991)|publisher=[[The Criterion Collection]]|access-date=August 10, 2015|url=https://www.criterion.com/films/249-my-own-private-idaho%7Ctitle=}}</ref>

== Accolades ==
{{Anchor|Awards|Accolades}}''My Own Private Idaho'' received the Showtime International Critics Award at the 1991 Toronto Film Festival.<ref name="Scott">{{cite news|last=Scott|first=Jay|title=Egoyan wins $25,000 prize – and gives it away|newspaper=[[The Globe and Mail]]|date=September 16, 1991}}</ref> [[River Phoenix]] received the [[Volpi Cup for Best Actor]] at the [[1991 Venice Film Festival]].<ref name="Gazette">{{cite news|title=Russian movie tops in Venice|newspaper=[[The Gazette (Montreal)|Montreal Gazette]]|date=September 16, 1991}}</ref> In response, he said, "I don't want more awards. [[Venice Film Festival|Venice]] is the most progressive festival. Anything else would be a token".<ref name="Green">{{cite news|last=Green|first=Tom|title=Phoenix's stock rising|newspaper=[[USA Today]]|date=October 18, 1991}}</ref> Phoenix was the Best Actor runner-up in the [[1991 New York Film Critics Circle Awards|1991 New York Film Critics Circle]] awards and received the [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Male Lead]] and [[National Society of Film Critics Award for Best Actor]], becoming the second-youngest winner of the former.<ref>{{cite web|last=Sharf|first=Zack|date=2018-03-04|title=Timothée Chalamet Wins Indie Spirit Award for Best Actor, Becomes Third Youngest Winner in History|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2018/03/timothee-chalamet-wins-indie-spirit-award-best-actor-1201934496/|access-date=2021-06-03|website=[[IndieWire]]}}</ref><ref name="Corliss">{{cite magazine|last=Corliss|first =Richard|title=His Own Private Agony|magazine=[[Time (magazine)|Time]]|date=November 15, 1993|url=http://www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,979576,00.html|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090328214150/http://www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,979576,00.html|archive-date=March 28, 2009|access-date=February 19, 2009}}</ref> The film's six nominations at the [[7th Independent Spirit Awards]] tied with ''[[Hangin' with the Homeboys]]'' for the most nominations at that ceremony. Winning three, it tied with ''[[Rambling Rose (film)|Rambling Rose]]'' for the most awards.<ref name="ISA"/>
