'''''A Fistful of Dollars''''' ({{langx|it|Per un pugno di dollari}}, "For a Fistful of Dollars") is a 1964 [[spaghetti Western]] film directed by [[Sergio Leone]] and starring [[Clint Eastwood]] in his first leading role, alongside [[Gian Maria Volonté]], [[Marianne Koch]], [[Wolfgang Lukschy]], [[Sieghardt Rupp]], [[José Calvo]], [[Antonio Prieto (Spanish actor)|Antonio Prieto]] and [[Joseph Egger]].<ref>''[[Variety Film Reviews|Variety]]'' film review; 18 November 1964, page 22.</ref> The film, an international [[Co-production (media)|co-production]] between Italy, [[West Germany]] and [[Francoist Spain|Spain]], was filmed on a low budget (reported to be US$200,000), and Eastwood was paid $15,000 for his role.<ref>"Ennio Morricone" by Jerry McCulley, essay in the 1995 CD "The Ennio Morricone Anthology", Rhino DRC2-1237</ref>

Released in Italy in 1964 and in the United States in 1967, the film initiated the popularity of the spaghetti Western genre. It is considered a landmark in cinema and [[List of films voted the best|one of the greatest and most influential films of all time]]. It was followed by ''[[For a Few Dollars More]]'' and ''[[The Good, the Bad and the Ugly]]'', both also starring Eastwood. Collectively, these three films became known as the ''[[Dollars Trilogy]]'', or the ''Man with No Name Trilogy'', after the [[United Artists]] publicity campaign referred to Eastwood's characters in all three films as the "[[Man with No Name]]". All three films were released in sequence in the United States in 1967, making Eastwood a national celebrity.<ref>{{cite book |last=McGilligan |first=Patrick |title=Clint: The Life and Legend (updated and revised) |publisher=[[OR Books]] |year=2015 |isbn=978-1-939293-96-1 |location=New York}}</ref>

The film has been identified as an unofficial remake of the [[Akira Kurosawa]] film ''[[Yojimbo]]'' (1961), which resulted in a successful lawsuit by [[Toho]], ''Yojimbo''{{'}}s production company.<ref>{{cite web|first=Dalton|last=Norman|url=https://screenrant.com/every-movie-based-on-akira-kurosawas-yojimbo/|title=Every Movie Based On Akira Kurosawa's Yojimbo|website=[[Screen Rant]]|date=February 26, 2023|accessdate=August 29, 2023|archive-date=29 August 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230829112721/https://screenrant.com/every-movie-based-on-akira-kurosawas-yojimbo/|url-status=live}}</ref> Kurosawa wrote to Leone directly, saying, "Signor Leone, I have just had the chance to see your film. It is a very fine film, but it is my film. Since Japan is a signatory of the Berne Convention on the international copyright, you must pay me." He and Toho received 15 percent of the film's revenue. Kurosawa earned more money from this settlement than from ''Yojimbo.''<ref>{{cite web | url=https://akirakurosawa.info/2013/07/01/film-club-yojimbo-1961/ | title=Film Club: Yojimbo (1961) }}</ref>

Few spaghetti Westerns had been released in the United States at the time, so many of the European cast and crew adopted American-sounding stage names. These included Leone ("Bob Robertson"), Gian Maria Volonté ("Johnny Wels") and the composer, [[Ennio Morricone]] ("Dan Savio"). ''A Fistful of Dollars'' was shot in [[Spain]], mostly near [[Hoyo de Manzanares]]<ref>{{cite web |url=https://historias-matritenses.blogspot.com/2008/09/los-primeros-decorados-del-oeste-en.html |title=Los primeros decorados del Oeste en España, en Hoyo de Manzanares |last=Caldito |first=Angel |publisher=Historias Matritenses |date=3 September 2008 |access-date=7 January 2014 |language=es |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140108065107/http://historias-matritenses.blogspot.com/2008/09/los-primeros-decorados-del-oeste-en.html |archive-date=8 January 2014 |url-status=live}}</ref> close to [[Madrid]], but also (like its two sequels) in the [[Tabernas Desert]] and in the [[Cabo de Gata-Níjar Natural Park]], both in the [[Province of Almería]].

==Plot==
An unnamed stranger{{#tag:ref|The character, notably publicized as "the [[Man with No Name]]", is listed in the credits as "Joe" and is so called, in multiple instances, by the character Piripero; nevertheless, that may not be his real name.|group=N}} arrives at the little frontier town of San Miguel. Silvanito, the town's innkeeper, tells the Stranger about a feud between two [[Smuggling|smuggler]] families vying to gain control of the town: the Rojo brothers 
— Don Miguel, Esteban and Ramón — and the family of the town sheriff, John Baxter; his matriarchal wife, Consuelo; and their son, Antonio. To make money, the Stranger decides to play these families against each other. He demonstrates his deadliness to both sides by shooting to death with ease the four men who insulted him as he entered town.

The Stranger witnesses the Rojos massacre a detachment of Mexican soldiers escorting a chest of gold (to exchange for rifles) and comes up with a plan. He takes two of the dead bodies to a nearby cemetery and props them up to make them appear alive. He sells information to both factions, claiming two Mexican soldiers survived the attack. Each faction races to the cemetery, the Baxters to get the supposed survivors to testify against the Rojos, and the Rojos to silence them. They engage in a gunfight, with Ramón "killing" the supposed survivors and Esteban capturing Antonio Baxter.

While the Rojos and the Baxters are fighting, the Stranger searches the Rojo hacienda for the gold, and accidentally knocks out a woman, Marisol, whom Ramón is in love with and is holding captive. He takes her to the Baxters, who arrange to return her to the Rojos in exchange for Antonio. During the hostage exchange, Marisol's son, Jesus, and husband, Julio, approach her. Ramón orders one of his men, Rubio, to kill Julio, but he is deterred by Silvanito and the Stranger. The Stranger orders Marisol to go back to Ramón, and Julio to take their son home. He learns from Silvanito that Ramón accused Julio of cheating during a card game and took Marisol as collateral.

That night, while the Rojos are celebrating, the Stranger kills the guards, frees Marisol, and wrecks the house where she was held to create the appearance of an attack by the Baxters. He gives money to Marisol and urges her family to leave town.

The Rojos eventually discover that it was the Stranger who freed Marisol. They capture and torture him, but he escapes. Believing that he is protected by the Baxters, the Rojos set fire to the Baxter home, massacring everyone, including Baxter's wife, as they flee the burning building. With help from the local coffinmaker named Piripero, the Stranger escapes town by hiding in a coffin, and recuperates in an abandoned mine.

When Piripero tells him that Silvanito has been captured and tortured by the Rojos for the Stranger's whereabouts, he returns to town to confront them. With a steel chest plate hidden beneath his [[poncho]], he taunts Ramón to "aim for the heart" as Ramón's shots deflect until Ramón exhausts his [[Winchester Model 1892|Winchester rifle]]'s ammunition. The Stranger shoots the weapon from Ramón's hand and kills Don Miguel, Rubio and the other Rojo men standing nearby. He uses the last shot to free Silvanito, who is hanging from a rope by his hands.

After challenging Ramón to reload his rifle faster than he can reload his revolver, the Stranger shoots and kills him. Esteban aims for the Stranger's back from a nearby building, but is shot dead by Silvanito. The Stranger bids Silvanito and Piripero farewell, and rides away from town.

==Development==
''A Fistful of Dollars'' was originally called ''Il Magnifico Straniero'' ("The Magnificent Stranger"), before the title was changed to ''A Fistful of Dollars''.<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.latimes.com/entertainment/movies/la-et-mn-spaghetti-western-20140629-story.html |title=A 'Fistful' of cinematic history |first=Joe |last=Ciardiello |date=June 28, 2014 |work=[[Los Angeles Times]] |access-date=28 October 2020 |archive-date=2 November 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201102053004/https://www.latimes.com/entertainment/movies/la-et-mn-spaghetti-western-20140629-story.html |url-status=live }}</ref> The production and development of ''A Fistful of Dollars'', from anecdotes, was described by Italian film historian, Roberto Curti, as both contradictory and difficult to decipher.<ref name="Curti25">{{cite book |title=Tonino Valerii: The Films |year=2016 |publisher=[[McFarland & Company|McFarland]] |last=Curti |first=Roberto |page=25 |isbn=978-1476626185}}</ref> [[Tonino Valerii]] alternatively said that Barboni and [[Stelvio Massi]] met Leone outside a theater in Rome where they had seen ''Yojimbo'', suggesting to Leone that it would make a good Western.<ref name="Curti25" /> The actor [[Mimmo Palmara]], a friend of Leone, told a similar story to Valerii, saying that Barboni had told him about ''Yojimbo'', and he would see it the next day with Leone and his wife, Carla.<ref name="Curti25" /><ref name="Curti26">{{cite book |title=Tonino Valerii: The Films |year=2016 |publisher=[[McFarland & Company|McFarland]] |last=Curti |first=Roberto |page=26 |isbn=978-1476626185}}</ref>

[[Adriano Bolzoni]] stated in 1978 that he had the idea of making ''Yojimbo'' into a Western, and brought the idea to Franco Palaggi, who sent Bolzoni with [[Duccio Tessari]] to watch the film and take notes on it.<ref name="Curti26" /> Bolzoni said that both he and Tessari wrote a first draft, which moved on to Leone, noting that Tessari wrote the majority of the script.<ref name="Curti26" />

[[Fernando Di Leo]] also claimed authorship of the script, saying that both ''A Fistful of Dollars'' and ''[[For a Few Dollars More]]'' were written by him and Tessari, and not [[Luciano Vincenzoni]].<ref name="Curti26" /> Di Leo claimed that after Leone had the idea for the film, Tessari wrote the script and he gave him a hand.<ref name="Curti26" /> Di Leo would repeat this story in an interview, saying that he was at the first meetings of Tessari and Leone discussing what kind of film to make from ''Yojimbo''.<ref name="Curti26" /> Di Leo noted that Leone did not like the first draft of the script, which led to him to drastically rewrite it with Tessari.<ref name="Curti26" /> Production papers for the film credit Spanish and German writers, but these were added to play into coproduction standards during this period of filmmaking, to get more financing from the Spanish and West German companies.<ref name="Curti26" /> Leone would suggest that he wrote the entire screenplay, based on Tessari's treatment.<ref name="Curti26" />

Originally, Sergio Leone intended [[Henry Fonda]] to play the "[[Man with No Name]]".<ref>Frayling 2006, p. 141.</ref> However, the production company could not afford to employ a major [[Cinema of the United States|Hollywood]] star. Next, Leone offered the part to [[Charles Bronson]]. He, too, declined, saying that the script was bad. Both Fonda and Bronson would eventually star in Leone's ''[[Once Upon a Time in the West]]''. Other actors who turned down the role were [[Henry Silva]], [[Rory Calhoun]], [[Tony Russel]],<ref>Barnum, Michael ''The Wild Wild Interview: Tony Russell Our Man On Gamma 1'' Video Watchdog No. 128 Dec/Feb 2007</ref> [[Steve Reeves]], [[Ty Hardin]] and [[James Coburn]].<ref>Hughes, p.3</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.drkrm.com/reeves2.html |title=Steve Reeves Interview Pt 2 |website=drkrm |access-date=7 January 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150221103811/http://www.drkrm.com/reeves2.html |archive-date=21 February 2015 |url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.tahoedailytribune.com/article/20040618/News/106180001 |title=Relive the thrilling days of the Old West in film |last=Carrerowbonanza |first=Jack |work=Tahoe Daily Tribune |date=11 June 2004 |access-date=7 January 2014 |url-status = dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140107103304/http://www.tahoedailytribune.com/article/20040618/News/106180001 |archive-date=7 January 2014}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.sensesofcinema.com/2003/cteq/fistful_of_dollars/ |title=A Fistful of Dollars |last=Williams |first=Tony |website=[[Senses of Cinema]] |date=October 2003 |access-date=7 January 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140107102151/http://sensesofcinema.com/2003/cteq/fistful_of_dollars/ |archive-date=7 January 2014 |url-status=live}}</ref> Leone said he wanted Coburn for the role, but he was deemed too expensive.<ref>{{cite web |last=Agustin |first=Francis |date=9 September 2024 |title='To me Clint closely resembled a cat': Sergio Leone on the role that made Eastwood a movie star |url=https://www.bbc.com/culture/article/20240906-the-role-that-made-clint-eastwood-a-star |website=BBC}}</ref> Leone turned his attention to [[Richard Harrison (actor)|Richard Harrison]], an expatriate American actor who had recently starred in the Italian Western, ''[[Gunfight at Red Sands]]''. Harrison, however, had not been impressed with his experience in that film, and refused. The producers presented a list of available, lesser-known American actors and asked Harrison for advice. Harrison suggested Eastwood, who he knew could play a cowboy convincingly.<ref>{{cite web |date=18 September 2012 |title=Richard Harrison sur Nanarland TV |trans-title=Richard Harrison on Nanarland TV |url=http://www.nanarland.com/nanarland-tv-2-richard-harrison.html |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140107101718/http://www.nanarland.com/nanarland-tv-2-richard-harrison.html |archive-date=7 January 2014 |access-date=7 January 2014 |publisher=Nanarland TV |language=fr}}</ref> Harrison would state, "Maybe my greatest contribution to cinema was not doing ''A Fistful of Dollars'' and recommending Clint for the part."<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.nanarland.com/interviews/entretiens/en/richard-harrison.html |title=Entretien avec Richard Harrison (English version) |publisher=Nanarland |access-date=2 November 2020 |language=en }}</ref> Eastwood said "In ''[[Rawhide (TV series)|Rawhide]],'' I did get awfully tired of playing the conventional white hat... the hero who kisses old ladies and dogs and was kind to everybody. I decided it was time to be an [[antihero]]."<ref name="Hughes4">Hughes, p.4</ref>

Eastwood said that he had a similar idea for adapting ''Yojimbo'' into a Western a few years earlier in [[Los Angeles]], when a friend who was a fan of [[samurai cinema]] took him to watch ''Yojimbo'' at a [[Western Avenue (Los Angeles)|Western Avenue]] theater that ran [[Cinema of Japan|Japanese films]]. Eastwood recalled that he "remembered sitting there" and saying, "Boy, this would be a great western if only someone had nerve enough to do it, but they'd never have enough nerve." A few years later, after someone handed him the script for ''A Fistful of Dollars'', "about five or 10 pages in" he "recognized it as an obvious rip-off" of ''Yojimbo'', which he found ironic.<ref>{{cite news |last1=Turan |first1=Kenneth |title=Eastwood says 'Unforgiven' could be his last western |url=https://www.newspapers.com/image/427330404/ |access-date=21 April 2022 |work=[[The Paducah Sun]] |via=[[Newspapers.com]] |date=3 August 1992 |page=58 |url-access=subscription |archive-date=21 April 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220421054550/https://www.newspapers.com/image/427330404/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

''A Fistful of Dollars'' was an Italian, German and Spanish co-production, so there was a significant language barrier on set. Leone did not speak English and Eastwood communicated with the Italian cast and crew mostly through the actor and stuntman [[Benito Stefanelli]], who also acted as an uncredited interpreter for the production, and later appeared in Leone's other pictures.<ref>{{cite web |title=More Than A Fistful of Interview: Christopher Frayling on Sergio Leone |url=http://www.fistful-of-leone.com/articles/prof.html |website=Fistful of Leone |access-date=20 February 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090210060328/http://www.fistful-of-leone.com/articles/prof.html|archive-date=10 February 2009|url-status=live}}</ref> Similar to other Italian films shot at the time, all footage was filmed silent, and the dialogue and sound effects were dubbed in post-production.<ref name="Munn48">Munn, p. 48</ref> For the Italian version of the film, Eastwood was dubbed by the actor [[Enrico Maria Salerno]], whose sinister rendition of the Man with No Name's voice contrasted with Eastwood's cocksure and darkly humorous interpretation.<ref name="Commentary">[[Christopher Frayling]], For a Few Dollars More audio commentary. Retrieved 25 January 2016.</ref>

==Visual style==
''A Fistful of Dollars'' became the first film to exhibit Leone's distinctive style of visual direction. This was influenced by both [[John Ford]]'s cinematic landscaping and the Japanese method of direction honed by [[Akira Kurosawa]]. Leone wanted an operatic feel to his Western, so there are many examples of extreme closeups of the faces of different characters, functioning like arias in a traditional opera. The rhythm, emotion and communication within scenes can be attributed to Leone's meticulous framing of his closeups.<ref>Frayling 2006. p. 100</ref> Leone's closeups are akin to portraits, often lit with [[Renaissance]]-type lighting effects, and are considered by some as pieces of design in their own right.<ref>[[Sir Christopher Frayling]], A Fistful of Dollars audio commentary (Blu-ray version). Retrieved on 15 September 2014.</ref>

Eastwood was instrumental in creating the Man with No Name's distinctive visual style. He bought black jeans from a sports shop on [[Hollywood Boulevard]]; the hat came from a [[Santa Monica]] wardrobe firm; and the trademark cigars from a [[Beverly Hills]] store.<ref>Munn, p. 46</ref> He also brought props from ''Rawhide'', including a Cobra-handled [[Colt's Manufacturing Company|Colt]], a gunbelt and spurs.<ref name="Hughes5">Hughes, p.5</ref> The poncho was acquired in Spain.<ref>Munn, p. 47</ref> Leone and the costume designer [[Carlo Simi]] decided on the Spanish poncho for the Man with No Name.<ref name="Hughes5"/> On the [[DVD]] commentary for ''[[The Good, the Bad and the Ugly]]'', it is said that although Eastwood is a non-smoker, he felt that the foul taste of the cigar in his mouth put him in the right frame of mind for his character. Leone reportedly took quickly to Eastwood's distinctive style, and commented, "More than an actor, I needed a mask, and Eastwood, at that time, only had two expressions: with hat and no hat."<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.cinemadelsilenzio.it/index.php?mod=interview&id=17 |title=Intervista: Sergio Leone |last=Mininni |first=Francesco |website=Cinema Del Silenzio |access-date=7 January 2014 |language=it |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131106041034/http://www.cinemadelsilenzio.it/index.php?mod=interview&id=17 |archive-date=6 November 2013 |url-status=live}}</ref>

==Soundtrack==
The film's music was written by [[Ennio Morricone]], initially credited as Dan Savio.

Leone requested Morricone to write a theme that would be similar to [[Dimitri Tiomkin]]'s ''[[El Degüello]]'' (used in ''[[Rio Bravo (film)|Rio Bravo]]''). Although the two themes are similar, Morricone stated that he used a [[lullaby]] that he had composed previously, and developed the theme from that. He added that what makes the two themes similar is the execution, not the arrangement.<ref>{{cite AV media |year=1989 |title=Viva Leone! |medium=Documentary |publisher=BBC}}</ref>

In 1962 the expatriate American folk singer [[Peter Tevis]] recorded a version of [[Woody Guthrie]]'s "[[Pastures of Plenty]]" that was arranged by Morricone. During a conference with Morricone discussing the music in the film, a recording of Tevis's "Pastures of Plenty" was played. Leone said, "That's it",<ref>Cumbow, Robert C. ''The Films of Sergio Leone''. Scarecrow Press, 15 February 2008.</ref> with Tevis claiming that the tune and musical arrangements were copied for the music for the opening title, "Titoli".

"Some of the music was written before the film, which is unusual," said Morricone. "Leone's films were made like that because he wanted the music to be an important part of it, and he often kept the scenes longer simply because he didn't want the music to end. That's why the films are so slow — because of the music."<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.theguardian.com/music/2007/mar/18/features.musicmonthly10 |title=Q&A - Ennio Morricone |work=[[The Observer]] |publisher=Guardian News and Media |date=18 March 2007 |access-date=13 April 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181106183033/https://www.theguardian.com/music/2007/mar/18/features.musicmonthly10 |archive-date=6 November 2018 |url-status=live}}</ref>

Although not used in the completed film, Peter Tevis recorded lyrics to Morricone's main theme for the film. As a [[movie tie-in]] to the American release, [[United Artists Records]] released a different set of lyrics to Morricone's theme, called "Restless One", and recorded by [[Little Anthony and the Imperials]].

==Release and reception==
===Box office===
Promoting ''A Fistful of Dollars'' was difficult, because no major distributor wanted to take a chance on a faux-Western and an unknown director. The film ended up being released in Italy 12 September 1964,<ref name="Curti192">{{cite book |title=Tonino Valerii: The Films |year=2016 |publisher=[[McFarland & Company|McFarland]] |last=Curti |first=Roberto |page=192 |isbn=978-1476626185}}</ref> which was typically the worst month for sales.

Despite the initial negative reviews from Italian critics, the film's popularity spread at a grassroots level, and, over the film's theatrical release, it grossed 2.7 billion [[Italian lira|lire]] ({{US$|4,375,000|long=no}}) in Italy, more than any other [[List of highest-grossing films in Italy|Italian film]] to that point.<ref name="Hughes7">Hughes, p.7</ref><ref name=itgross>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=October 11, 1967|page=33|title=Top Italian Film Grossers}}</ref> It sold admissions of 14,797,275 ticket sales in Italy.<ref name="ItAd">{{cite The release of the film was delayed in the United States, because distributors feared being sued by Kurosawa. As a result, it was not shown in American cinemas until 18 January 1967.<ref name="McGillagan144"/> The film grossed $4.5 million for the year.<ref name="Hughes7"/> In 1969, it was rereleased, earning $1.2 million in [[theatrical rental]]s.<ref>{{cite magazine |title=Big Rental Films of 1969 |magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] |date=7 January 1970}}</ref> It eventually grossed $14.5 million in the United States and Canada,<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.boxofficemojo.com/movies/?id=fistfulofdollars.htm |title=A Fistful of Dollars (1967) |website=[[Box Office Mojo]] |access-date=30 April 2013 |publisher=[[IMDb]] |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130511212702/http://boxofficemojo.com/movies/?id=fistfulofdollars.htm |archive-date=11 May 2013 |url-status=live}}</ref> totaling more than {{US$|{{#expr:4.4+14.5+1}} million|long=no}} grossed worldwide.

===Critical response===
The film was initially shunned by Italian critics, who gave it extremely negative reviews. Some American critics felt differently from their Italian counterparts, with ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' praising it as having "a [[James Bond]]ian vigor and tongue-in-cheek approach that was sure to capture both sophisticates and average cinema patrons".<ref name="McGillagan144">McGillagan (1999), p.144</ref>

On the film's American release in 1967, both Philip French and Bosley Crowther were unimpressed with the film itself. The critic [[Philip French]] of ''[[The Observer]]'' stated: <blockquote>The calculated sadism of the film would be offensive were it not for the neutralizing laughter aroused by the ludicrousness of the whole exercise. If one didn't know the actual provenance of the film, one would guess that it was a private movie made by a group of rich European Western fans at a dude ranch... ''A Fistful of Dollars'' looks awful, has a flat dead soundtrack, and is totally devoid of human feeling.<ref>{{cite news |last1=French |first1=Philip |title=Under Western disguise |work=[[The Observer]] |publisher=Guardian News and Media |date=11 June 1967 |access-date=7 January 2014 |url=https://theguardian.newspapers.com/image/258884102/?terms=Under%2BWestern%2Bdisguise|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171014183707/https://theguardian.newspapers.com/image/258884102/?terms=Under%2BWestern%2Bdisguise|archive-date=14 October 2017|url-status=live}}</ref></blockquote>

[[Bosley Crowther]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' treated the film not as pastiche, but as camp-parody, stating that nearly every Western cliché could be found in this "egregiously synthetic but engrossingly morbid, violent film". He went on to patronize Eastwood's performance, stating: "He is simply another fabrication of a personality, half cowboy and half gangster, going through the ritualistic postures and exercises of each... He is a morbid, amusing, campy fraud."<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1967/02/02/archives/screen-a-fistful-of-dollars-opens-western-film-cliches-all-used-in.html |title=Screen: 'A Fistful of Dollars' Opens; Western Film Cliches All Used in Movie Cowboy Star From TV Featured as Killer |last=Crowther |first=Bosley |work=[[The New York Times]] |date=2 February 1967 |access-date=19 January 2009 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120626103041/http://movies.nytimes.com/movie/review?res=9B03E1DD1439E53BBC4A53DFB466838C679EDE |archive-date=26 June 2012 |url-status = live}}</ref>

When the film was televised on the [[American Broadcasting Company|ABC]] network 23 February 1975,<ref name="tvg">{{cite web |url=https://tvguidegal.blogspot.com/2014/08/a-fistful-of-dollars-ad.html |title=A Fistful of Dollars Ad |work=The TV Guide Historian |last=Merrick |first=Amy |date=24 August 2014 |access-date=13 April 2018 |publisher=[[Blogspot]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180418054258/http://tvguidegal.blogspot.com/2014/08/a-fistful-of-dollars-ad.html|archive-date=18 April 2018|url-status=live}}</ref> a four-and-a-half-minute prologue was added to the film to contextualize the character and justify the violence. Written and directed by [[Monte Hellman]], it featured an unidentified U.S. official ([[Harry Dean Stanton]]) offering the imprisoned Man With No Name a chance at a pardon in exchange for cleaning up San Miguel. Closeups of Eastwood's face from archival footage are inserted into the scene alongside Stanton's performance.<ref>{{cite book |last=Stevens |first=Brad |title=Monte Hellman: His Life and Films |publisher=McFarland |year=2003 |isbn=9780786414345 |page=200}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.fistful-of-leone.com/classic/films/afod/prologue.html |title=A Fistful of Dollars Prologue |website=Fistful of Leone |date=5 July 1998 |access-date=3 June 2012 |author=Chasman | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131014093250/http://www.fistful-of-leone.com/classic/films/afod/prologue.html | archive-date=14 October 2013 | url-status=live}}</ref> This prologue opened television presentations for a few years before disappearing; it reappeared on the Special Edition DVD and the more recent [[Blu-ray]], along with an interview with Monte Hellman about its production.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://film.thedigitalfix.com/content/id/56749/a-fistful-of-dollars-special-edition.html |title=A Fistful Of Dollars (Special Edition) |last=Sutton |first=Mike |website=The Digital Fix |date=10 April 2005 |access-date=3 June 2012 |publisher=Poisonous Monkey |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130614021522/http://film.thedigitalfix.com/content/id/56749/a-fistful-of-dollars-special-edition.html |archive-date=14 June 2013 |url-status=dead }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.dvdtalk.com/reviews/50647/fistful-of-dollars-clint-eastwood-sergio-leone-a/ |title=A Fistful of Dollars (Blu-ray) |last=Rich |first=Jamie S. |website=DVD Talk |date=29 August 2011 |access-date=3 June 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121203214125/http://www.dvdtalk.com/reviews/50647/fistful-of-dollars-clint-eastwood-sergio-leone-a/ |archive-date=3 December 2012 |url-status=live}}</ref>

The retrospective reception of ''A Fistful of Dollars'' has been much more positive, noting it as a hugely influential film in regard to the rejuvenation of the Western genre. Film historian Howard Hughes, in his 2012 book, ''Once Upon a Time in the Italian West'', reflected by stating, "American and British critics largely chose to ignore Fistful's release, few recognizing its satirical humor or groundbreaking style, preferring to trash the shoddy production values...."<ref>{{cite book |last=Hughes |first=Howard |title=Once Upon A Time in the Italian West: The Filmgoers' Guide to Spaghetti Westerns |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=M6D3AgAAQBAJ&pg=PT7 |year=2006 |publisher=I.B. Tauris & Co |isbn=978-0-85773-045-9 |page=7}}</ref>

''A Fistful of Dollars'' has achieved a 98% approval rating from 55 critical reviews on [[Rotten Tomatoes]], with an average rating of 8.3/10. The website's critical consensus reads: "With Akira Kurosawa's ''Yojimbo'' as his template, Sergio Leone's ''A Fistful of Dollars'' helped define a new era for the Western and usher in its most iconic star, Clint Eastwood." It has also placed at number 8 on the site's "Top 100 Westerns".<ref name="Rotten Tomatoes">{{cite web |title=A Fistful of Dollars (Per un Pugno di Dollari) (1964) |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/fistful_of_dollars/ |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |publisher=[[Fandango Media]] |access-date=20 February 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150223092901/http://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/fistful_of_dollars/|archive-date=23 February 2015|url-status=live}}</ref> [[Metacritic]], which uses a [[Weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average]], assigned the {{#switch:  | episode = episode | series = series | season = season | film }} a score of 65 out of 100, based on 7 critics, indicating "generally favorable" reviews.<ref name="Metacritic">{{cite web |title=''A Fistful of Dollars'' |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/a-fistful-of-dollars |website=[[Metacritic]] |publisher=[[Fandom (website)#Fandom, Inc.|Fandom, Inc.]] |access-date=April 20, 2025 |archive-url= |archive-date= |url-status=}}</ref><ref>{{Cite Metacritic |title=A Fistful of Dollars |id=a-fistful-of-dollars |type=movie |access-date=April 20, 2025}}</ref>

The [[67th Cannes Film Festival]], held in 2014, celebrated the "50th anniversary of the birth of the spaghetti Western... by showing ''A Fistful of Dollars''".<ref name="Cannes Film Festival">{{cite web |title=A Fistful of Dollars to be shown at Cannes Film Festival |url=https://www.telegraph.co.uk/culture/film/cannes-film-festival/10829541/A-Fistful-of-Dollars-to-be-shown-at-Cannes-Film-Festival.html |work=[[The Daily Telegraph]] |access-date=20 February 2015 |last=Chilton |first=Martin|url-status = dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150220170515/https://www.telegraph.co.uk/culture/film/cannes-film-festival/10829541/A-Fistful-of-Dollars-to-be-shown-at-Cannes-Film-Festival.html |archive-date=20 February 2015}}</ref>

==Legal dispute==
The film was effectively an unofficial and unlicensed remake of Akira Kurosawa's 1961 film ''[[Yojimbo (film)|Yojimbo]]'' (written by Kurosawa and Ryūzō Kikushima); Kurosawa insisted that Leone had made "a fine movie, but it was ''my'' movie".<ref>{{cite book |last=Galbraith IV |first=Stuart |title=The Emperor and the Wolf |publisher=Faber and Faber |location=New York |year=2001 |url=http://www.movingimage.us/film_programs/program_notes/y/yojimbo.html |access-date=29 February 2008 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070928081727/http://www.movingimage.us/film_programs/program_notes/y/yojimbo.html |archive-date=28 September 2007}}</ref> This led to a lawsuit from [[Toho]], ''Yojimbo''{{'}}s production company. Leone ignored the resulting lawsuit, but eventually settled out of court, reportedly for 15% of the worldwide receipts of ''A Fistful of Dollars'' and over $100,000.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.spaghetti-western.net/index.php/FISTFUL_-_The_Whole_Story,_part_2 |title=FISTFUL - The Whole Story, part 2 - The Spaghetti Western Database |website=Spaghetti Western Database |last=Gelten |first=Simon |access-date=3 June 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120503032804/http://www.spaghetti-western.net/index.php/FISTFUL_-_The_Whole_Story,_part_2 |archive-date=3 May 2012 |url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://sidebmag.com/2011/04/14/1535/ |archive-url=https://archive.today/20130203101941/http://sidebmag.com/2011/04/14/1535/ |url-status = usurped|archive-date=3 February 2013 |title=A Fistful of Dollars and Yojimbo |work=Side B Magazine |date=14 April 2011 |access-date=3 June 2012}}</ref>

The critic [[Christopher Frayling]] identifies three principal sources for ''A Fistful of Dollars'': "Partly derived from Kurosawa's samurai film ''Yojimbo'', partly from [[Dashiell Hammett]]'s novel ''[[Red Harvest]]'' (1929), but most of all from [[Carlo Goldoni]]'s eighteenth-century play ''[[Servant of Two Masters]]'' [''[[sic]]'']."<ref>''The BFI Companion to the Western'', 1988.</ref> Leone cited those alternate sources in his defense. He claimed a thematic debt, for both ''Fistful'' and ''Yojimbo'', to Carlo Goldoni's ''Servant of Two Masters —'' the basic premise of the protagonist playing two camps against each other. Leone asserted that this rooted the origination of ''Fistful''/''Yojimbo'' in European, and specifically, Italian, culture. The plot of ''The'' ''Servant of Two Masters'' can also be seen in Hammett's detective novel, ''Red Harvest.'' The [[Continental Op]] hero of the novel is, significantly, a ''man without a name.'' Leone believed that ''Red Harvest'' had influenced ''Yojimbo'': "Kurosawa's ''Yojimbo'' was inspired by an American novel of the series-noire so I was really taking the story back home again."<ref>Frayling 2006, p. 151.</ref>

Leone also referenced numerous American Westerns in the film, most notably ''[[Shane (film)|Shane]]''<ref>{{cite book |last=Fridlund |first=Bert |chapter=Classical American Western and Spaghetti Western: A Comparison of Shane and A Fistful of Dollars |title=2003 Film & History CD-ROM Annual |location=Cleveland |publisher=Film & History |date=2005}}</ref> and ''[[My Darling Clementine]]'', both of which differ from ''Yojimbo''.

==Digital restoration==
In 2014, the film was [[Film preservation#Digital Film Preservation|digitally restored]] by [[Cineteca di Bologna]] and Unidis Jolly Film for its Blu-ray debut and 50th anniversary. Frame-by-frame digital restoration by [[Prasad Corporation]] removed dirt, tears, scratches and other defects.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.prasadgroup.org/services/digital-film-restoration/ |publisher=[[Prasad Group]] |title=Services: Digital Film Restoration |access-date=13 April 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180421171115/http://www.prasadgroup.org/services/digital-film-restoration/ |archive-date=21 April 2018 |url-status=usurped}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/cannes-quentin-tarantino-host-screening-703584 |work=[[The Hollywood Reporter]] |title=Cannes: Quentin Tarantino to Host Screening of 'A Fistful of Dollars' |date=13 May 2014 |first=Stuart |last=Kemp |access-date=16 September 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141217082842/http://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/cannes-quentin-tarantino-host-screening-703584 |archive-date=17 December 2014 |url-status=live}}</ref> The directorial credit for Leone, which replaced the "Bob Robertson" card years ago, has been retained, but otherwise, the original credits (with pseudonyms, including "Dan Savio" for Morricone) remain the same.
