'''''Desperate Living''''' is a 1977 American [[black comedy]] film directed, produced, and written by [[John Waters]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.allrovi.com/movies/movie/desperate-living-v13410|website=[[AllMovie]]|title=Desperate Living (1977)|last=Firsching|first=Robert|access-date=September 27, 2012|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110809075209/http://www.allrovi.com/movies/movie/desperate-living-v13410|archive-date=August 9, 2011}}</ref> The film stars [[Liz Renay]], [[Mink Stole]], [[Susan Lowe]], [[Edith Massey (actress)|Edith Massey]], [[Mary Vivian Pearce]], and [[Jean Hill (actress)|Jean Hill]].

It is the third installment of what Waters has labeled the "Trash Trilogy", which also includes ''[[Pink Flamingos]]'' (1972) and ''[[Female Trouble]]'' (1974), and the only one without [[Divine (performer)|Divine]].{{sfn|Levy|2015|p=[https://books.google.com/books?id=uwnSBgAAQBAJ&pg=PA282 282]}} The film generated a [[cult following]] around Renay, who appeared in at least two dozen other films.<ref>{{cite magazine|url=https://variety.com/2007/film/news/renay-was-a-cult-film-favorite-1117958264/|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|title=Renay was a cult film favorite|access-date=July 4, 2020|date=January 29, 2007}}</ref>

Following the murder of her husband, a suburban housewife and her maid agree to be exiled to Mortville, a [[shanty town|shantytown]] ruled by a tyrannical [[queen regnant]].

==Plot==
Neurotic [[Guilford, Baltimore|Guilford]] housewife Peggy Gravel and her housekeeper Grizelda Brown go on the lam after killing Peggy's husband. After being detained by a [[cross-dressing]] police officer, they are given an ultimatum: arrest for murder or exile to Mortville, a [[shantytown]] of criminals and social outcasts ruled by the [[fascist]] Queen Carlotta.

Peggy and Grizelda choose Mortville and settle at the boarding house of lesbian wrestler Mole McHenry and her lover Muffy St. Jacques, the "most beautiful woman in Mortville". Mole wins the [[Maryland Lottery]] with a ticket confiscated from Peggy and uses the money for [[gender reassignment surgery]] at [[Johns Hopkins Hospital]], but when Muffy is repulsed by Mole's [[phalloplasty]], he gives himself a [[penectomy]].

Carlotta's rebellious daughter Princess Coo-Coo attempts to elope with a [[Nudism|nudist]] garbage collector, but he is killed by Carlotta's royal guards. Coo-Coo hides in Peggy and Grizelda's home, but Peggy alerts the guards to her location, and Grizelda is killed in the ensuing fight after their house collapses on her. Carlotta, having grown weary of her subjects, enlists Peggy in a plot to genocide the residents of Mortville by infecting them with [[rabies]]. An armed group led by Mole storms the castle, where they overthrow Carlotta and install Muffy as the new queen. The townsfolk celebrate their new freedom by roasting Carlotta on a spit and serving her, [[pig roast|pig-like]], on a platter.

==Production==
[[File:John Waters by David Shankbone.jpg|thumb|[[John Waters (filmmaker)|John Waters]]]]
[[Art director]] [[Vincent Peranio]] built the exterior sets for Mortville on a 26-acre farm in [[Hampstead, Maryland]], owned by Waters' friend, [[Peter Koper]].<ref>{{cite book|last=Griffin|first=Chloe|title=Edgewise: A Picture of Cookie Mueller|title-link=Edgewise (book)|year=2014|publisher=b_books|isbn=978-3-942214-20-9|page=98}}</ref> The exterior sets were largely constructed of [[plywood]] and rubbish Peranio and Waters had collected from around Baltimore.{{sfn|Waters|1981|p=167}} Production manager [[Robert Maier]] recalled the challenges of shooting without adequate facilities, how the cast and crew overwhelmed the farm's septic system, how heavy rains nearly washed away the set, and how "charmed" Waters seemed through it all.{{sfn|Maier|2011|pp=116–122}}

The Mortville interiors were filmed in a 5000-sq-ft, second-story loft in a rented warehouse located in [[Fells Point, Baltimore]]. The space was unheated, noisy, and poorly suited for film production according to Maier.{{sfn|Maier|2011|pp=106–108}}

''Desperate Living'' was edited for 10 weeks in the basement of [[Film editor|editor]] Charles Roggero's home. It was Waters' first film with original music, by Chris Lobingier and Allen Yanus, whom Waters asked to provide a "cheesy ''[[Doctor Zhivago (film)|Doctor Zhivago]]''-type score".{{sfn|Waters|1981|p=173}}

===Casting===
''Desperate Living'' is the only feature film Waters made without Divine prior to the actor's death in 1988. Divine had to reluctantly back away from the film because he was committed to appearing in ''[[Women Behind Bars#1976 revival|Women Behind Bars]]''. [[Susan Lowe]], who had appeared in small or supporting roles in Waters' previous films, was chosen to take over for the role of Mole McHenry. This was also Waters' first film without [[David Lochary]], because of Lochary's addiction to drugs. Waters said, "The reason that David wasn't in ''Desperate Living'' is because of [[Phencyclidine|PCP]]. That's all that's to it. I know that's why he wasn't in the film, and he knows it, too."{{sfn|Levy|2015|p=[https://books.google.com/books?id=uwnSBgAAQBAJ&pg=PA298 298]}} Lochary died a few weeks after the film's release, when he injured himself while under the influence of the drug.<ref>{{cite book |first=Dana |last=Heller |title=Hairspray |publisher=[[Wiley-Blackwell]] |location=Malden, Massachusetts |year=2011 |isbn=978-1-4051-9162-3 |page=17}}</ref>

Waters had received a copy of Liz Renay's autobiography ''My Face for the World to See'' and wanted to offer her a role in the film. He went to see Renay in a [[burlesque]] show in Boston, then traveled to Los Angeles to offer her the role of Muffy St. Jacques. He offered her only a brief outline of the story, withholding some of the more graphic details for fear that she might refuse the role. Renay accepted the offer and flew to [[Baltimore]] for three weeks of shooting (which was, reportedly, all that the production could afford to pay Renay for her services).{{sfn|Waters|1981|p=162}}

==Release==
{{More citations needed section|date=June 2025}}

As with Waters' previous films, the premiere was held in the auditorium of the [[University of Baltimore]]. A brief controversy arose when lesbian groups attacked the film for its depiction of lesbianism, and for taking the title of a defunct lesbian magazine.{{citation needed|date=June 2025}}

[[New Line Cinema]] blew the film up from [[16 mm film|16]] to [[35mm movie film|35 mm]] and opened it at [[Midnight movie|midnight]] in [[Manhattan]], though the original poster (featuring a cooked rat on a plate) was rejected by ''[[The New York Times]]'' to run, forcing a new poster to be created three days before the opening.{{citation needed|date=June 2025}} The new poster featured Liz Renay in a screaming pose, fashioned from a production still.

Critics from ''[[Good Housekeeping]]'' walked out of the film after 10 minutes.{{Citation needed|date=January 2022}} Otherwise, ''[[Playboy]]'' enjoyed the film, stating it had to be "seen to be believed". David Chute of ''[[The Phoenix (newspaper)|The Boston Phoenix]]'' said of the film: "In ''Desperate Living'', Waters comes close to creating a work of true trash art."{{Citation needed|date=January 2022}}

On the [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film holds an approval rating of 73% based on 11 reviews, with an average rating of 5.9/10.<ref>{{rotten-tomatoes|desperate_living|Desperate Living}}</ref>
