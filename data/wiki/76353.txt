'''''The Shop Around the Corner''''' is a 1940 American [[Romance film|romantic]] [[comedy-drama]] film produced and directed by [[Ernst Lubitsch]] starring [[Margaret Sullavan]], [[James Stewart]], [[Frank Morgan]], and [[Joseph Schildkraut]]. The screenplay by [[Samson Raphaelson]] is based on the 1937 Hungarian play ''[[Parfumerie]]'' by [[Miklós László]].<ref name="THR">{{cite magazine|title=The Shop Around the Corner: THR's 1940 Review|magazine=[[The Hollywood Reporter]]|url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/shop-around-the-corner-1940-review-1235067096/ |access-date=March 20, 2023}}</ref><ref>László, Miklós (1957). ''Parfumerie: a comedy in three acts''. M. Liebman Productions, New York. {{OCLC|31155371}}</ref>

The film is about two employees at a leather goods shop in pre-war [[Budapest]] who can barely stand each other, not realizing they are falling in love as anonymous correspondents through their letters. It follows social themes associated with the lives of the middle class.

Though the film did not do well at the box office, it was met with generally positive reviews from film critics. It has since been adapted into three productions. In 1999, ''The Shop Around the Corner'' was selected for preservation in the [[National Film Registry]], deemed as "culturally, historically or aesthetically" significant and is also included in the all-time top 100 movies selected by ''[[Time (magazine)|Time]]'' magazine.

== Plot ==

During the [[Great Depression]], Alfred Kralik is the top salesman at Matuschek and Company, a leathergoods shop in [[Budapest]] owned by the high-strung Mr. Hugo Matuschek. Kralik's co-workers include his friend, Pirovitch, a kindly family man; Ferencz Vadas, a two-faced womanizer; saleswoman Ilona Novotny; clerk Flora Kaczek; and Pepi Katona, an ambitious, precocious delivery boy. One morning, Kralik reveals to Pirovitch that he has been corresponding anonymously with an intelligent and cultured woman whose ad he came across in the newspaper. Kralik responded to the ad, wanting to increase his knowledge about culture. As they correspond, they agree to withhold details about their personal lives.

Kralik is Mr. Matuschek's oldest and most trusted employee, but tension starts arising between the two. They get into an argument over Mr. Matuschek's idea to sell a cigarette box that plays "[[Dark Eyes (Russian song)|Ochi Chërnye]]" when opened. After their exchange, Klara Novak enters the gift shop looking for a job. Kralik and Mr. Matuschek tell her there are no openings, but when she is able to sell one of the cigarette boxes as a candy box, Mr. Matuschek hires her. However, she and Kralik do not get along. Mr. Matuschek is becoming irritable because he suspects his wife is having an affair, as she stays out late and requests money from him.

Six months later, as Christmas approaches, Kralik is preparing to meet his mystery correspondent (Novak) for a dinner date. On that day, Mr. Matuschek suddenly demands that everyone stay after work to decorate the shop. Kralik asks to speak with Matuschek in his office and requests the night off. Mr. Matuschek becomes angry, and Kralik implies that he is willing to leave his job. Novak also asks to have the night off, which further infuriates Matuschek. Now, Mr. Matuschek calls Kralik to his office and fires him, giving him one month's pay and a letter of recommendation. No one in the shop understands Mr. Matuschek's actions are related to his suspicions that Kralik is having an affair with his wife. Later, Mr. Matuschek meets with a private investigator who informs him that his wife is having an affair with Vadas, not Kralik. Shocked, Matuschek says, almost to himself, that he was married for 22 years to his wife and proud of her, but "I guess she didn't want to grow old with me." Later, Pepi returns to the shop just in time to prevent Mr. Matuschek from committing suicide by shooting himself.

Meanwhile, Kralik arrives at the Cafe Nizza, where he discovers that his mystery woman is Novak. Despite his disappointment, Kralik goes in and talks with her, pretending he is there to meet Pirovitch. In his mind, Kralik tries to reconcile the cultured woman of his letters with his annoying co-worker, secretly hoping that things might work out with her. Concerned that Kralik's presence will spoil her first meeting with her mystery correspondent, she conducts a continuously abrasive conversation with Kralik, finally calling him a "little insignificant clerk." Deeply insulted, Kralik leaves.

[[Image:Shop-Around-the-Corner.jpg|thumb|[[Margaret Sullavan|Sullavan]], [[James Stewart|Stewart]] & [[Frank Morgan|Morgan]] [[promotional still]]]]
Later that night, Kralik goes to the hospital to visit Mr. Matuschek, who apologizes for suspecting him of having an affair with his wife before offering him a job as manager of Matuschek and Company. Grateful to Pepi for saving his life, Mr. Matuschek promotes him to clerk. The next day, Novak calls in sick after her mystery man failed to show, and at Mr. Matuschek's behest, Kralik fires Vadas. That night, when Kralik visits Novak at her apartment, she receives a letter from her correspondent and reads it in front of Kralik, who wrote the letter.

Two weeks later, on Christmas Eve, Matuschek and Company achieves record sales. Kralik and Novak, alone in the shop as they close up, discuss their planned dates for the evening. Novak reveals that she had a crush on Kralik when they first met. She explains that she was initially irritable with him in an effort to pique his interest in her. After pretending to have met Novak's mystery man, Kralik describes him in unflattering terms. When Novak expresses her disappointment, Kralik quotes one of her letters and places a red carnation in his lapel, revealing to her that he is her mystery correspondent. They embrace and kiss.

== Production ==
[[File:1940 - Colonial Theater - 25 Jan - Allentown PA.jpg|thumb|left|upright=0.8|Colonial Theater advertisement]]

=== Development ===
[[Ernst Lubitsch]] purchased the rights to [[Miklós László]]'s play ''Parfumerie'' in 1938.{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=62}} According to ''Variety'', Lubitsch initially planned to create a film adaptation of the play working with [[Myron Selznick]].<ref name=":0" /> After Steffie Trondle translated the play into English, Lubitsch worked with screenwriter Samson Raphaelson to write the script from 1938 to 1939.{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|p=263}} Lubitsch considered it “the best script I have had in a long time in my hands”.{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|p=265}} Selznick proved unable to fund the production, so the filmmakers turned to other methods.{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|p=264}} Lubitsch and Selznick pitched the film to [[Paramount Pictures|Paramount]], among other studios, but none were interested in producing it.{{Sfn|McBride|2018|p=364}} At the time Hollywood was still hesitant to produce films about the middle class.{{Sfn|McBride|2018|p=364}} Studios were also not interested in working with Lubitsch.{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|p=264}} When Lubitsch's plan did not come to fruition, he decided to work on it after joining [[Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer|MGM]].<ref name=":0">{{Cite journal |date=January 10, 1940 |title=Film Reviews: The Shop Around the Corner |url=https://archive.org/details/variety137-1940-01/page/n177 |journal=Variety |volume=137 |issue=5 |pages=14 |via=Internet Archive}}</ref> Before he joined MGM, the studio had purchased the rights to the film from him for $62,500.{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|p=266}} MGM wanted him to produce and direct ''[[Ninotchka]],'' to which he agreed on the condition that he also produce and direct ''The Shop Around the Corner''.{{Sfn|McBride|2018|p=366}} The studio agreed on the condition that ''Ninotchka'' be produced first{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|p=266}} and assigned a low production budget to ''The Shop Around the Corner'', which meant that the film's screenwriter Samson Raphaelson received a lower salary than usual.{{Sfn|McBride|2018|p=366}} In an interview Raphaelson commented that the story was substantially dialogue-based but felt that it did not threaten the film's overall quality.{{Sfn|Weinberg|1977|p=266}} He also claimed that, while the film was based on the play, it was vastly different.{{Sfn|Weinberg|1977|p=231}} In his own words, Lubitsch felt that none of his preceding films had "atmosphere and...characters [that] were truer than in this picture".{{Sfn|Weinberg|1977|pp=266-267}}

=== Casting and production ===
[[File:The Shop Around the Corner trailer.JPG|thumb|[[James Stewart|Stewart]] & [[Margaret Sullavan|Sullavan]]]]
[[Dolly Haas]], [[Margaret Tallichet]] and [[Janet Gaynor]] were each at one point attached to the film before [[Margaret Sullavan]] was cast in the lead role alongside [[James Stewart]]; neither was available at the time that production was originally set to begin, so Lubitsch decided to postpone the start date.<ref name="AFI" /> Sullavan and Stewart had worked together before in leading roles.{{Sfn|Dewey|1996|p=208}} Lubitsch chose to cast Sullavan because he decided the plotline was too risky to use an actress who was less-known.{{Sfn|Fishgall|1997|p=135}} His worries stemmed from thinking audiences might not find the plot interesting enough to watch it.{{Sfn|Fishgall|1997|p=135}} Years later Lubitsch remarked that Sullavan and Stewart worked well together and did not try to out-do each other in their acting.{{Sfn|Dewey|1996|p=210}} Lubitsch called Sullavan "a tonic for the cast" because of her playfulness between scenes.{{Sfn|Coe|1994|p=64}} Raphaelson commented that Stewart's portrayal of Kralik "is one of the great performances in film history".{{Sfn|Coe|1994|p=60}} Stewart remarked, "It was wonderful to work with [Lubitsch]. He had such great style and an inspiring sort of comic touch."{{Sfn|Fishgall|1997|p=136}}

Several weeks before filming began, Henry Nordlinger researched leather goods shops so the one in the film could be an accurate portrayal.{{Sfn|Paul|1983|p=169}} Lubitsch decided the studio should use "real snow" for the Christmas Eve scenes instead of using [[fake snow]]. As a result, thirty tons of ice were shaved to create the effect.{{Sfn|Fishgall|1997|p=135}} Filming began on November 2, 1939, the day after James Stewart completed work on ''[[Destry Rides Again]]''.{{Sfn|Fishgall|1997|p=135}} The scenes were shot in the order as they appear.<ref name="AFI">{{cite web |url= https://catalog.afi.com/Film/4630-THE-SHOPAROUNDTHECORNER?sid=55d169cc-b25c-4223-a924-b7b17d295616&sr=12.285115&cp=1&pos=0 |title= The Shop Around the Corner (1940) |website= [[AFI Catalog of Feature Films]] |access-date= December 5, 2024}}</ref>{{Sfn|Paul|1983|p=171}} In total, filming took about a month.<ref>{{harvnb|Fishgall|1997|p=137}}; {{harvnb|Carringer|Sabath|1978|p=149}}; {{harvnb|Weinberg|1977|p=287}}</ref> The final cost was $474,000.{{Sfn|McBride|2018|p=366}}

== Release and box office ==
[[File:The Shop Around the Corner trailer (1940).webm|thumb|left|thumbtime=15|upright=0.75|[[:File:The Shop Around the Corner trailer (1940).webm|Film trailer]]]]
The release trailer features Morgan as Matuschek introducing the other characters and who plays them. At the end he introduces Lubitsch''.{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=63}}'' Film historian Joseph McBride suggests that this connects the film to Lubitsch's early roots as an actor who appeared in films as a shop assistant.{{Sfn|McBride|2018|p=387}} ''The Shop Around the Corner'' was released on January 12, 1940{{Sfn|Carringer|Sabath|1978|p=149}} and premiered in the Radio City Music Hall on January 25.{{Sfn|Fishgall|1997|p=137}} Upon its release it performed poorly and consequently box office rates were low.{{Sfn|Weinberg|1977|p=174}} At the same time, it received more box office success than Lubitsch's previous film, 1939's ''Ninotchka''.{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|p=279}} ''The Shop Around the Corner'' earned a net profit of $380,000 and has grossed $1.3 million worldwide.{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|pp=179-180}}

== Reception ==
[[File:The_Shop_Around_the_Corner_Lobby_Card.jpg|thumb|Lobby card]]
=== Contemporary ===
''The Shop Around the Corner'' received generally positive reviews when it was released. Critics praised the actors for their performance. ''[[Photoplay]]'' remarked that "Morgan…offers a truly sterling performance, as do Joseph Schildkraut and all the others”.<ref name=":1">{{Cite journal |title=The Shop Around the Corner—MGM |url=https://archive.org/details/photoplayvolume554chicx/page/n243 |journal=Photoplay |date=June 1940 |volume=54 |issue=1 |pages=63, 89 |via=Internet Archive}}</ref> ''[[Newsweek]]'' also praised Morgan for his performance as Matuschek, commenting that he "handles deftly one of his rare opportunities to play a straight dramatic role”. It also wrote that "James Stewart and Margaret Sullavan are perfectly cast as the youngsters”.<ref>{{Cite journal |date=January 29, 1940 |title=Lubitsch and the Simple Life |url=https://archive.org/details/sim_newsweek-us_1940-01-29_15_5/page/34 |journal=Newsweek |volume=15 |issue=5 |pages=34 |via=Internet Archive}}</ref> ''[[The Hollywood Reporter]]'' claimed it would "win new audiences for pictures and return many of the old customers who have deserted”. It also called [[William Daniels (cinematographer)|William Daniels]]' camera work "top class".<ref>{{Cite web |date=December 23, 2021 |orig-date=January 3, 1940 |title='The Shop Around the Corner': THR's 1940 Review |url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/shop-around-the-corner-1940-review-1235067096/ |website=The Hollywood Reporter}}</ref> ''Variety'' praised the film's "vivaciousness and piquant humor" and remarked that the "production background and settings—although unpretentious—are excellent, with photography…of top calibre".<ref name=":0" /> William R. Weaver of ''Motion Picture Herald'' wrote that it "succeeds in being pleasantly entertaining" and that it "seemed to satisfy generally" at a preview showing.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Weaver |first=William R. |date=January 6, 1940 |title=Showmen's Reviews: The Shop Around the Corner |url=https://archive.org/details/motionpictureher138unse/page/n49 |journal=Motion Picture Herald |volume=138 |issue=1 |pages=42 |via=Internet Archive}}</ref> According to film historian Thomas Doherty, some critics praised the film for not having as many sexual undertones as Lubitsch's other films.{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=62}} Other audience members were hesitant about its European setting because of [[World War II]],{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=63}} while others felt that Stewart's portrayal of his character was not European enough.{{Sfn|Dewey|1996|p=211}}

=== Recent ===
''The Shop Around the Corner'' continues to receive critical attention. On the [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film holds an approval rating of 99% based on 96 reviews, with an average rating of 8.6/10. The website's critics consensus reads: "Deftly directed by Ernst Lubitsch from a smart, funny script by Samson Raphaelson, ''The Shop Around the Corner'' is a romantic comedy in the finest sense of the term."<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/shop_around_the_corner |title=The Shop Around the Corner |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |access-date=November 25, 2025}}</ref> [[Metacritic]], which uses a [[Weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average]], assigned the {{#switch:  | episode = episode | series = series | season = season | film }} a score of 96 out of 100, based on 15 critics, indicating "universal acclaim".<ref name="Metacritic">{{cite web |title=''The Shop Around the Corner'' |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-shop-around-the-corner |website=[[Metacritic]] |publisher=[[Fandom (website)#Fandom, Inc.|Fandom, Inc.]] |access-date=November 15, 2024 |archive-url= |archive-date= |url-status=}}</ref> It ranked as #202 in the [[British == Adaptations and remakes ==

=== Adaptations and awards ===
''The Shop Around the Corner'' was dramatized in two half-hour broadcasts of ''[[The Screen Guild Theater]].'' The first aired on September 29, 1940, and starred Sullavan, Stewart,<ref>{{cite journal|title=Those Were The Days|journal=Nostalgia Digest|date=Summer 2015|volume=41|issue=3|pages=32–39}}</ref> and Morgan in their respective roles; in this version the adultery aspect was removed.{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=63}} The performance was held to benefit the Motion Picture Relief Fund.{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=63}} The second broadcast aired on February 26, 1945, with [[Van Johnson]] and [[Phyllis Thaxter]].{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=63}} It was also dramatized as a one-hour program on ''[[Lux Radio Theater]]''{{'}}s June 23, 1941, broadcast with [[Claudette Colbert]] and [[Don Ameche]] as Novak and Kralik, along with [[Cecil B. DeMille]] as narrator.{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=63}} Both radio adaptations were included on the December 2020 [[Warner Archive Collection|Warner Archive]] Blu-ray release of the film, sourced from the brand new 2K digital restoration of the 35mm interpositive.{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=63}}

''The Shop Around the Corner'' is ranked number 28 on [[AFI's 100 Years... 100 Passions]]<ref>{{Cite web |title=AFI's 100 YEARS…100 PASSIONS |url=https://www.afi.com/afis-100-years-100-passions/ |access-date=2025-04-17 |website=American Film Institute |language=en}}</ref> and is listed in ''Time''{{'}}s All-Time 100 Movies.<ref name="time">{{cite magazine |last1=Corliss |first1=Richard |last2=Schickel |first2=Richard |date=February 12, 2005 |title=All-Time 100 Movies |url=https://entertainment.time.com/2005/02/12/all-time-100-movies/#the-shop-around-the-corner-1940 |access-date=March 23, 2012 |magazine=Time}}</ref> In 1999, the film was selected for preservation in the United States [[National Film Registry]] by the [[Library of Congress]] as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".<ref name="loc">{{cite web |title=Complete National Film Registry Listing |url=https://www.loc.gov/film/titles.html |access-date=October 18, 2022 |publisher=Library of Congress}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Madigan |first=Nick |date=1999-11-16 |title=National Film Registry adds 25 pix to archive |url=https://variety.com/1999/film/news/national-film-registry-adds-25-pix-to-archive-1117758137/ |access-date=2022-10-18 |website=Variety}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |title=Preserving the Silver Screen (December 1999) – Library of Congress Information Bulletin |url=https://www.loc.gov/loc/lcib/9912/nfb.html |access-date=2020-08-18 |website=www.loc.gov}}</ref> Sullavan, Stewart, and Morgan were awarded "Best Performances of the Month" in ''Photoplay'' magazine.<ref name=":1" /> It was also named one of the "Best Pictures of the Month".<ref name=":1" />

=== Remakes ===
The film has spawned numerous [[remake]]s, including:

* A [[musical film|musical]], ''[[In the Good Old Summertime]]'' (1949), starring [[Judy Garland]] and [[Van Johnson]].{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=62}} The Budapest setting is replaced with Chicago.{{Sfn|Fishgall|1997|p=137n1}}
* The 1963 Broadway musical ''[[She Loves Me]]''{{Sfn|Doherty|2021|p=62}} by Jerry Bock and Sheldon Harnick.{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|p=279}}

== Themes ==
''The Shop Around the Corner'' has different social themes than Lubitsch's previous films. According to [[David Sanjek]], ''The Shop Around the Corner'' differs from Lubitsch's other films because it focuses on community rather than the individual.<ref name=":02">{{Cite web |last=Sanjek |first=David |title=The Shop Around the Corner |url=https://www.sensesofcinema.com/2009/cteq/shop-around-the-corner/ |website=Senses of Cinema |date=4 April 2010 }}</ref> Lubitsch was beginning to focus more on the middle class in his films.{{Sfn|Poague|1978|pp=161-162}} In 1939 he remarked, “We must show people living in the real world. No one used to care how characters made their living if the picture was amusing. Now they do care. They want their stories tied up to life.”<ref name=":12">{{Cite news |last=Rozen |first=Leah |date=November 15, 1998 |title=For the Old Kind, Sharpen Your Pencil |work=The New York Times |pages=2}}</ref> ''The Shop Around the Corner'' steps away from "sophisticated comedy", which focuses on people belonging to the upper class.{{Sfn|Novak|2024|pp=137-138, 144}} William Paul, professor of  Film and Media Studies, claims that because it instead focuses on the middle class, it is "more thoroughly political than any of Lubitsch's historical dramas".{{Sfn|Paul|1983|p=168}}

Author Scott Eyman describes it as "a film…about men and women who are happy to be middle class and trying to stay that way".{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|p=278}} He further argues that  the film highlights "the extraordinary qualities" of average middle-class people.{{Sfn|Eyman|2000|p=279}} Of the characters, Vadas lives beyond his social class, as manifest by his affair with Mrs. Matuschek.{{Sfn|Paul|1983|p=183}} Paul explains that the difference between Vadas, Kralik, and Novak, is that Vadas attempts to live his desire for a higher-class life, while Kralik and Novak keep their desires more private.{{Sfn|Paul|1983|p=183}} Author Leland Poague sees Vadas as being symbolic of less desirable qualities in the middle class and his being fired as symbolic of the other characters getting rid of those qualities.{{Sfn|Poague|1978|p=36}} He also views the film as highlighting the emotions of middle class people.{{Sfn|Poague|1978|p=38}} In her analysis of the film, film critic and author Ivana Novak calls Kralik and Klara Novak "two awkward and shallow phonys" because they are two average people pretending to be cultured.{{Sfn|Novak|2024|p=149}} They seek to appear cultured in their letters because they are afraid of being perceived as insignificant.{{Sfn|Toles|2010|p=2}} Both Kralik and Novak believe their letters are sincere and accurately reflect who they are.{{Sfn|Toles|2010|p=2}} Their in-person interactions versus their correspondence reveal a rift between their realities and what they desire.{{Sfn|Paul|1983|p=182}} Poague argues that, because Kralik and Novak are not satisfied with their lives, they do not accept that other people are as passionate as themselves.{{Sfn|Poague|1978|p=33}} Initially, Kralik hesitates to reveal his identity to Novak because he comes to recognize that his letters do not accurately reflect who he is.{{Sfn|Toles|2010|p=10}} Actor and author George Toles suggests that Kralik and Novak are spared from living outside of their realities once they accept that they are ordinary.{{Sfn|Toles|2010|p=1}}

William Paul and author Jonathan Coe point out that money concerns play a large role in the plot. Much of the dialogue is about money,{{Sfn|Coe|1994|p=62}} and Kralik will only consider marriage if he receives a pay raise.{{Sfn|Paul|1983|p=162}} There are also concerns of hierarchy.{{Sfn|Paul|1983|p=178}} The characters believe that their positions within the shop will reflect how they are seen by society, which motivates Pepi's drive to become a clerk.{{Sfn|Paul|1983|p=178}} Both Novak and Kralik have connected their identities to their positions of power within the shop, believing that it affects how they are seen by others.{{Sfn|Paul|1983|p=179}}
