'''''The Wiz''''' is a 1978 American [[musical film|musical]] [[contemporary fantasy]] film directed by [[Sidney Lumet]]. Adapted from the [[The Wiz|1974 Broadway musical]], the film reimagines the classic children's novel ''[[The Wonderful Wizard of Oz]]'' by [[L. Frank Baum]] with an [[African Americans|African-American]] cast. [[Dorothy Gale]], a 24-year-old teacher from [[Harlem]], is transported to the magical [[urban fantasy|urban-fantasy]] [[Land of Oz]]. While on an adventure in order to seek help from the [[Wizard of Oz (character)|Wiz]], Dorothy encounters a [[Scarecrow (Oz)|Scarecrow]], a  [[Tin Woodman|Tin Man]], a [[Cowardly Lion]] and a [[Wicked Witch]].

Produced by [[Universal Pictures]] and [[de Passe Entertainment|Motown Productions]], filming took place in [[Queens]], New York City, from July to December 1977, with a cast starring [[Diana Ross]], [[Michael Jackson]] (in his only feature film role), [[Nipsey Russell]], [[Ted Ross]], [[Mabel King]], [[Theresa Merritt]], [[Thelma Carpenter]], [[Lena Horne]] and [[Richard Pryor]]. The story is reworked from [[William F. Brown (writer)|William F. Brown]]'s Broadway [[libretto]] by [[Joel Schumacher]], and [[Quincy Jones]] supervised the adaptation of songs by [[Charlie Smalls]] and [[Luther Vandross]]. A handful of new songs, written by Jones and the songwriting team [[Ashford and Simpson|Nickolas Ashford and Valerie Simpson]], were added for the project.

==Plot==
A crowded [[Thanksgiving (United States)|Thanksgiving]] dinner brings a host of family together in a [[Harlem]] apartment, where 24-year-old elementary schoolteacher [[Dorothy Gale]] lives with her [[Aunt Em]] and [[Uncle Henry (Oz)|Uncle Henry]]. She is teased by Aunt Em for having delayed moving out to start her life as an adult. While Dorothy cleans up after the meal, her dog [[Toto (Oz)|Toto]] runs out the open kitchen door into a snowstorm. She retrieves him but finds herself trapped in the storm. A magical whirlwind made of snow materializes and transports them to the land of [[Land of Oz|Oz]].

As Dorothy descends from the atmosphere, she smashes through an electric "Oz" sign which falls upon and kills Evermean, the [[Wicked Witch of the East]] who rules [[Munchkin Country|Munchkinland]]. As a result, she frees the [[Munchkin]]s who populate the playground into which she lands. The Munchkins' main benefactress, Miss One, is the [[Good Witch of the North (Baum)|Good Witch of the North]], a magical [[Numbers game|"numbers runner"]] who gives Dorothy Evermean's charmed silver shoes. Since Dorothy is far from home, Miss One urges her to follow the [[yellow brick road]] to the [[Emerald City]] and seek the help of the powerful "[[Wizard of Oz (character)|Wiz]]". After telling her never to take the shoes off, Miss One and the Munchkins disappear and Dorothy is left to search for the road on her own.

The next morning, Dorothy happens upon a [[Scarecrow (Oz)|scarecrow]] made of garbage and saves him from being teased by humanoid crows. The Scarecrow hopes that the Wiz will give him the one thing he feels that he lacks: a brain. They discover the yellow brick road and begin their journey to the Emerald City. They eventually meet a [[Tin Woodman|man made out of tin]] in an abandoned amusement park and a [[Cowardly Lion|cowardly lion]] who was banished from the jungle. The Tin Man and Lion join them on their quest to find the Wiz, hoping to gain a heart and courage, respectively. En route, the foursome escape from a haunted subway station and a group of prostitutes known as the "Poppy Girls", who seduce the Lion. The Lion feels ashamed of leading Dorothy into a trap, but she and the rest of the gang cheer him up.

Finally reaching the Emerald City - depicted as a rendition of the [[World Trade Center (1973–2001)|World Trade Center]] plaza, the quartet are granted an audience with the Wiz, who appears as a giant fire-breathing metallic head. He proclaims that he will only grant their wishes on the condition that they kill Evermean's sister, Evillene, the [[Wicked Witch of the West]], who runs a sweatshop in Oz's underground sewers. Before they can reach her domain, Evillene learns of their quest to kill her and sends out her army of [[Winged monkeys|Flying Monkeys]] to capture them. As revenge on Dorothy for killing her sister, she dismembers the Scarecrow, flattens the Tin Man, and hangs the Lion up by his tail to make Dorothy give her the shoes. When she threatens to throw Toto into a fiery cauldron, Dorothy nearly gives in until the Scarecrow hints to her to activate a fire sprinkler switch. The sprinklers put out the fire and melt Evillene. With Evillene dead, her spells lose their power, resulting in the freedom of all her sweatshop workers and prisoners.

The Monkeys give Dorothy and her friends a ride back to the Emerald City. Upon going in the back entrance, they discover that the Wiz is actually Herman Smith, a washed-up politician from [[Atlantic City, New Jersey|Atlantic City]] who came to Oz by accident in a [[hot air balloon]]. The Scarecrow, Tin Man, and Lion are distraught that they will never receive their brain, heart, and courage, but Dorothy makes them realize that they already have had these things all along even as she fears she will never find her own way home. [[Glinda the Good Witch|Glinda, the Good Witch of the South]], appears and implores her to use the magic of the slippers. After thanking Glinda and saying goodbye to her friends, she reminisces about [[Home (The Wiz song)|home]]. She clicks her heels together three times and discovers that she is back near home with Toto in her arms.

==Production==
===Pre-production and development===
''The Wiz'' is the eighth feature film produced by [[de Passe Entertainment|Motown Productions]], the film and television division of [[Berry Gordy]]'s [[Motown Records]] label. Gordy originally wanted the teenaged [[Rhythm and blues|R&B]] singer [[Stephanie Mills]], who had originated the role on [[Broadway theatre|Broadway]], to be cast as [[Dorothy Gale]]. When Motown star [[Diana Ross]] asked Gordy if she could be cast as Dorothy, he declined, saying that Ross—then 33 years old—was too old for the role.<ref name="dreamgirl">{{cite book | last =Adrahtas | first = Thomas | title = A Lifetime to Get Here: Diana Ross: The American Dreamgirl | publisher = AuthorHouse | year = 2006 | pages =163–167 | isbn = 1-4259-7140-7}}</ref> Ross went around Gordy and convinced executive producer [[Rob Cohen]] at [[Universal Pictures]] to arrange a deal in which he would produce the film if Ross was cast as Dorothy. Gordy and Cohen agreed to the deal. Additionally, the casting of Diana Ross as Dorothy was due to the fact that she was a well-known star, which guaranteed more funds.<ref name="time.com">{{Cite magazine |last=Skow |first=John |date=1978-10-30 |title=Cinema: Nowhere Over the Rainbow |url=https://time.com/archive/6845970/cinema-nowhere-over-the-rainbow/ |access-date=2024-12-18 |magazine=TIME |language=en}}</ref> [[Pauline Kael]], a film critic, described Ross's efforts to get the film into production as "perhaps the strongest example of sheer will in film history".<ref name="dreamgirl" />

After filmmaker [[John Badham]] learned that Ross was cast as Dorothy, he decided not to direct, and Cohen replaced him with [[Sidney Lumet]].<ref name="dreamgirl" /> Of his decision not to direct ''The Wiz'', Badham recalled telling Cohen that he thought that Ross was "a wonderful singer. She's a terrific actress and a great dancer, but she's not this character. She's not the little six-year-old girl Dorothy in ''[[The Wonderful Wizard of Oz|The Wizard of Oz]]''."<ref>{{cite book | last = Emery | first = Robert J. | title = The Directors: Take One | publisher = Allworth Communications, Inc. | year = 2002 | isbn = 1-58115-219-1 | page = [https://archive.org/details/directorstaketwo0000emer/page/333 333] | url = https://archive.org/details/directorstaketwo0000emer/page/333 }}</ref> Lumet's hiring was met with skepticism, for he was known as a dramatic filmmaker with no previous musical directing experience.<ref name=":0" />

Although [[20th Century Fox]] had financially backed the [[The Wiz|stage musical]], it exercised its [[Right of first refusal|first refusal rights]] to the film production, which gave [[Universal Pictures|Universal]] an opening to finance the film.<ref name="lumet" /> Initially, Universal was so excited about the film's prospects that it did not set a budget for production.<ref name="lumet" />

===Writing===
[[Joel Schumacher]]'s script is influenced by [[Werner Erhard]]'s teachings and his [[Erhard Seminars Training]] ("est") movement because both Schumacher and Ross were "very enamored of Werner Erhard".<ref name="silvester">{{cite book | last = Silvester | first = Christopher |author2=Steven Bach | title = The Grove Book of Hollywood | publisher = Grove Press | year=2002 | pages = 555–560 | isbn = 0-8021-3878-0}}</ref> "Before I knew it," said Rob Cohen, "the movie was becoming an est-ian fable full of est buzzwords about knowing who you are and sharing and all that. I hated the script a lot. But it was hard to argue with [Ross] because she was recognizing in this script all of this stuff that she had worked out in est seminars."<ref name="silvester" /> Schumacher spoke positively of the results of the est training, stating that he was "eternally grateful for learning that I was responsible for my life".<ref name="silvester" /> However, he also complained that "everybody stayed exactly the way they were and went around spouting all this bullshit".<ref name="silvester" /> Regarding est and Erhard references in the film, ''The Grove Book of Hollywood'' notes that the speech delivered by [[Glinda|Glinda the Good Witch]] ([[Lena Horne]]) at the end of the film was "a litany of est-like platitudes", and the book makes est comparisons to the song "Believe in Yourself".<ref name="silvester" /> Although Schumacher had seen the Broadway play before writing the script, none of the play's writing was incorporated into the film, a fact that was noted and heavily criticized after release.<ref name="movieline.com">{{cite news |last=Vanairsdale |first=S.T. |year=2011 |title=Joel Schumacher Tells Movieline About the Time He Wrote The Wiz |work=[[Movieline.com]] |url=http://movieline.com/2011/10/10/joel-schumacher-tells-movieline-about-the-time-he-wrote-the-wiz/ |access-date=2015-12-29 |archive-date=August 9, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200809114841/http://movieline.com/2011/10/10/joel-schumacher-tells-movieline-about-the-time-he-wrote-the-wiz/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

During production, Lumet felt that the finished film would be "an absolutely unique experience that nobody has ever witnessed before".<ref name="lumet">{{cite book | last = Lumet | first = Sidney | author-link = Sidney Lumet | author2 = Joanna E. Rapf | title =Sidney Lumet: Interviews | publisher = Univ. Press of Mississippi | year =2006 | pages = 78, 80 | isbn = 1-57806-724-3}}</ref> When asked about any possible influence from [[MGM]]'s popular 1939 film adaptation titled ''[[The Wizard of Oz (1939 film)|The Wizard of Oz]]'', Lumet stated that "there was nothing to be gained from [the 1939 film] other than to make certain we didn't use anything from it. They made a brilliant movie, and even though our concept is different – they're Kansas, we're New York; they're white, we're black, and the score and the books are totally different – we wanted to make sure that we never overlapped in any area."<ref name="lumet" />

===Casting===
[[Michael Jackson]], a former Motown star and close friend of Ross, was cast as [[Scarecrow (Oz)|the Scarecrow]]. At the start of development, he and his brothers [[The Jackson 5|The Jacksons]] (with the exception of [[Jermaine Jackson]]) had departed Motown for [[Epic Records]] in 1975, after the release of their tenth album ''[[Moving Violation]]'' (although Michael had not released a solo album since ''[[Forever, Michael]]''). Cohen, head of Motown Productions, thought that Jackson would be perfect for the role of the Scarecrow, and approached Gordy with the idea. Gordy agreed, although Lumet was harder to convince.<ref>{{Cite magazine|url=https://time.com/4135018/the-wiz-michael-jackson/|title=Behind the Scenes of The Wiz With Michael Jackson|magazine=Time|access-date=April 7, 2022|archive-date=April 7, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220407164928/https://time.com/4135018/the-wiz-michael-jackson/|url-status=live}}</ref> Lumet wanted [[Jimmie Walker]], star of [[CBS|CBS-TV]]'s ''[[Good Times]]'', telling Cohen, "Michael Jackson's a [[Las Vegas|Vegas]] act. The Jackson 5's a Vegas act."

[[Quincy Jones]] was also skeptical of Jackson, but after Cohen arranged a meeting, flying 18-year-old Jackson to New York, Lumet and Jones saw the qualities that Cohen saw. Jackson's father [[Joe Jackson (manager)|Joseph]] was wary of the project and saw it as a threat to the Jacksons group cohesion. Cohen moved Michael (and his sister [[La Toya Jackson|La Toya]]) into a [[Manhattan]] apartment, allowing him to be on his own for the first time. During production, Jackson became a frequent visitor to New York's famous [[Studio 54]]. Jackson was dedicated to the Scarecrow role and watched videotapes of gazelles, cheetahs and panthers to learn graceful movements for his part.<ref name="quincy" />

The long hours of uncomfortable prosthetic makeup by [[Stan Winston]] did not bother him. During production, Jackson asked Quincy Jones who he would recommend as a producer on his yet unrecorded solo album project. Jones, impressed by Jackson's professionalism, talent and work ethic, offered to be the producer of what became ''[[Off the Wall]]'' (1979), ''[[Thriller (Michael Jackson album)|Thriller]]'' (1982) and ''[[Bad (album)|Bad]]'' (1987).

[[Ted Ross]] and [[Mabel King]] were brought in to reprise their respective roles of the [[Cowardly Lion]] and [[Wicked Witch of the West|Evillene]] from the stage musical, while [[Nipsey Russell]] was cast as the [[Tin Woodman|Tin Man]]. [[Lena Horne]], Lumet's mother-in-law at the time of production, was cast as Glinda the Good Witch, and [[Richard Pryor]] portrayed [[Wizard of Oz (character)|The Wiz]].<ref name="dreamgirl" /><ref name="pecktal" /> The film's choreographer was [[Louis Johnson (dancer)|Louis Johnson]].<ref>{{cite news |last1=Kourlas |first1=Gia |title=Louis Johnson, 90, Genre-Crossing Dancer and Choreographer, Dies |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2020/04/10/arts/dance/louis-johnson-90-genre-crossing-dancer-and-choreographer-dies.html |access-date=1 June 2020 |work=[[The New York Times]] |date=10 April 2020 |archive-date=June 16, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210616171239/https://www.nytimes.com/2020/04/10/arts/dance/louis-johnson-90-genre-crossing-dancer-and-choreographer-dies.html |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Filming===
[[Principal photography]] began on July 13, 1977, and concluded on December 29, 1977.<ref name=AFI>{{cite web|url=https://catalog.afi.com/Film/57058-THE-WIZ|publisher=American Film Institute|title=The Wiz|access-date=April 19, 2021|archive-date=April 19, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210419235106/https://catalog.afi.com/Film/57058-THE-WIZ|url-status=live}}</ref> It took place at [[Kaufman Astoria Studios|Astoria Studios]] in [[Queens]], New York. The decaying [[New York State Pavilion]] from the [[1964 New York World's Fair]] was used as the set for [[Munchkin Country|Munchkinland]], Meadow Lake Bridge was the first [[Yellow brick road|Yellow Brick Road]] scene after the Scarecrow was helped from his pole. [[Astroland]] at [[Coney Island]] was used for the Tin Man scene, with [[Coney Island Cyclone|The Cyclone]] as a backdrop, while the [[World Trade Center (1973–2001)|World Trade Center]] served as the [[Emerald City]].<ref name="campbell">{{cite book | last =Campbell | first = Lisa D. | title = Michael Jackson: The King of Pop | publisher = Branden Books | year =1993 | isbn = 0-8283-1957-X | page =41}}</ref> The Emerald City scenes were elaborate, using 650 dancers, 385 crew members and 1,200 costumes.<ref name="campbell" /><ref name="kempton">{{cite book | last = Kempton | first = Arthur | title = Boogaloo: The Quintessence Of American Popular Music | publisher =University of Michigan Press | year =2005 | isbn = 0-472-03087-6 | page =316}}</ref> [[Costume designer]] [[Tony Walton]] enlisted the help of high fashion designers in New York City for the Emerald City sequence, and obtained exotic costumes and fabric from designers such as [[Oscar de la Renta]] and [[Norma Kamali]].<ref name="pecktal">{{cite book | last = Pecktal | first = Lynn | author2 = Tony Walton | author2-link = Tony Walton | title =Costume Design: Techniques of Modern Masters | publisher =Back Stage Books | year = 1999 | pages = 215–218 | isbn =0-8230-8812-X }}</ref> [[Albert Whitlock]] created the film's visual special effects,<ref name="lumet" /> while Stan Winston served as the head makeup artist.<ref name="pecktal" />

===Songs and score===
Quincy Jones served as musical supervisor and music producer.<ref name="quincy" /> He later wrote that he initially did not want to work on the film, but did it as a favor to Lumet.<ref name="quincy">{{cite book | last = Jones | first = Quincy | author-link = Quincy Jones | title = Q: The Autobiography of Quincy Jones | publisher = Broadway Books | year = 2002 | pages = [https://archive.org/details/qautobiographyof00jone_0/page/229 229, 259] | isbn = 0-7679-0510-5 | url = https://archive.org/details/qautobiographyof00jone_0/page/229 }}</ref> The film marked Jones's first time working with Jackson, and would eventually produce three hit albums for Jackson: ''Off the Wall'' (1979), ''Thriller'' (1982) and ''Bad'' (1987).<ref>{{cite book | last =Bronson | first =Fred | title =Billboard's Hottest Hot 100 Hits | publisher = Watson-Guptill | year =2003 | isbn = 0-8230-7738-1 | page =107}}</ref> Jones recalled working with Jackson as one of his favorite experiences from ''The Wiz'', and spoke of Jackson's dedication to his role, comparing his acting style to [[Sammy Davis Jr.]]<ref name="quincy" /> Jones had a brief [[cameo appearance]] during the "Gold" segment of the Emerald City sequence, playing what looks like a fifty-foot grand piano.

Of the 28 numbers originally composed by [[Charlie Smalls]] and company, 17 were retained in full or in part for the film. "Tornado Ballet", "I Was Born on the Day Before Yesterday", "Kalidah Battle", "Lion's Dream", "Emerald City Ballet (Psst)", "So You Wanted to Meet the Wizard", the Act 2 Entr'acte, "Funky Monkeys", "Who Do You Think You Are?", "Y'all Got It", and "A Rested Body" were cut.{{citation needed|date=December 2024}}

Four new numbers were added: "Can I Go On?", the instrumental "Poppy Girls Theme", "Emerald City Sequence" and "Is This What Feeling Gets? (Dorothy's Theme)" (the latter of which was cut from the film but featured on the soundtrack). "[[You Can't Win (song)|You Can't Win]]", a song originally written for the musical's [[Baltimore]] run that was cut after it was transferred to Broadway, was reincorporated.

==Release==
''The Wiz'' premiered at [[Palladium Times Square|Loew's Astor Plaza]] in New York City on October 24, 1978, and also opened in five other theaters in the New York area.<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=November 1, 1978|page=6|title=New York Soundtrack}}</ref><ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=November 1, 1978|page=10|title=Picture Grosses}}</ref> It opened on October 26 at the [[Cinerama Dome]] in [[Los Angeles]] and on October 27 in 29 other theaters, including in [[Chicago]], [[Washington, D.C.|Washington D.C.]], [[Philadelphia]] and [[Detroit]]. On November 3, it expanded to 67 theaters, and was due to open nationwide in 400 to 500 theaters on December 22.<ref name=open>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=November 1, 1978|page=3|title=Trade Wisdom Shaping 'The Wiz'|first1=Charles|last1=Schreger|last2=Ginsberg|first2=Steven}}</ref>

To commemorate the 50th anniversary of the stage version's opening on Broadway, the film returned to theaters for a limited theatrical engagement on May 18 and 21, 2025.<ref>[https://www.regmovies.com/movies/the-wiz-2025-event-ho00018545] {{dead link|date=June 2026}}</ref>

===Box office===
''The Wiz'' eventually proved to be a [[Box-office bomb|commercial failure]], for the $24&nbsp;million production earned only $13.6&nbsp;million at the box office.<ref name="sharp" /><ref name="harpole" /><ref name="dreamgirl" /> The film did well in its opening week, grossing $942,814 from 36 theaters in 8 markets, finishing third at the US box office behind ''[[Midnight Express (film)|Midnight Express]]'' and ''[[Animal House]]''.<ref name=open/><ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|title=50 Top-Grossing Films|date=November 8, 1978|page=9}}</ref> In its second week, grosses from the 36 theaters reduced by 12.6%, and, with additional theaters added, the film grossed $2.5 million after 10 days of release.<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=November 8, 1978|page=1|title=2d Week Steady, U Sees 'The Wiz' Recoup Bound}}</ref>

Although pre-release television broadcast rights had been sold to CBS for more than $10&nbsp;million, the film ultimately produced a net loss of $10.4&nbsp;million for Motown and Universal.<ref name="harpole">{{cite book | last = Harpole | first = Charles | title = History of the American Cinema | publisher = Simon & Schuster | year = 2003 | pages = [https://archive.org/details/historyofamerica0000unse_x8l5/page/64 64, 65, 219, 220, 290] | isbn = 0-684-80463-8 | url = https://archive.org/details/historyofamerica0000unse_x8l5/page/64 }}</ref><ref name="dreamgirl" /> At the time, it was the most expensive film musical ever made.<ref name="skow">{{cite magazine | last = Skow | first = John | title = Nowhere Over the Rainbow | magazine = [[Time (magazine)|TIME]] | publisher = [[Time Warner]] | date = October 30, 1978 | url = http://www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,912236,00.html | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20081205190138/http://www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,912236,00.html | url-status = dead | archive-date = December 5, 2008 | access-date = 2007-11-06}}</ref> For several years, the film's failure steered Hollywood studios away from producing the all-black film projects that had become popular during the [[blaxploitation]] era of the early-to-mid-1970s.<ref name="Moon 1997 xii" /><ref name="benshoff" /><ref name="George" />

==Reception==
===Critical reception===
{{Rotten Tomatoes prose|38||116|The Wiz is a workmanlike movie musical that lacks the electricity of the stage version and its cinematic inspiration, despite some committed performances from its all-star cast.|access-date={{RT data|access date}}}}<ref>{{Cite web |date=October 24, 1978 |title=The Wiz |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/wiz |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200821013011/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/wiz |archive-date=August 21, 2020 |access-date=November 17, 2025 |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]}}</ref> [[Metacritic]], which uses a [[Weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average]], assigned the {{#switch:  | episode = episode | series = series | season = season | film }} a score of 53 out of 100, based on 11 critics, indicating "mixed or average" reviews.<ref name="Metacritic">{{cite web |title=''The Wiz'' |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-wiz |website=[[Metacritic]] |publisher=[[Fandom (website)#Fandom, Inc.|Fandom, Inc.]] |access-date=15 September 2025 |archive-url= |archive-date= |url-status=}}</ref><ref>{{Metacritic film}}</ref>

Initial trade reviews for ''The Wiz'' from previews at [[Loew's State Theatre (New York City)|Loew's State 2]] in New York City were positive. ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' wrote that Universal had a "big, rousing, juicy hit on its hands that should enthrall the young in heart" and that the "cast is virtually flawless". It noted that the preview audience "roared, stomped, applauded" their approval of all the numbers. It also believed that it was the loudest film ever made. The overflowing crowd at the preview required a second showing.<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=October 4, 1978|page=18|title=Film Reviews: The Wiz|author=Robe.}}</ref>

Most professional critics published negative reviews for ''The Wiz'' upon its release.<ref name="sharp">{{cite book |last=Sharp |first=Kathleen |title=Mr. and Mrs. Hollywood: Edie and Lew Wasserman and Their Entertainment Empire |publisher=Carroll & Graf Publishers |year=2003 |pages=[https://archive.org/details/mrmrshollywooded00shar/page/357 357–358] |isbn=0-7867-1220-1 |url=https://archive.org/details/mrmrshollywooded00shar/page/357}}</ref><ref name="Posner">{{cite book | last =Posner | first =Gerald | title =Motown: Music, Money, Sex, and Power | publisher =Random House | year =2002 | location =New York | pages =s. 293–295 }}</ref><ref name="open" />
Many reviewers directed their criticism at Diana Ross; who, at 33 years old at the time of filming, was considered to be too old to play Dorothy.<ref name="George">{{cite book | last = George | first =Nelson | title =Where Did Our Love Go? The Rise and Fall of the Motown Sound | publisher = St. Martin's Press | year =1985 | page =193 }}</ref><ref name="screendoor">{{cite book | last = Hischak | first =Thomas S. | title = Through the Screen Door: What Happened to the Broadway Musical When It Went to Hollywood | publisher = Scarecrow Press | year =2004 | pages =140–142 | isbn = 0-8108-5018-4}}</ref><ref name="halstead">{{cite book | last =Halstead | first =Craig |author2=Chris Cadman | title =Michael Jackson the Solo Years | publisher =Authors on Line Ltd | year =2003 | pages =25, 26 | isbn =0-7552-0091-8 }}</ref><ref name="studwell">{{cite book | last =Studwell | first =William E. | author2 =David F. Lonergan | title =The Classic Rock and Roll Reader | publisher =Haworth Press | year =1999 | isbn =0-7890-0151-9 | page =[https://archive.org/details/classicrockrollr0000stud/page/137 137: "Ease On down the Road"] | url =https://archive.org/details/classicrockrollr0000stud/page/137 }}</ref> ''The Grove Book of Hollywood'' noted that "the picture finished off Diana Ross's screen career", for the film is Ross's final theatrical feature and her subsequent films premiered on television.<ref name="silvester" /><ref name="kempton" /><ref>{{cite book | last = Laufenberg | first =Norbert B. | title = Entertainment Celebrities | publisher =Trafford Publishing | year = 2005 | isbn = 1-4120-5335-8 | page = 562}}</ref>

Hischak's ''Through the Screen Door: What Happened to the Broadway Musical When It Went to Hollywood'' criticized "Joel Schumacher's cockamamy screenplay", and called "Believe in Yourself" the score's weakest song.<ref name="screendoor" /> He described Diana Ross's portrayal of Dorothy as "cold, neurotic and oddly unattractive".<ref name="screendoor" />

[[Leonard Maltin]] observed how ''The Wiz'' has “good musical numbers” and a “fine supporting cast,” but the movie was undermined by a “dreary finale” and “drearier performance” by Ross.<ref name="p443">{{cite book | last=Maltin | first=Leonard | title=Leonard Maltin's 2013 Movie Guide: The Modern Era | publisher=Penguin | date=2012-09-04 | isbn=978-1-101-60463-2 | url=https://books.google.com/books?id=yzPADT7BTRcC&pg=PT2785&dq=drearier+performance+ross&hl=en&newbks=1&newbks_redir=0&source=gb_mobile_search&sa=X&ved=2ahUKEwjnhLK2rtCTAxV7FDQIHZIGDJAQ6AF6BAgMEAM#v=onepage&q=the%2520wiz&f=false | access-date=2026-04-02 | page=}}</ref>

In his work ''History of the American Cinema'', Harpole characterized the film as "one of the decade's biggest failures" and "the year's biggest musical flop".<ref name="harpole" /> 

In his 2004 book ''[[Blockbuster (book)|Blockbuster]]'', [[Tom Shone]] referred to ''The Wiz'' as "expensive crud".<ref>{{cite book |last=Shone |first=Tom |title=Blockbuster: How Hollywood Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Summer |publisher=[[Simon & Schuster]] |year=2004 |isbn=0-7432-3568-1 |page=[https://archive.org/details/blockbusterhowho00shon/page/34 34] |url=https://archive.org/details/blockbusterhowho00shon/page/34}}</ref> In the book ''Mr. and Mrs. Hollywood'', the author criticized the script, noting, "''The Wiz'' was too scary for children, and too silly for adults."<ref name="sharp" /> [[Ray Bolger]], who played the Scarecrow in the 1939 film ''The Wizard of Oz'', did not think highly of ''The Wiz'', stating, "''The Wiz'' is overblown and will never have the universal appeal that the classic MGM musical has obtained."<ref>{{cite book | last =Fantle | first = David |author2=Tom Johnson | title =Reel to Real | publisher =Badger Books Inc. | year =2004 | isbn = 1-932542-04-3 | page =58}}</ref>

While Ross was largely the target of much criticism in ''The Wiz'', some critics noticed a contrast in the music given to Ross (Dorothy) and the music given to her cast mates. John Skow of ''[[Time (magazine)|Time]]'' described the Tin Man's music as the "best characterization of the film". He also characterized Evelline's as a "menacing [[Hard rock|hard-rock]]<nowiki/>er" and The Scarecrow's as a "piteous lament". Contrastingly, he describes Dorothy's music as dull ballads.<ref name="time.com"/>

The decision to hire Sidney Lumet, a dramatic filmmaker without any prior experience with directing musicals, was criticized, with many critics believing his style was unsuited to the material or genre.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Zimberoff |first=Tom |date=2022-10-30 |title=Get Off My Set! |url=https://zimberoff.medium.com/get-off-my-set-67d4396f67 |access-date=2023-01-25 |website=Medium |language=en |archive-date=January 25, 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230125202832/https://zimberoff.medium.com/get-off-my-set-67d4396f67 |url-status=live}}</ref> The cinematography and production design, which replaced the fantastical Oz settings of the stage version with gritty urban cityscapes, were likewise criticized.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Just how bad was the movie of 'The Wiz?' |url=https://www.baltimoresun.com/2010/10/11/just-how-bad-was-the-movie-of-the-wiz |access-date=2023-01-25 |website=Baltimore Sun |date=October 11, 2010 |archive-date=May 30, 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240530070545/https://www.baltimoresun.com/2010/10/11/just-how-bad-was-the-movie-of-the-wiz/ |url-status=live}}</ref> While some critics described ''The Wiz'' as "dark, washed out, and boring",<ref>{{Cite book |last=Adrahtas |first=Tom |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=McDPdRcU_dQC&q=Cohen |title=Diana Ross: The American Dream Girl : a Lifetime to Get Here |date=2006 |publisher=AuthorHouse |isbn=978-1-4259-7140-3 |language=en}}</ref> Lumet might have done this to reflect the urban setting and serious themes being presented. Critics also questioned the decision to cut large portions of the original score, in some cases substituting original compositions regarded as inferior.<ref name=":0" />

Positive reviews included Charles Ryweck of ''[[The Hollywood Reporter]]'' who called it "truly film magic", and Stephen Traiman of ''[[Billboard (magazine)|Billboard]]'' who declared: "''The Wiz'' is a wow!"<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=October 4, 1978|pages=28–29|title=It's Wows For The Wiz (advertisement)}}</ref>[[Gene Siskel]] of the ''[[Chicago Tribune]]'' and [[Roger Ebert]] of the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'' also gave the film some of its most best reviews on the syndicated TV show ''[[Sneak Previews]]''. Siskel called it "superior musical theater", said that Diana Ross was "superb", "terrific" and came across as "a real star", but he had reservations about the film's "heavy message". Ebert praised other cast members and numerous technical aspects of the film, saying that it was "fun" and in the "great tradition of the American musical".<ref name="sneakpreviews">{{cite AV media|url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iC6br7UPw7Y |archive-url=https://ghostarchive.org/varchive/youtube/20211214/iC6br7UPw7Y |archive-date=2021-12-14 |url-status=live|title=The Wiz (1978) movie review - Sneak Previews with Roger Ebert and Gene Siskel|date=May 15, 2019|access-date=February 22, 2021|via=YouTube}}{{cbignore}}</ref> Ebert's print review in ''The Chicago Sun Times'' gave the film 3 stars out of a possible 4, describing ''The Wiz'' as filled with "great moments and a lot of life, sensational special effects and costumes" and good performances from the main cast apart from Ted Ross, who "sort of disappears into his lion’s costume" and was difficult for viewers to connect with. Despite the overall positive review, Ebert felt the film was less engaging than ''The Wizard of Oz'' because some of the songs were too saccharine and "'The Wiz' has just a touch too much calculation" in trying to appeal to a broad audience rather than simply telling the story.<ref name="Ebert">{{Cite web|url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/the-wiz-1978|title=The Wiz movie review & film summary|website=Rogerebert.com|access-date=June 12, 2026}}</ref>

Jackson's performance as the Scarecrow was one of the few universally praised elements, with critics saying he possessed "genuine acting talent" and "provided the only genuinely memorable moments",<ref name="campbell" /><ref name="dineen">{{cite book | last =Jackson | first =Michael | author-link =Michael Jackson |author2=Catherine Dineen | title =Michael Jackson: In His Own Words | publisher =Omnibus Press | year =1993 | isbn =0-7119-3216-6 | page =4 }}</ref> while Ebert said Jackson "fills the role with humor and warmth".<ref name="Ebert"/> Of the results of the film, Jackson stated, "I don't think it could have been any better, I really don't."<ref>{{cite book | last =Crouse | first =Richard | title = Big Bang Baby: The Rock and Roll Trivia Book | publisher =Dundurn Press Ltd. | year =2000 | pages =158–159 | isbn =0-88882-219-7 }}</ref> In 1980, Jackson stated his time working on ''The Wiz'' was "my greatest experience so far... I'll never forget that."<ref name="dineen" />

The film received another positive critique for its elaborate set design in the book ''American Jewish Filmmakers'', which noted that it "features some of the most imaginative adaptations of New York locales since the glory days of the Astaire-Rogers films."<ref name="desser">{{cite book | last = Desser | first =David |author2=Lester D. Friedman | author-link = David Desser|title =American Jewish Filmmakers | publisher =University of Illinois Press | year =2004 | isbn = 0-252-07153-0 | page =198}}</ref>

In a 2004 review of the film, [[Christopher Null]] wrote positively of Ted Ross's and Richard Pryor's performances.<ref name="filmcritic.com">{{cite news | last =Null | first =Christopher | title =The Wiz Movie Review, DVD Release | work =[[Filmcritic.com]] | publisher =[[Christopher Null]] | year =2004 | url =http://www.filmcritic.com/misc/emporium.nsf/reviews/The-Wiz | archive-url =https://archive.today/20130123014847/http://www.filmcritic.com/misc/emporium.nsf/reviews/The-Wiz | url-status =dead | archive-date =2013-01-23 | access-date =2007-11-06 }}</ref> However, Null's overall review of the film was critical, and he wrote that, other than the song "[[Ease On down the Road]]", "the rest is an [[LSD|acid]] trip of bad dancing, garish sets, and a Joel Schumacher-scripted mess that runs 135 agonizing minutes."<ref name="filmcritic.com" />

A 2005 piece by [[Hank Stuever]] in ''[[The Washington Post]]'' described the film as "a rather appreciable delight, even when it's a mess", and felt that the singing—especially Diana Ross's—was "a marvel".<ref>{{cite news | last =Stuever | first =Hank | title =Michael Jackson on Film: No Fizz After 'The Wiz' | newspaper =[[The Washington Post]] | page =N01 | date =January 30, 2005 | url =https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-dyn/articles/A44409-2005Jan28.html | access-date =2007-11-05 | archive-date =January 22, 2021 | archive-url =https://web.archive.org/web/20210122083256/https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-dyn/articles/A44409-2005Jan28.html | url-status =live }}</ref>

''[[The New York Times]]'' analyzed the film within a discussion of the genre of blaxploitation: "As the audience for blaxploitation dwindled, it seemed as if ''[[Car Wash (film)|Car Wash]]'' and ''The Wiz'' might be the last gasp of what had been a steadily expanding black presence in mainstream filmmaking."<ref>{{cite news| last = Harvey | first = Doug | title = December 24–30 – Who's the Man? Shaft, John Shaft | work = [[The New York Times]] | page = 2 | date = December 31, 2000}}</ref>

The ''[[St. Petersburg Times]]'' noted, "Of course, it only took one flop like ''The Wiz'' (1978) to give Hollywood an excuse to retreat to safer (i.e., whiter) creative ground until [[John Singleton]] and [[Spike Lee]] came along. Yet, without blaxploitation there might not have been another generation of black filmmakers, no [[Denzel Washington]] or [[Angela Bassett]], or they might have taken longer to emerge."<ref>{{cite news| last = Persall | first = Steve | title = The Return of Shaft: Bullets babes bad muthas and blaxploitation | work = [[St. Petersburg Times]] | page = 22W | date =June 16, 2000}}</ref>

''[[The Boston Globe]]'' commented, "The term 'black film' should be struck from the critical vocabulary. To appreciate just how outmoded, deceptive and limiting it is, consider the following, all of which have been described as black films," and included ''The Wiz'' in a list of disparate genre films as ''[[Shaft (1971 film)|Shaft]]'' (1971), ''[[Blacula]]'' (1972) and ''[[Super Fly (1972 film)|Super Fly]]'' (1972).<ref>{{cite news| last = Blowen | first = Michael | title = Abolish term 'black films' | work = [[The Boston Globe]] | publisher = Globe Newspaper Company | page = B1 | date = January 11, 1987}}</ref>

Despite its lack of critical or commercial success from its original release, ''The Wiz'' became a [[cult classic]],<ref name="cultclassic1">{{cite news | last = Han | first = Angie | title = NBC Teaming With Cirque du Soleil for 'The Wiz' Live Musical | work = [[Slashfilm]] | page = N01 | publisher = [[Slashfilm]] | date = March 31, 2015 | url = https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-dyn/articles/A44409-2005Jan28.html | access-date = 2015-04-06 | archive-date = January 22, 2021 | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20210122083256/https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-dyn/articles/A44409-2005Jan28.html | url-status = live }}</ref> especially because it features Michael Jackson in his first starring theatrical film role.<ref name="cultclassic2">{{cite news | last = Howard | first = Adam | title = How Lumet's 'The Wiz' became a black cult classic | work = [[The Grio]] | publisher = [[The Grio]] | date = April 11, 2011 | url = http://thegrio.com/2011/04/11/how-lumets-the-wiz-became-a-black-cult-classic/ | access-date = 2025-10-12 | archive-date = September 3, 2023 | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20230903170223/https://thegrio.com/2011/04/11/how-lumets-the-wiz-became-a-black-cult-classic/ | url-status = live }}</ref>

=== ''The Wiz'' and the black experience in America ===
''[[Essence (magazine)|Essence]],'' a magazine written for African American women, describes ''The Wiz'' as a portrayal of the black experience in America. Sharon Stockard Martin describes the film as having undertones of slavery, and highlights this through various plot points. Martin states that "it is the story of innocence taken by an uncompromising force and deposited in the midst of a strange and beautiful but evil land. Sound familiar?"<ref name=":03">{{cite magazine |id={{ProQuest|1818432160}} |last1=Martin |first1=Sharon Stockard |title=THE TANNING OF OZ: REFLECTIONS ON THE USURPATION OF A MYTH |magazine=Essence |volume=6 |issue=5 |date=September 1975 |pages=32, 35 }}</ref> Martin describes how ''The Wiz'' is based on African enslavement and reflects the forced transplantation of individuals from their homes into a new and unfamiliar world. Additionally, Martin identifies the freeing of the Winkies to be a large plot point that contributes to the themes of slavery in ''The Wiz''.<ref name=":03"/> The Wicked Witch is seen as an oppressive figure who enslaves the Winkies. This can be representative of the systems and individuals who upheld slavery and segregation.

Martin also analyzes the characters in a perspective that reflects the black experience in America. She describes Dorothy in the [[Judy Garland]] version as an "escapist", but contrasts her with Dorothy in ''The Wiz''. For Dorothy in ''The Wiz'', she equates her home with love and "... she never wished to leave it".<ref name=":03"/>

Martin views Dorothy's male co-stars as various behavior mechanisms displayed by black men as the result of slavery and racism. Martin describes the Scarecrow as "the result of complete undoing", and compares him to a "slave stripped of his past and made into the American Negro".<ref name=":03" /> In other words, he represents the idea that black individuals have been characterized to believe in their own intellectual inadequacy. Martin explains the Tin Man as an individual who "develops a hard exterior to protect soft feelings from the inevitable hurt of a hostile environment".<ref name=":03" /> In this perspective, the Tin Man is a man who has been forced to travel through the world with strength and intensity. The Tin Man embodies the idea that black men must be strong to survive, an idea that originated from slavery. Lastly, Martin views the Cowardly Lion as a self-defeating man<ref name=":03" /> who is paralyzed by fear and internalized narratives that discourage action.

The show and film were described as a "beloved cultural touchstone for many generations of black people."<ref>{{cite web |title=What 'The Wiz' Was And Is to black Culture {{!}} Notes from America with Kai Wright |url=https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/anxiety/articles/what-the-wiz-was-and-is-to-black-culture |website=WNYC Studios |language=en}}</ref>
