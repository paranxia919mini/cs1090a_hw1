'''''The Lighthouse''''' is a 2019<!-- Please do not add a genre to the opening sentence. See the talk page of this article for more information--> film directed and produced by [[Robert Eggers]], from a screenplay he wrote with his brother Max Eggers. It stars [[Willem Dafoe]] and [[Robert Pattinson]] as turmoiled nineteenth-century [[lighthouse keeper]]s stranded at a remote [[New England]] outpost by a violent storm. The film has defied [[Film genre|genre]] categorization in media, leading to multiple interpretations from critics.

The idea of ''The Lighthouse'' originated from a script based on [[Edgar Allan Poe]]'s incomplete short story "[[The Light-House]]", which was written by Max. Eggers researched for source material and developed the plot from [[Smalls Lighthouse#Smalls Lighthouse Tragedy|a nineteenth-century legend of an accident at a Welsh lighthouse]]. ''The Lighthouse'' draws visually from photography of 1890s New England, maritime-themed French films from the 1930s, and [[Symbolism (movement)|symbolist]] art. [[Principal photography]] took place in [[Nova Scotia]], Canada, beginning in April 2018 and lasting slightly over a month. It was shot in [[black-and-white]], with a nearly-square [[Aspect ratio (image)#Previous and currently used aspect ratios|1.19:1 aspect ratio]] to express the lighthouse's vertical orientation and isolation of the characters. [[Mark Korven]] developed the film's score, evoking the sounds and [[the sea in culture|mythologies of the sea]].

The film premiered at the [[2019 Cannes Film Festival|Cannes Film Festival]] on May 19, 2019, and was theatrically released in the United States by [[A24]] on October 18, 2019. It grossed over $18 million, against an $11 million budget, and received critical acclaim, with particular praise for the direction, visuals, and performances of Dafoe and Pattinson. Among its numerous accolades, the film was nominated for Best Cinematography at the [[92nd Academy Awards]] and the [[73rd British Academy Film Awards]].

==Plot==
<!-- Per WP:FILMPLOT, plot summaries for feature films should be set between 400 to 700 words only. -->
In the 1890s, Ephraim Winslow begins a four-week stint as a "wickie" ([[lighthouse keeper]]) on an isolated island off the coast of New England, under the supervision of former sailor Thomas Wake. In his quarters, Winslow discovers a small [[scrimshaw]] of a [[mermaid]] and keeps it in his jacket. Wake immediately proves to be very demanding, subjecting Winslow to taxing jobs such as emptying [[chamber pot]]s, maintaining the machinery, carrying heavy [[kerosene]] tanks up the stairs, and painting the lighthouse, while barring Winslow from the lantern room. Winslow observes that, every night after ascending the lighthouse, Wake disrobes before the light. During his stay on the island, Winslow begins to hallucinate [[sea monster]]s and logs floating in the sea, and [[masturbation|masturbates]] to the mermaid on the scrimshaw. Winslow is bothered by a one-eyed [[gull]], but Wake warns him against killing it under the [[Sailors' superstitions|superstitious]] belief that gulls are reincarnated sailors. One evening while dining, Wake reveals to Winslow that his previous wickie died after losing his sanity, while Winslow reveals that he is a former [[Lumberjack|timberman]] from [[Maine]] who was stationed in Canada and is now seeking a new trade.

The day before the scheduled departure, Winslow discovers a dead gull inside the [[cistern]], bloodying the drinking water. He is attacked by the one-eyed gull and brutally bludgeons it to death. The wind drastically changes direction and a fierce storm hits the island. Winslow and Wake spend the night getting drunk, and the storm prevents the [[lighthouse tender]] from collecting them the next day. As Winslow empties the chamber pots, he discovers the beached body of a mermaid, which waves and howls at him. He flees back to the cottage, where Wake informs him the storm has spoiled their rations. Winslow is not worried because he thinks the tender is only a day late, but Wake says that they have already been stranded for weeks. The pair unearths a crate at the lighthouse's base that Winslow assumes contains reserve rations, but it is full of bottles of alcohol.

As the storm continues to rage, Winslow and Wake get drunk every night and alternate between moments of intimacy and hostility. One night, Winslow tries unsuccessfully to steal the lantern room keys from Wake and contemplates murdering him. Winslow later sees the one-eyed head of Wake's previous wickie in a [[lobster trap]]. While drunk, Winslow confesses to Wake that his real name is Thomas Howard, and he assumed the identity of Ephraim Winslow, his cruel foreman in Canada whom he deliberately allowed to drown during a [[log driving|log drive]]. Howard has a menacing vision of Wake accusing Howard of "spilling [his] beans" and runs to the [[Dory (boat)|dory]] to try to leave the island, but Wake appears and destroys the boat with an axe. After chasing Howard back to their lodgings, Wake claims it was Howard who chased him and hacked up the dory, as Howard was driven mad by his confession.

Howard and Wake run out of alcohol and turn to drinking sweetened [[turpentine]]. That night, a giant wave crashes through the wall of their cottage and floods it. In the morning, Howard finds Wake's logbook, in which Wake has criticized him as a drunken and incompetent employee and recommended he be fired without pay. The two men argue, and Howard attacks Wake while hallucinating the mermaid, the real Winslow, and Wake as a [[Proteus]]-like figure. Howard beats Wake into submission and takes him to the hole at the base of the lighthouse to [[Premature burial|bury him alive]]. Before losing consciousness, Wake describes a "[[Prometheus|Promethean]]" punishment that awaits those who look in the lantern, and Howard takes the keys to the lantern room.

Howard goes to get a cigarette, and Wake returns and strikes him with the axe. Howard disarms and kills Wake before ascending the lighthouse. In the lantern room, the [[Fresnel lens]] opens to Howard, who reaches in and violently screams in distortion before falling down the lighthouse steps. Later, a barely-alive Howard lies nude on the rocks with a damaged eye as a flock of gulls peck at his exposed organs.

==Production==
===Development===
[[File:Robert Eggers - The Witch,Fantastic Fest 2015-1667 (28894993650) (cropped).jpg |thumb|upright|alt=|Robert Eggers in 2015]]

The original idea for ''The Lighthouse'' was first articulated at a dinner between director [[Robert Eggers]] and his younger brother, Max Eggers. Eggers was unhappy with his film industry prospects after the pitching of his first major feature, ''[[The Witch (2015 film)|The Witch]]'' (2015), failed to secure funding.<ref name=RS1025>{{cite magazine |url=https://www.rollingstone.com/movies/movie-features/robert-eggers-the-lighthouse-interview-898545/ |title=Drunken Sailors and Movie Stars: Robert Eggers on Making 'The Lighthouse' |magazine=[[Rolling Stone]] |url-access=limited |first=David |last=Fear |date=October 25, 2019 |access-date=November 17, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191028235947/https://www.rollingstone.com/movies/movie-features/robert-eggers-the-lighthouse-interview-898545/ |archive-date=October 28, 2019 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url= https://deadline.com/2019/10/the-lighthouse-robert-eggers-willem-dafoe-robert-pattinson-contenders-london-1202753044/ |title= How Robert Eggers Built 'The Lighthouse' – The Contenders London |website=[[Deadline Hollywood]] |first=Anthony |last=D'Alessandro |date=October 5, 2019 |access-date=February 3, 2021|archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20210203204340/https://deadline.com/2019/10/the-lighthouse-robert-eggers-willem-dafoe-robert-pattinson-contenders-london-1202753044/ |archive-date=February 3, 2021|url-status=live}}</ref> Max shared the basic outline of his screenplay, a lighthouse-set ghost tale tentatively titled ''Burnt Island'', based on his interpretation of [[Edgar Allan Poe]]'s unfinished short story "[[The Light-House]]".<ref name=RS1025/> Max struggled with his treatment to the extent that progress stalled.<ref name=RS1025/> Eggers began brainstorming ideas and, with his brother's support, soon began investigating for source material.<ref name=RS1025/>

One story that caught the director's attention in his research was a nineteenth-century myth of an accident at [[Smalls Lighthouse]] in [[Wales]], wherein one of two wickies, both named Thomas, died while trapped at the outpost by a destructive storm. That both men were named Thomas, Eggers recalled, persuaded him to create a film with an underlying story of identity.<ref name="Vox">{{cite web |last=Wilkinson |first=Alissa |date=October 15, 2019 |title=The Witch director Robert Eggers spills his beans about The Lighthouse |url=https://www.vox.com/culture/2019/10/15/20914097/robert-eggers-lighthouse-interview-witch |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210202015136/https://www.vox.com/culture/2019/10/15/20914097/robert-eggers-lighthouse-interview-witch |archive-date=February 2, 2021 |access-date=February 1, 2021 |website=[[Vox (website)|Vox]]}}</ref> However, Eggers stopped working on ''The Lighthouse'' when he found an investor to finance ''The Witch''.<ref name=RS1025/>

The unexpected success of ''The Witch'' elevated Eggers's directing profile. To exploit his newfound credibility, he pushed ''The Lighthouse'', among several other projects, in his negotiations with studio executives.<ref name=RS1025/> He and Max then resumed their work by exchanging and revising drafts. This coincided with more rigorous research of the period to develop the onscreen world; Eggers sought out photos of 1890s New England, 1930s maritime-themed French films, and [[Symbolism (movement)|symbolist]] art for visual reference.<ref name=RS1025/><ref name=Vox/>

The Eggers' study of literature with maritime and [[Surrealism|surrealist]] themes influenced the film's dialogue.<ref name=slate/> They looked into the writings of [[Herman Melville]], [[Robert Louis Stevenson]], and [[H. P. Lovecraft]], among others, before coming across the work of [[Sarah Orne Jewett]], a novelist best known for her [[American literary regionalism|local color works]] set in coastal Maine. Her dialect-heavy writing style provided the cadences of the lead characters, rooted in the experiences of her novel characters and real-life farmers, fishermen, and captains she had interviewed.<ref name=RS1025/><ref name="slate">{{cite web |last=Bloomer |first=Jeffrey |date=October 22, 2019 |title=The Director of The Lighthouse Spills a Few Beans About His Movie's Puzzling Ending |url=https://slate.com/culture/2019/10/the-lighthouse-ending-meaning-director-robert-eggers-interview.html |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210206002443/https://slate.com/culture/2019/10/the-lighthouse-ending-meaning-director-robert-eggers-interview.html |archive-date=February 6, 2021 |access-date=February 4, 2021 |work=[[Slate (magazine)|Slate]]}}</ref> The Eggers also referred to a dissertation on Jewett's use of dialects as guidance for more dramatic conversational scenes.<ref name=RS1025/><ref name=slate/>

Another creative force shaping ''The Lighthouse'' was the Eggers' theater background. The two men drew from playwrights that influenced their work as young teens, chiefly artists such as [[Samuel Beckett]], [[Harold Pinter]], and [[Sam Shepard]], whose writings examine male-centric perspectives of existential crises and psychosis.<ref name=RS1025/>

The film stars [[Willem Dafoe]] and [[Robert Pattinson]], who approached Eggers independently.<ref name="latimes">{{Cite news |last=Whipp |first=Glenn |date=October 24, 2019 |title=Robert Pattinson, Willem Dafoe and the 'orgasm that won't stop' in 'The Lighthouse' |work=[[Los Angeles Times]] |url=https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/movies/story/2019-10-24/lighthouse-explained-robert-pattinson-willem-dafoe |url-status=live |url-access=limited |access-date=March 14, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210206100256/https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/movies/story/2019-10-24/lighthouse-explained-robert-pattinson-willem-dafoe |archive-date=February 6, 2021}}</ref> Pattinson originally met Eggers to discuss a proposal to portray a [[Victorian era|Victorian]] socialite in an unrelated project. This was rejected by Pattinson as he believed the role would fail to challenge his acting ability.<ref name=RS1025/> His next meeting with Eggers took place once he finished reading ''The Lighthouse''{{'s}} completed script. According to Eggers, during their conversation, Pattinson showed him a clip of an intoxicated man screaming "I am a demon" to express his understanding of the expectations of his role.<ref name=RS1025/>

Eggers's initial film proposals with Dafoe were also not fruitful.<ref name=RS1025/><ref name=Dl2>{{cite web |url= https://deadline.com/2019/12/the-lighthouse-robert-pattinson-willem-dafoe-robert-eggers-the-partnership-interview-news-1202810915/ |title= The Partnership: Robert Pattinson & Willem Dafoe On Their Tense Two-Hander, Contrasting Styles & Appreciation For Robert Eggers |website=[[Deadline Hollywood]] |first=Joe |last=Utichi |date=December 23, 2019|access-date=February 8, 2021|archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20210208184655/https://deadline.com/2019/12/the-lighthouse-robert-pattinson-willem-dafoe-robert-eggers-the-partnership-interview-news-1202810915/ |archive-date=February 8, 2021|url-status=live}}</ref> The director used Pattinson's participation in ''The Lighthouse'' to convince Dafoe because they already had a rapport.<ref name=Dl2/> When they met in person to discuss the project, Dafoe recalled that Eggers was plainspoken in the conversation: "There was no discussion. 'This is the way we're going to do this. My way or the highway.' That's very unusual, especially for a [[two-hander]], for a director to say, 'This is the way I see it. Yes or no?{{Single+double}}<ref name=Dl2/>

''The Lighthouse'' is director of photography [[Jarin Blaschke]]'s third collaboration with Eggers.<ref name=asc/><ref name=LATimes1112>{{cite web |url=https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/movies/story/2019-11-12/crafts-lighthouse |title=Atmosphere infuses the 'particularly photographic' black-and-white 'Lighthouse' |website=[[Los Angeles Times]] |first=Daron |last=James |date=November 12, 2019 |access-date=November 18, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191117110143/https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/movies/story/2019-11-12/crafts-lighthouse |archive-date=November 17, 2019 |url-status=live |url-access=limited}}</ref> Eggers wanted to film in [[black-and-white]] with a boxy [[aspect ratio]].<ref name=RS1025/><ref name=kodak>{{cite web |url= https://www.kodak.com/en/motion/blog-post/robert-eggers-the-lighthouse |title= Kodak B&W; film delivers a unique visual signature to Robert Eggers' acclaimed fantasy horror 'The Lighthouse' |publisher=[[Kodak]]|date=May 16, 2019|access-date=May 23, 2022|archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20211224041504/https://www.kodak.com/en/motion/blog-post/robert-eggers-the-lighthouse |archive-date=December 24, 2021|url-status=live}}</ref> They faced opposition from executives seeking a more widespread distribution in theaters, but felt shooting in color would undermine the film's artistic integrity.<ref name=asc/><ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.creativeplanetnetwork.com/news/lost-in-time-how-the-cinematography-of-the-lighthouse-evokes-the-19th-century|title=Lost in Time: How the Cinematography of "The Lighthouse" Evokes the 19th Century|date=December 3, 2019|first=Jennifer|last=Wolfe|website=Creative Planet Network|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210405180225/https://www.creativeplanetnetwork.com/news/lost-in-time-how-the-cinematography-of-the-lighthouse-evokes-the-19th-century|archive-date=April 5, 2021|url-status=live|access-date=April 5, 2021}}</ref> In pre-production, Eggers planned to shoot the film in 1.33:1 aspect ratio. He reconsidered when Blaschke suggested shooting in [[Aspect ratio (image)#Previous and currently used aspect ratios|1.19:1 aspect ratio]], which was used fleetingly during the film industry's transition to [[sound film|sound]], as a joke.<ref name=asc/><ref name=kodak/> They found that the footage better conveyed the lighthouse's vertical orientation, the isolation of the characters, and a sense of confinement when Winslow and Wake are together.<ref name=asc/> The filmmakers cited ''[[M (1931 film)|M]]'' (1931), ''[[Man of Aran]]'' (1934), ''[[Pickpocket (film)|Pickpocket]]'' (1959), ''[[In Cold Blood (film)|In Cold Blood]]'' (1967), and the works of [[Béla Tarr]] and [[Ingmar Bergman]] as significant influences on the film's visual hallmarks.<ref name=asc/><ref name=kodak/>

===Cinematography===

''The Lighthouse'' was shot on [[35 mm movie film|35&nbsp;mm film]] using [[Panavision cameras#Panaflex Millennium|Panavision Panaflex Millennium XL2]] cameras with vintage Bausch and Lomb Baltar lenses. Occasionally, to capture flashback sequences or scenes of heightened conflict, the camera crew shot with three [[Petzval lens]]es and another 50 mm lens that had been refurbished by [[Panavision]].<ref name=asc/> Filmmakers created the onscreen world with a stark visual palette reminiscent of [[Orthochromasia|orthochromatic]] film. One of Blaschke's initial responsibilities was to produce the shots with a sufficient antique quality. He developed a process of testing the utility of digital footage in color negative film stock, first with [[List of motion picture film stocks#Vision3 color negative (ECN-2 process 2007–present)|Kodak Vision3 500T 5219]] film and then [[Eastman Kodak|Eastman]] Double-X 5222 stock.<ref name=kodak/> Blaschke continued this process after acquiring the Baltar lenses for the shoot, this time with [[optical filter#Shortpass|shortpass filters]]—a class of scientific optical filters—and [[photographic filter]]s most sensitive to blue-green and ultraviolet light.<ref name=kodak/> The specifications were so unusual that it required the manufacture of custom sets of filters by [[Schneider Kreuznach]], which was expensive and time-consuming. Blaschke recalled, "I sketched a desired spectrograph on graph paper, indicating a complete elimination of all light beyond 570 nanometers [mid-yellow] while allowing all shorter wavelengths to pass freely. At that point, I was unsure of the true light loss and I was pretty nervous about it."<ref name=asc/> Post-production editing of ''The Lighthouse'' occurred simultaneously at the [[FotoKem]] film laboratories in [[Burbank, California]].<ref name=kodak/>

[[File:TheLighthouseLighting.jpg|thumb|upright=1.1|Screenshot of Wake and Winslow in a drunken argument at night. The filmmakers deployed artificial lighting for both day and nighttime scenes, in this case a single lantern equipped with a halogen bulb.]]

Lighting, especially for night scenes, posed a challenge for the camera crew because they were shooting at a low [[film speed]], which made film stock less sensitive to light.<ref name=kodak/><ref name=LATimes1112/> This was compounded by the fact that the Double-X 5222 stock had a substantially lower capacity for [[exposure latitude]] than either color film stock or digital film.<ref name=kodak/><ref name=varietylighting>{{cite magazine |last=Valentini |first=Valentina I. |date=October 17, 2019 |title=How Bright Bulbs Enabled ‘The Lighthouse’s’ Tough Black-and-White Shoot |url=https://variety.com/2019/artisans/spotlight/the-lighthouse-cinematography-black-and-white-1203373400/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250817121841/https://variety.com/2019/artisans/spotlight/the-lighthouse-cinematography-black-and-white-1203373400/ |archive-date=August 17, 2025 |access-date=August 7, 2026 |magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]}}</ref> The filmmakers therefore deployed artificial lights when scenes could not be sufficiently rendered with only natural lighting.<ref name=kodak/> They rigged exterior nighttime scenes with 9K and 18K [[hydrargyrum medium-arc iodide lamp|HMI]] lamps manufactured in Nova Scotia and Toronto; these lamps were also used for daytime shots, in a setup involving the reflection of light off of stretched [[muslin]] fixtures.<ref name=LATimes1112/><ref name=indielighting>{{cite web |last=O'Falt |first=Chris |date=January 31, 2020 |title=The Visual Language of ‘The Lighthouse’ Is So Much More Than Black-and-White |url=https://www.indiewire.com/awards/industry/the-lighthouse-best-cinematography-oscar-nominee-jarin-blaschke-robert-eggers-1202207495/|url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250312200506/https://www.indiewire.com/awards/industry/the-lighthouse-best-cinematography-oscar-nominee-jarin-blaschke-robert-eggers-1202207495/ |archive-date=March 12, 2025 |access-date=August 6, 2026 |website=[[IndieWire]]}}</ref> For the interior night scenes, sets were lit by a single [[lantern]] equipped with 500- and 800-[[watt]] [[halogen lamp|halogen bulbs]], occasionally supplemented by a [[paper lantern]] for close up shots.<ref name=LATimes1112/><ref name=varietylighting/> The lights, according to Blaschke, were so bright that some camera crew wore sunglasses on the set.<ref name=indielighting/>

===Genre===

===Psychoanalysis===
Eggers said the film's subtext was influenced by [[Sigmund Freud]] and he hoped that "it's a movie where both [[Carl Jung|Jung]] and Freud would be furiously eating their popcorn".<ref name="Joho-2019">{{Cite web|url=https://mashable.com/article/the-lighthouse-robert-eggers-meaning-explained-ending/|title=What the hell did 'The Lighthouse' even mean?|last=Joho|first=Jess|date=October 20, 2019|website=[[Mashable]]|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200329002700/https://mashable.com/article/the-lighthouse-robert-eggers-meaning-explained-ending/|archive-date=March 29, 2020|access-date=March 28, 2020}}</ref><ref name="Jacobs-2019">{{Cite news|last=Jacobs|first=Matthew|url=https://www.huffingtonpost.ca/entry/the-lighthouse-homoeroticism-robert-pattinson-willem-dafoe_n_5da9c888e4b0e71d65b801ae?ri18n=true|title='He Sort Of Wants A Daddy': Decoding The Homoeroticism In 'The Lighthouse'|date=October 20, 2019|work=[[Huffington Post]]|access-date=March 28, 2020|archive-date=June 25, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210625173607/https://www.huffingtonpost.ca/entry/the-lighthouse-homoeroticism-robert-pattinson-willem-dafoe_n_5da9c888e4b0e71d65b801ae?ri18n=true|url-status=live}}</ref> Given his simultaneous fear and admiration of the senior lighthouse keeper, the younger keeper displays an [[Oedipus complex|Oedipal]] fixation. Pattinson commented on the father–son dynamic in the film by stating that "I was pretty conscious of how I wanted the relationship to come across. In a lot of ways, he sort of wants a daddy" and that, as the film progresses, his character is increasingly "looking for Willem [Dafoe]'s validation" as both a boss and a father-figure.<ref name="Jacobs-2019" /><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dim9GE891gQ|title=Robert Pattinson & Robert Eggers Break Down a Scene from 'The Lighthouse'|last=Fandango All Access|date=October 29, 2019|via=Youtube|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200127193204/https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dim9GE891gQ|archive-date=January 27, 2020|access-date=March 28, 2020}}</ref> The film also echoes the [[Jungian archetypes|Jungian archetype]] of the [[Shadow (psychology)|shadow]], the unknown "dark side" or [[Bias blind spot|blind spot]] of one's personality. Dafoe illustrated that the two keepers are "put in this situation that's like a [[purgatory]] and then the little personality, the little sense of self that they've created for themselves starts to get stripped away. You see what their real nature is and that points them into a kind of desperation." Rosie Fletcher of ''[[Den of Geek]]'' gathered: "The way the pair embody wisdom and foolishness, hedonism and inspiration, honesty and trickery and play with masculine and feminine roles [...] seems to support the idea that one is the shadow of the other on some level and speaks further to Jung's theories."<ref name="Fletcher-2020">{{Cite web |last=Fletcher |first=Rosie |date=February 1, 2020 |title=The Lighthouse: the myths and archetypes behind the movie explained |url=https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/the-lighthouse-the-myths-and-archetypes-behind-the-movie-explained/ |access-date=June 18, 2023 |website=Den of Geek |language=en-US}}</ref>

===Mythology===
[[File:Albrecht Dürer - The Sea Monster - Google Art Project.jpg |thumb|left|upright|''[[The Sea Monster]]'' ({{circa}} 1493–1503) by [[Albrecht Dürer]]]]
In the film, the senior lighthouse keeper Thomas warns the younger keeper Ephraim of a maritime [[superstition]] that is bad luck to kill a seabird, specifically a [[seagull]]. However, after getting irritated by one, Ephraim kills it later and brings on a storm that traps the two men on the island. At the end of the film, Ephraim is seen on the ground with seagulls plucking out his organs. This plot invokes the 1798 poem "[[The Rime of the Ancient Mariner]]" by [[Samuel Taylor Coleridge]], in which a mariner kills an Albatross and brings disaster to his ship.<ref name="Fletcher-2020"/>

The fate of the younger lighthouse keeper also invokes the myth of [[Prometheus]], as, after finally reaching the light and learning what is in it, he falls down the stairs of the lighthouse and his organs are plucked out by seagulls. On the other hand, the older keeper was modeled on [[Proteus]], a "prophecy-telling ocean god who serves [[Poseidon]]", as he "makes that uncannily accurate prediction for how Ephraim will die at the end of the movie"<ref name="Joho-2019" /> and is even seen with tentacles and sea creatures stuck to his body in one of the younger man's hallucinations. [[Albrecht Dürer]]'s engraving ''[[The Sea Monster]]'' inspired Wake's appearance, with Eggers saying: "The Proteus figure that is more clearly nautical is somewhat based on a sea monster by Dürer, who carries a tortoise shell shield."<ref name="Han">{{Cite web|url=https://www.polygon.com/2019/10/23/20914085/the-lighthouse-robert-eggers-interview-ending-spoilers-mermaid-lamp-robert-pattinson|title=''The Lighthouse'' director took the movie's creepy mythology very seriously|last=Han|first=Karen|date=October 23, 2019|website=[[Polygon (website)|Polygon]]|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191129121816/https://www.polygon.com/2019/10/23/20914085/the-lighthouse-robert-eggers-interview-ending-spoilers-mermaid-lamp-robert-pattinson|archive-date=November 29, 2019|access-date=May 21, 2020}}</ref> Eggers explained the allusions to classical mythology by saying they are present "Partially because [[Herman Melville|Melville]] goes there and partially because of I'm sure our unhealthy Jungian leanings".<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.thrillist.com/entertainment/nation/the-lighthouse-movie-ending-explained-twist-robert-eggers|title=Director Robert Eggers Breaks Down the Mysteries of 'The Lighthouse'|last=Zuckerman|first=Esther|date=October 18, 2019|website=Thrillist|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200329002702/https://www.thrillist.com/entertainment/nation/the-lighthouse-movie-ending-explained-twist-robert-eggers|archive-date=March 29, 2020|access-date=March 28, 2020}}</ref>

===Sexuality===
[[File:Hypnose (Schneider).jpg|thumb|upright|right|''Hypnosis'' (1904) by [[Sascha Schneider]], which provided visual reference for a scene in the film]]
The film primarily depicts two men alone in close quarters on an island and contains explicit depictions of male sexuality and [[homoeroticism]], but, when asked whether the film was "a love story", Robert Eggers replied:
{{blockquote|Am I saying these characters are gay? No. I'm not saying they're not either. Forget about complexities of human sexuality or their particular inclinations. I'm more about questions than answers in this movie.<ref name="Jacobs-2019"/>}}

The [[Phallus|phallic]] imagery of the lighthouse is explicit, as Eggers described it in the script as an erect penis, revealing that the film was meant to include "a very juvenile shot of a lighthouse moving like an erect penis and a match-cut to actual erect penis" belonging to Howard, but this sequence was removed at the request of the financiers.<ref name="Jacobs-2019" /> A [[Double (occupation)|body double]] of Pattinson was used to film the scene. Pattinson said that when he first saw the shot, he didn't even realize what he was looking at, initially mistaking it for the lighthouse. He then joked in Sky News interview that the phallus might have belonged to director Robert Eggers.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Dazed |date=January 27, 2020 |title=Robert Pattinson on masturbation and the lonely eroticism of The Lighthouse |url=https://www.dazeddigital.com/film-tv/article/47574/1/robert-pattinson-willem-dafoe-robert-eggers-the-lighthouse-interview |access-date=March 29, 2023 |website=Dazed |language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news |last=Stern |first=Marlow |date=October 17, 2019 |title=The Most Bonkers Movie Sex Scene of the Year Features a Very Horny Robert Pattinson and a Mermaid |work=The Daily Beast |url=https://www.thedailybeast.com/the-lighthouse-robert-pattinson-gets-weird-in-the-most-bonkers-movie-sex-scene-of-the-year |url-status=live |access-date=March 28, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200509140036/https://www.thedailybeast.com/the-lighthouse-robert-pattinson-gets-weird-in-the-most-bonkers-movie-sex-scene-of-the-year |archive-date=May 9, 2020}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news |title=Robert Pattinson: 'I like perversity, I don't care what people think' |url=https://news.sky.com/story/robert-pattinson-i-like-perversity-i-dont-care-what-people-think-11909419 |archive-url=http://web.archive.org/web/20230402131240/https://news.sky.com/story/robert-pattinson-i-like-perversity-i-dont-care-what-people-think-11909419 |archive-date=2023-04-02 |access-date=2026-08-04 |work=Sky News |language=en-GB}}</ref>

Sexual fantasy and masturbation are also recurring themes in the film. For Dafoe, the [[Androphilia and gynephilia|androphilia]] in the film is blatant, but it is also used to explore what it means to be a man: "They have a sense of guilt, of wrong [...] it's got existential roots [...] about masculinity and domination and submission."<ref name="Jacobs-2019" /> After beating the older lighthouse keeper into submission, the younger keeper assumes a dominant role, calling the older man "dog" and dragging him on a leash. Commenting on this scene, Pattinson said "there's definitely a take where we were literally trying to pull each other's pants down. It literally almost looked like [[foreplay]]."<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2019/10/jojo-rabbit-the-lighthouse-gay-subtext-1202182957/|title=Why the Gay Subplots in 'The Lighthouse' and 'Jojo Rabbit' Don't Go Far Enough|last=Dry|first=Jude|date=October 21, 2019|website=IndieWire|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191205034734/https://www.indiewire.com/2019/10/jojo-rabbit-the-lighthouse-gay-subtext-1202182957/|archive-date=December 5, 2019|access-date=March 28, 2020}}</ref>

The mythological and artistic influences of the film underscore its eroticism. Eggers acknowledged the visual influence of [[Symbolism (movement)|symbolist]] artists [[Sascha Schneider]] and [[Jean Delville]], whose "mythic paintings in a homoerotic style become perfect candidates as imagery that's going to work itself into the script."<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.bafta.org/media-centre/transcripts/screenwriters-lecture-series-2019-robert-eggers|title=Screenwriters' Lecture Series 2019: Robert Eggers|last=Eggers|first=Robert|date=December 9, 2019|website=[[British Academy of Film and Television Arts|BAFTA.org]]|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191220173545/http://www.bafta.org/media-centre/transcripts/screenwriters-lecture-series-2019-robert-eggers|archive-date=December 20, 2019|access-date=May 21, 2020}}</ref> The composition of a shot in the film was consciously adapted from Schneider's ''Hypnosis''.<ref name="Han" /><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.vice.com/en/article/the-lighthouse-director-robert-eggers-on-making-the-most-bonkers-film-of-2019-with-robert-pattinson-and-willem-dafoe/|title='The Lighthouse' Director Robert Eggers on Making the Most Bonkers Film of 2019|last=Donaghey|first=River|date=October 15, 2019|website=[[Vice (magazine)|Vice]]|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191227064722/https://www.vice.com/en_us/article/a354yz/the-lighthouse-director-robert-eggers-on-making-the-most-bonkers-film-of-2019-with-robert-pattinson-and-willem-dafoe|archive-date=December 27, 2019|access-date=May 21, 2020}}</ref>

===Critical response===
On [[review aggregation]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film holds an approval rating of 90% based on 398 reviews, with an average score of 8/10. The site's consensus reads: "A gripping story brilliantly filmed and led by a pair of powerhouse performances, ''The Lighthouse'' further establishes Robert Eggers as a filmmaker of exceptional talent."<ref>{{Cite Rotten Tomatoes |id={{RT data|rtid|noprefix=y}} |type=m |title=The Lighthouse |access-date={{RT data|access date}} |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190919214703/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/the_lighthouse_2019 |archive-date=September 19, 2019 |url-status=live}}</ref> On [[Metacritic]], the film has a [[Weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average]] score of 83 out of 100 based on 52 critics, indicating "universal acclaim".<ref>{{Cite Metacritic |id=the-lighthouse-2019 |type=movie |title=The Lighthouse |access-date=April 21, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210421183135/https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-lighthouse-2019 |archive-date=April 21, 2021 |url-status=live}}</ref>

[[Owen Gleiberman]] of ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' called the film "darkly exciting" and "made with extraordinary skill," commenting that "the movie, building on ''[[The Witch (2015 film)|The Witch]]'', proves that Robert Eggers possesses something more than impeccable genre skill. He has the ability to lock you into the fever of what's happening onscreen."<ref name="varrev">{{cite magazine |url=https://variety.com/2019/film/reviews/the-lighthouse-review-robert-pattinson-willem-dafoe-1203220127/ |title=Cannes Film Review: Robert Pattinson and Willem Dafoe in 'The Lighthouse' |last=Gleiberman |first=Owen |author-link=Owen Gleiberman|date=May 19, 2019 |magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|access-date=May 27, 2019|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190522232123/https://variety.com/2019/film/reviews/the-lighthouse-review-robert-pattinson-willem-dafoe-1203220127/|archive-date=May 22, 2019}}</ref> [[Robbie Collin]] of ''[[The Daily Telegraph]]'' gave the film a perfect score, calling Dafoe's performance "astounding" and comparing Pattinson's to that of [[Daniel Day-Lewis]] in ''[[There Will Be Blood]]'', saying, "that's no comparison to make lightly, but everything about ''The Lighthouse'' lands with a crash. It's cinema to make your head and soul ring."<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.telegraph.co.uk/films/0/lighthouse-review-film-will-make-head-soul-ring/ |title=The Lighthouse, review: 'A film that will make your head and soul ring' |last=Collin |first=Robbie |author-link=Robbie Collin |date=May 19, 2019 |website=[[The Daily Telegraph]] |access-date=September 15, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190610224450/https://www.telegraph.co.uk/films/0/lighthouse-review-film-will-make-head-soul-ring/ |archive-date=June 10, 2019 |url-status=live|url-access=registration}}</ref> [[Peter Bradshaw]] of ''[[The Guardian]]'', in addition to praising the performances of Dafoe and Pattinson, also praised the screenplay, stating that the "script is barnacled with resemblances to [[Samuel Taylor Coleridge|Coleridge]], [[William Shakespeare|Shakespeare]], [[Herman Melville|Melville]] – and there's also some staggeringly cheeky black-comic riffs and gags and the two of them resemble no-one so much as [[Wilfrid Brambell]] and [[Harry H. Corbett]]: [[Steptoe and Son]] in hell."<ref>{{cite web |last1=Bradshaw |first1=Peter |author-link=Peter Bradshaw|title=The Lighthouse review – Robert Pattinson shines in sublime maritime nightmare |url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2019/may/19/the-lighthouse-review-robert-pattinson-shines-in-sublime-maritime-nightmare |work=[[The Guardian]] |date=May 19, 2019 |access-date=September 3, 2020 |archive-date=May 19, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190519140006/https://www.theguardian.com/film/2019/may/19/the-lighthouse-review-robert-pattinson-shines-in-sublime-maritime-nightmare |url-status=live }}</ref> [[Manohla Dargis]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' praised the character development, production design, acting, and themes,<ref name="NYT" /> and [[Michael Phillips (critic)|Michael Phillips]] of the ''[[Chicago Tribune]]'' gave the film three stars out of five, comparing it to ''[[The Odd Couple (film)|The Odd Couple]]'' (1968) and ''[[The Dumb Waiter]]'' (1957), and lauding the cinematography.<ref name="CTReview"/>
