'''''Earth vs. the Flying Saucers''''' ({{a.k.a.}} '''''Invasion of the Flying Saucers''''' and '''''Flying Saucers from Outer Space''''')<ref>[https://www.moriareviews.com/sciencefiction/earth-vs-the-flying-saucers-1956.htm "Earth vs. the Flying Saucers AKA Invasion of the Flying Saucers."] ''moria.co.'' Retrieved: January 6, 2015.</ref> is a 1956 American [[science-fiction film]] from [[Columbia Pictures]]. It was produced by [[Charles H. Schneer]], directed by [[Fred F. Sears]], and stars [[Hugh Marlowe]] and [[Joan Taylor]].<ref name="AFIsearch">{{Cite web |title=Earth vs. the Flying Saucers |url=https://catalog.afi.com/Film/51816-EARTH-VSTHEFLYINGSAUCERS |access-date=6 November 2024 |website=American Film Institute Catalog}}</ref> The [[stop-motion animation]] [[special effects]] were created by [[Ray Harryhausen]]. The storyline was suggested by the bestselling 1953 nonfiction book ''[[Flying Saucers from Outer Space]]'' by Maj. [[Donald Keyhoe]].<ref name="Jacobs">Jacobs [https://books.google.com/books?id=tkqW7YxEH1oC&pg=PA11 2008, pp. 11–16].</ref> The film was released as a [[double feature]] with ''[[The Werewolf (1956 film)|The Werewolf]]''.<ref>McGee, Mark Thomas; Robertson, R.J. (2013). "You Won't Believe Your Eyes". Bear Manor Media. {{ISBN|978-1-59393-273-2}}. Page 254</ref>

==Plot summary==
Scientist Dr. Russell Marvin and his new bride Carol are driving to work when a [[flying saucer]] appears overhead. With no proof of the encounter other than a tape recording of the ship's sound, Dr. Marvin is hesitant to notify his superiors. He is in charge of Project Skyhook, an American space program that has already launched 10 research satellites into orbit. General Hanley, Carol's father, informs Marvin that many of the satellites have since fallen back to Earth. Marvin admits that he has lost contact with all of them and privately suspects [[extraterrestrials in fiction|alien]] involvement. The Marvins then witness the 11th falling from the sky shortly after launch.

When a saucer lands at the Skyhook facility the next day in front of the main building, a group of aliens in metallic suits exits<!-- group is singular --> with one alien walking peacefully into the entrance of the building, while being fired upon by the infantry guards, killing the one alien, while the remaining aliens and saucer are protected by a [[Force field (fiction)|force field]]. After retrieving the body of the dead alien, the saucer lifts off, returning the fire and spreading destruction across the facility, and killing everyone but the Marvins, who were trapped below ground. General Hanley is captured and taken away in the saucer. Too late, Russell discovers and decodes a message on his tape recorder. The aliens just wanted to meet with Dr. Marvin and for that purpose landed at Skyhook, where they were met with violence. ("Shoot first, ask questions later.")

Marvin contacts the aliens by radio and sneaks away to meet them, followed closely by Carol and Major Huglin. A pursuing motorcycle patrol officer and they are taken aboard a saucer, where the aliens have extracted knowledge directly from the general's brain. The aliens explain that they are the last of their species, having fled from their destroyed star system. They are extremely aged and are kept alive only by their protective garments. They have shot down all the launched satellites, fearing them as weapons. As proof of their power, the aliens give Dr. Marvin the coordinates of a naval destroyer that opened fire on them, and which they have since destroyed. Horrified by the cold, unempathetic nature of the aliens, Carol begins to break down, and the patrol officer, despite an attempt by Marvin to stop him, pulls his revolver and fires on the aliens; the aliens subject him to the same mind-control process as General Hanley. The aliens state that they will eventually return Hanley and the patrol officer. As the interaction continues, Carol becomes increasingly irrational, while Dr. Marvin tries to remain calm. Major Huglin and the Marvins are released with the message that the aliens want to meet with the world's leaders in 56 days in [[Washington, DC]], to negotiate an occupation of Earth.

Dr. Marvin's later observations lead to the discovery that the aliens' protective suits are made of solidified electricity and grant them enhanced [[hearing|auditory perception]]. Marvin develops a counter-weapon against their flying saucers, which he later successfully tests against a single saucer. As they escape, the aliens jettison Hanley and the patrol officer, who fall to their deaths. Groups of alien saucers then attack Washington, [[Paris]], [[London]], and [[Moscow]], and resistance by conventional weapons is futile, but they are destroyed by Dr. Marvin's sonic weapon. The defenders also discover that the aliens can be easily killed by [[Firearm#Types|small-arms]] gunfire when they are outside the force fields of their saucers. Upon repelling the attack and no further threats anticipated, Project Skyhook is re-established with Dr. Marvin once again placed in charge.

==Production==
===Visual effects===

[[Ray Harryhausen]] used stop-motion animation to create the scenes of the flying spacecraft. For increased realism in the scenes depicting saucers crashing into monuments and government buildings, he also animated falling masonry.<ref>{{cite web |last=Dixon |first=Wheeler Winston |authorlink=Wheeler Winston Dixon |work=Frame by Frame blog |url=http://blog.unl.edu/dixon/2012/03/12/earth-vs-the-flying-saucers/ |title=Earth vs. the Flying Saucers |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161019060517/http://blog.unl.edu/dixon |archive-date=2016-10-19}} University of Nebraska–Lincoln, 2015. Retrieved: January 6, 2015.</ref> Some animation of figures was used to show the aliens emerging from the saucers. Much [[stock footage]] was used, including the shots, during the invasion, of a missile launch and of batteries of [[90 mm gun M1/M2/M3|90 mm M3 guns]] firing. Film of the [[HMS Barham (04)#Sinking|destruction of HMS ''Barham'']] during [[World War II]] was used to depict the sinking of a [[destroyer]]. Scenes showing satellite launches use footage of a [[Viking (rocket)|Viking rocket]] takeoff and a failed [[V-2 rocket]] launch.<ref name="Wood">{{cite web |last=Wood |first=Bret |authorlink=Bret Wood |url=https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/16718/earth-vs-the-flying-saucers#articles-reviews?articleId=28072 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20111023002327/http://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/16718/Earth-Vs-The-Flying-Saucers/#articles-reviews?articleId=28072 |url-status=dead |archive-date=October 23, 2011 |title=Earth vs. the Flying Saucers |website=Turner Classic Movies |access-date=January 6, 2015}}.</ref> A scene depicting planes crashing after being hit by an alien ray uses film of [[list of accidents and incidents involving military aircraft (1943–1944)#Spokane 1944|a 1944 accident]] at an airshow near [[Spokane, Washington]], involving military aircraft.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.check-six.com/lib/Movie_Crash.htm |title=Movie Crash - Earth vs. The Flying Saucers}}</ref>

The film's iconic flying saucer design—a stationary central cabin encircled by a rotating outer ring with slotted vanes in it—matches purported eyewitness descriptions recorded by Maj. Donald Keyhoe in his best-selling nonfiction book about UFOs. At a tribute to Harryhausen held in [[Sydney]], Australia, the animator said that he consulted with well-known 1950s UFO "[[contactee]]" [[George Adamski]] about the depiction of the flying saucers in the film. He noted that Adamski had become quite paranoid by that time.<ref>Hankin [https://books.google.com/books?id=1voGbBwpB9sC&q=George%20Adamski%20Harryhausen 2008, p. 146.]</ref>

The voice of the aliens was produced by recording [[Paul Frees]]—in an uncredited role—on [[reel-to-reel audio tape recording|reel-to-reel audio tape]]. The speed control was then rapidly turned up and down by hand while the tape was played back, which had the effect of causing Frees's voice to waver in pitch and speed.<ref>Hagerty and Rogers 2008, p. 204.</ref>

==Reception==

[[File:Encina Drive-in Ad - 19 August 1956, CA.jpg|thumb|150px|right|[[Drive-in theater|Drive-in]] advertisement from 1956 for ''Earth vs. the Flying Saucers'' and co-feature, ''[[The Werewolf (1956 film)|The Werewolf]]'']]
A reviewer for ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' commented that the special effects were the real stars of ''Earth vs. the Flying Saucers'': "This exploitation program does a satisfactory job of entertaining in the science-fiction class. The technical effects created by Ray Harryhausen come off excellently in the Charles H. Schneer production, adding the required out-of-this-world visual touch to the screenplay, taken from a screen story by Curt Siodmak, suggested by Major Donald E. Keyhoe's ''Flying Saucers from Outer Space''."<ref>[https://variety.com/1955/film/reviews/earth-vs-the-flying-saucers-1200418071/ "Earth vs. the Flying Saucers" (abridged online version)], ''Variety'', December 31, 1955, p. 6, 16.</ref><ref>[https://archive.org/details/variety203-1956-06/page/n5/mode/1up "Earth vs. the Flying Saucers" (original scan)] ''Variety'', December 31, 1955, p. 6, 16.</ref> The ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' was also fairly positive, writing that although the saucers "look like art department concoctions", the film still "has a sort of pseudoscientific charm".<ref name="la times" /> The ''[[Monthly Film Bulletin]]'' gave a negative review, stating that the "semidocumentary technique" was "pretentious" and the use of stock footage, "crude model-work", and most of the best-known science-fiction clichés made the movie "a long-winded and rather bleak invasion from outer space".<ref name="mfb">{{cite magazine |magazine=[[Monthly Film Bulletin]] |publisher=[[British Film Institute]] |title=Earth vs. the Flying Saucers |volume=24 |issue=276 |page=70 |year=1957}}</ref> [[Bosley Crowther]] of ''[[the New York Times]]'' was also negative, calling it "utter nonsense that is childishly and humorlessly put forth".<ref>{{cite journal |last=Crowther |first=Bosley |date=August 5, 1956 |title=A Blessed Relief |journal=[[The New York Times]] |page=X1}}</ref>

''Earth vs. the Flying Saucers'' spawned a subgenre of subsequent flying-saucer films, many of which incorporated elements contributed by Harryhausen to the seminal movie.<ref name="Wood" /> In an article for ''the New York Times'', film reviewer [[Hal Erickson (author)|Hal Erickson]] noted, "Anyone who's seen the 1996 science-fiction lampoon ''[[Mars Attacks!]]'' may have trouble watching ''Earth vs. the Flying Saucers'' with a straight face". The later film could be seen as a campy homage to the era, especially the innovations of ''Earth vs. the Flying Saucers''.<ref>Erickson, Hal. [https://web.archive.org/web/20150721042805/http://www.nytimes.com/movies/movie/15130/Earth-vs-the-Flying-Saucers/overview "Overview: Earth vs. the Flying Saucers."] ''The New York Times''. Retrieved: January 6, 2015.</ref>

==Legacy==
The four-issue comic book miniseries ''[[TidalWave Productions#Flying Saucers vs. the Earth|Flying Saucers vs. the Earth]]'' (2008), released by [[TidalWave Productions]] as part of their [[TidalWave Productions#Ray Harryhausen Signature Series|Ray Harryhausen Signature Series]], reimagines the events of the film from the perspective of the alien invaders, identified in the comics as the Sons of Aberrann.<ref name="CBR0801">{{cite web |title=Ray Harryhausen's "Flying Saucers VS The Earth" #1 sells out |url=https://www.cbr.com/ray-harryhausens-flying-saucers-vs-the-earth-1-sells-out/ |work=[[CBR.com]] |date=2008-05-16 |access-date=2017-09-24}}</ref> A preview of the first issue was included on the 50th-anniversary DVD release of the film.<ref name="IGN0701">{{cite web |title=Ray Harryhausen's Collection |url=https://www.ign.com/articles/2007/09/24/ray-harryhausens-collection |work=[[IGN]] |date=2007-09-24 |access-date=2017-09-24}}</ref>

The film is partially featured in the ''[[Breaking Bad]]'' episode "[[Cancer Man (Breaking Bad)|Cancer Man]]".
