'''''M*A*S*H''''' is a 1970 American [[Satire|satirical]] [[black comedy]] [[war film]] directed by [[Robert Altman]] and written by [[Ring Lardner Jr.]], based on [[Richard Hooker (author)|Richard Hooker]]'s 1968 novel ''[[MASH: A Novel About Three Army Doctors]]''. 

The film depicts a unit of medical personnel stationed at a [[Mobile Army Surgical Hospital]] (MASH) during the [[Korean War]]. It stars [[Donald Sutherland]], [[Tom Skerritt]], and [[Elliott Gould]], with [[Sally Kellerman]], [[Robert Duvall]], [[René Auberjonois]], [[Gary Burghoff]], [[Roger Bowen]], [[Michael Murphy (actor)|Michael Murphy]], and in his film debut, professional football player [[Fred Williamson]]. Although the Korean War is the film's storyline setting, the [[subtext]] is the [[Vietnam War]]&nbsp;– a current event at the time the film was made.<ref name="Entertainment">{{cite book |title=The Entertainment Weekly Guide to the Greatest Movies Ever Made |url=https://archive.org/details/entertainmentwee00newy |url-access=registration |publisher=Warner Books |year=1996 |location=New York |page=[https://archive.org/details/entertainmentwee00newy/page/49 49]}}</ref> 

After having a panel screen the film, the Army and Air Force Motion Picture Service initially banned ''M*A*S*H'' from being shown at Army and Air Force base theaters because it "reflected unfavorably" on the armed services.<ref name="s059">{{cite news | title='M*A*S*H' Banned From Being Shown At Military Bases | newspaper=The New York Times | date=March 17, 1970 | url=https://www.nytimes.com/1970/03/17/archives/mash-banned-from-being-shown-at-military-bases.html | access-date=March 31, 2025}}</ref><ref>{{cite web | title=AFI Catalog - M*A*S*H | website=American Film Institite | date=November 24, 1997 | url=https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/23421 | access-date=March 31, 2025}}</ref>

''M*A*S*H'' became one of the biggest films of the early 1970s for [[20th Century Fox]]. It won the Grand Prix du Festival International du Film, later named the {{Lang|fr|[[Palme d'Or]]|italic=Or}}, at the [[1970 Cannes Film Festival]], received five [[Academy Awards|Academy Award]] nominations, including [[Academy Award for Best Picture|Best Picture]], and won for [[Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay|Best Adapted Screenplay]]. In 1996, ''M*A*S*H'' was selected for preservation in the [[National Film Registry]] by the [[Library of Congress]] being deemed "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".<ref>{{Cite web |title=Complete National Film Registry Listing |url=https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-film-preservation-board/film-registry/complete-national-film-registry-listing/ |access-date=December 4, 2020 |website=Library of Congress|archive-date=December 17, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141217172059/https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-film-preservation-board/film-registry/complete-national-film-registry-listing/ |url-status=live}}</ref> The [[Academy Film Archive]] preserved ''M*A*S*H'' in 2000.<ref>{{cite web |title=Preserved Projects |url=http://www.oscars.org/academy-film-archive/preserved-projects?title=M*A*S*H&filmmaker=&category=All&collection=All |website=Academy Film Archive |access-date=August 3, 2016 |archive-date=August 13, 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160813044506/http://www.oscars.org/academy-film-archive/preserved-projects?title=M*A*S*H&filmmaker=&category=All&collection=All |url-status=live}}</ref>

The film inspired the television series [[M*A*S*H (TV series)|of the same name]], which ran from 1972 to 1983 on [[CBS]]. [[Gary Burghoff]], who played Radar O'Reilly, was the only actor playing a major character who appeared in both the film and the television series. Altman despised the TV series, calling it "the antithesis of what we were trying to do" with the film.<ref name="fishman2018">{{cite magazine |title=What 'M*A*S*H' Taught Us |magazine=The New Yorker |url=https://www.newyorker.com/culture/culture-desk/what-mash-taught-us | date=July 24, 2018|access-date=September 23, 2022 | first=Howard | last=Fishman}}</ref>

==Plot==
In 1951, the 4077th Mobile Army Surgical Hospital in South Korea is assigned two new surgeons, [[List of M*A*S*H characters#Hawkeye Pierce|"Hawkeye" Pierce]] and [[List of M*A*S*H characters#Duke Forrest|"Duke" Forrest]], who arrive in a stolen [[Jeep]]. They are insubordinate, womanizing, mischievous rule-breakers, but they prove to be excellent combat surgeons. Other characters include the bungling commanding officer [[List of M*A*S*H characters#Henry Blake|Henry Blake]], his hyper-competent chief clerk [[List of M*A*S*H characters#Radar O'Reilly|Radar O'Reilly]], dentist Walter "Painless Pole" Waldowski, the pompous and incompetent surgeon [[List of M*A*S*H characters#Frank Burns|Frank Burns]], and the contemplative Chaplain [[List of M*A*S*H characters#Father John Mulcahy|Father Mulcahy]].

Irritated by Frank's religious fervor, Hawkeye and Duke get Blake to move him to another tent so newly arrived chest surgeon [[List of M*A*S*H characters#Trapper John McIntyre|Trapper John McIntyre]] can move in. The three draftee doctors (the "Swampmen", after the nickname for their tent) have little respect for military protocol, and pass time with pranks and heavy drinking. Frank, a straitlaced by-the-book military officer, bonds with [[List of M*A*S*H characters#Margaret Houlihan|Margaret Houlihan]]<!--do not change to O'Houlihan; see Talk Page and help reach a consensus-->, the 4077th's head nurse and they develop a love affair. Radar and the Swampmen sneak a microphone into her tent and broadcast the pair's sexual encounter over the public address system, earning Houlihan the nickname "Hot Lips". The next morning, Hawkeye goads Frank into assaulting him, and Burns is removed for psychiatric evaluation. Later, when Houlihan is showering, the Swampmen pull the tent sides off and expose her, in order to settle a bet over whether she is a natural blonde. Houlihan confronts Blake for his weak leadership and threatens to resign her commission; Blake bluntly tells her she can do so.

Painless, described as "best-equipped dentist in the Army" and "the dental Don Juan of Detroit", fears an incident of [[Erectile dysfunction|impotence]] has turned him homosexual and he announces his intent to commit suicide. The Swampmen agree to help, stage a feast to evoke [[The Last Supper (Leonardo)|Leonardo da Vinci's Last Supper]], arrange for Father Mulcahy to give absolution and communion, and provide him with a "black capsule" (actually a sleeping pill) to speed him on his way. Hawkeye persuades the gorgeous Lieutenant "Dish" Schneider&nbsp;– who has remained faithful to her husband and is being transferred back to the United States for discharge&nbsp;– to spend the night with Painless and allay his concern about his "[[latent homosexuality]]". The next morning, Painless is his usual cheerful self, and a smiling Dish departs for home in a helicopter.

Hawkeye and Trapper are ordered to Japan to operate on a Congressman's son, which they exploit as an opportunity to play golf. When they also perform an unauthorized operation on a local infant, they face disciplinary action from the hospital commander for misusing Army resources. Trapper uses anesthetic gas to sedate the commander. Using staged photographs of him as he awakens in bed with a prostitute, they [[blackmail]] him into keeping his mouth shut.

Blake and General Hammond organize a [[American football|football]] game between the 4077th and the 325th Evac Hospital and heavily wager on the outcome. At Hawkeye's suggestion, Blake applies to have a specific [[neurosurgery|neurosurgeon]]&nbsp;– [[List of M*A*S*H characters#Capt. Spearchucker Jones|Dr. Oliver Harmon "Spearchucker" Jones]], a former professional football player for the [[San Francisco 49ers]]&nbsp;– transferred to the 4077th as a [[List of sports idioms#R|ringer]]. Hawkeye also suggests Blake bet half his money up front and keep Jones out of the first half of the game. The 325th scores repeatedly and easily, even after the 4077th drugs one of their star players to incapacitate him. Hammond confidently offers high odds, against which Blake bets the rest of his money. Jones enters the second half, which quickly devolves into a free-for-all, and the 4077th gets the 325th's second ringer thrown out of the game and wins with a final trick play.

Not long after the football game, Hawkeye and Duke get their discharge orders and begin their journey home&nbsp;– taking the same stolen Jeep in which they arrived.<ref>{{Citation |title=MASH (1970) |url=https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0066026/plotsummary |access-date=January 14, 2019 |archive-date=March 7, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170307195824/http://www.imdb.com/title/tt0066026/plotsummary |url-status=live}}</ref>

==Production==

===Development and writing===
The screenplay, by [[Ring Lardner Jr.]], is different from Hooker's original novel. In the DVD [[audio commentary]], Altman describes the novel as "pretty terrible" and somewhat "racist." He says that the novel was used only as a springboard.<ref name=DVDcommentary /> Despite this assertion by Altman, most sequences are in the novel, with the main deletion being a subplot of Ho-Jon's return to the 4077th as a casualty. The main deviation from the script is the trimming of much of the dialogue.

In his director's commentary, Altman says that ''M*A*S*H'' was the first major studio film to use the word "fuck" in its dialogue.<ref name=DVDcommentary>Altman, Robert ''M*A*S*H Collector's Edition'' (Twentieth Century Fox Home Entertainment), "Director's Commentary" track. ASIN No. B000BZISTE. Released February 7, 2006.</ref>{{efn|However, while it is the earliest film released by a mainstream studio to use the word, it had been used earlier in a number of "[[cult film|underground]]" and/or "[[independent film|independent]]" films through the [[1960s in film|1960s]], including [[Andy Milligan]]'s ''[[Vapors (film)|Vapors]]'',<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://player.bfi.org.uk/rentals/film/watch-vapors-1965-online |title=Vapors at the BFI player (paywalled) |access-date=January 28, 2021 |archive-date=February 1, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210201222701/https://player.bfi.org.uk/rentals/film/watch-vapors-1965-online |url-status=live}}</ref> [[Andy Warhol]]'s ''[[Poor Little Rich Girl (1965 film)|Poor Little Rich Girl]]'' and ''[[My Hustler]]'' (all 1965),<ref>{{Cite book |last=Grudin |first=Anthony E. |title=Warhol's Working Class: Pop Art and Egalitarianism |publisher=[[University of Chicago Press]] |year=2017 |isbn=9780226347776}}</ref> [[Joseph Strick]]'s ''[[Ulysses (1967 film)|Ulysses]]'', [[Michael Winner]]'s ''[[I'll Never Forget What's'isname]]'',<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.smh.com.au/entertainment/movies/well-i-swear-a-brief-fing-history-of-profanity-in-the-movies-20140901-10axdu.html |title=Well, I Swear! The actor, [[John Schuck]], said in an interview that [[Andy Sidaris]], who was handling the football sequences, encouraged Schuck to "say something that'll annoy him." Schuck did so, and that particular statement made it into the film without a second thought.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.avclub.com/article/john-schuck-67216 |title=John Schuck |work=[[The A.V. ''M*A*S*H'' was rated R in the United States by this system.

Although a number of sources have reported that Lardner was upset with the liberties taken with his script,<ref name="fishman2018" /><ref name=Prigge /> he denied it in his autobiography: "[...] But the departures weren't as drastic as he [Altman] made out; much of the improvisation involved a couple of scenes between Donald Sutherland and Elliott Gould in which they rephrased lines in their own words. [...] For all of Bob's interpolations and improvisations, however, the basic structure of the movie is the one laid out in my script, and each scene has the beginning, middle, end that I gave it."<ref>{{Cite book |last=Lardner |first=Ring Jr. |title=I'd Hate Myself in the Morning: A Memoir |publisher=[[Thunder's Mouth Press]] |year=2000 |isbn=9781560252962 |pages=170–172}}</ref>

===Filming and production===
Altman, relatively new to the filmmaking establishment at that time, lacked the credentials to justify his unorthodox filmmaking process and had a history of turning down work rather than creating a poor-quality product.<ref name=NCMA /> Altman: "I had practice working for people who don't care about quality, and I learned how to sneak it in."<ref name=NCMA>Film Curator, [[North Carolina Museum of Art]].  [http://www.moviediva.com/MD_root/reviewpages/MDMASH.htm "M*A*S*H (1970)"] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071020021122/http://moviediva.com/MD_root/reviewpages/MDMASH.htm |date=October 20, 2007 }} (Raleigh, North Carolina, 2001). Retrieved January 12, 2020.</ref> "[[Twentieth Century Fox]] had two other war movies in production, ''[[Patton (film)|Patton]]'' and ''[[Tora! Tora! Tora!]]''" Altman remembered.  "Those were big-budget pictures, and we were cheap.  I knew that if I stayed under budget and didn't cause too much trouble, we could sneak through."<ref name="theattic.space">{{cite web |title=The Movie that Spoke the Truth to War |url=https://www.theattic.space/home-page-blogs/2020/2/8/test-draft |website=The Attic |date=February 11, 2020 |access-date=March 3, 2020 |archive-date=March 3, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200303193402/https://www.theattic.space/home-page-blogs/2020/2/8/test-draft |url-status=live}}</ref>

The filming process was difficult because of tensions between the director and his cast. During [[principal photography]], Sutherland and Gould allegedly spent a third of their time trying to get Altman fired,<ref name=NCMA /> although this has been disputed.<ref name=Prigge>Prigge, Matt. [http://www.metro.us/newyork/entertainment/movies-entertainment/2014/08/04/elliott-gould-talks-robert-altman-says-never-tried-get-fired/ "Elliott Gould talks Robert Altman and says he never tried to get him fired,"] ''Metro: Entertainment'' (August 4, 2014). {{webarchive | url = https://web.archive.org/web/20141020100707/http://www.metro.us/newyork/entertainment/movies-entertainment/2014/08/04/elliott-gould-talks-robert-altman-says-never-tried-get-fired/ |date=October 20, 2014 }}.</ref> Altman later commented that if he had known about Gould and Sutherland's protests, he would have resigned.<ref name=DVDcommentary /> Gould later sent a letter of apology, and Altman used him in a number of his later works – including ''[[The Long Goodbye (film)|The Long Goodbye]]'', ''[[California Split]]'', ''[[Nashville (film)|Nashville]]'', and ''[[The Player (1992 film)|The Player]]'' – but Altman never worked with Sutherland again.

Because of the context of the film being made – during the height of America's involvement in the [[Vietnam War]] – Fox was concerned that audiences would not understand that it was ostensibly taking place during the [[Korean War]]. Fox requested a caption that mentions the Korean setting be added to the beginning of the film,<ref name=DVDcommentary /> and PA announcements throughout the film served the same purpose.<ref name=NCMA /> Only a few loudspeaker announcements were used in the original cut. When Altman realized he needed more structure to his largely episodic film, editor Danford Greene suggested using more loudspeaker announcements to frame different episodes of the story. Greene took a second-unit crew and filmed additional shots of the speakers. On the same night these scenes were shot, American [[astronauts]] landed on the moon.<ref>"Enlisted: The Story of ''M*A*S*H''" (making-of documentary), Twentieth Century Fox Home Entertainment, 2001.</ref> The Korean War is explicitly referenced in announcements on the camp public address system<ref name=NCMA /> and during a radio announcement that plays while Hawkeye and Trapper are [[golf|putting]] in Col. Merrill's office, which also cites the film as taking place in 1951.{{Citation needed|date=December 2007}}  However, one of the PA announcements mentions that the camp movie will be ''[[The Glory Brigade]]'', a 1953 Korean War film starring [[Victor Mature]].

===Soundtrack music===
[[Johnny Mandel]] composed incidental music used throughout the film. Also heard on the soundtrack are Japanese vocal renditions of such songs as "Tokyo Shoe Shine Boy", "[[My Blue Heaven (song)|My Blue Heaven]]", "[[Happy Days Are Here Again]]", "[[Chattanooga Choo Choo]]", and "[[Hi-Lili, Hi-Lo]]"; impromptu performances of "[[Onward, Christian Soldiers]]", "[[When the Lights Go On Again]]", and "[[Hail to the Chief]]" by cast members; and the instrumental "[[Washington Post March]]" during the climactic football game.

''M*A*S*H'' features the song "[[Suicide Is Painless]]",<ref name="MASH-intro">{{Citation |title=MASH series, every intro! |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F8adfLbLqIs |language=en |access-date=March 26, 2020 |archive-date=April 27, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200427174142/https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F8adfLbLqIs&gl=US&hl=en |url-status=live}}</ref> with music by Mandel and lyrics by Mike Altman, the director's then 14-year-old son. The song is reprised later in the film by Pvt. Seidman (played by Ken Prymus) in the scene in which Painless attempts to commit suicide.

==Release==
===Home media===
''M*A*S*H'' received its first home video release in 1977 on both [[VHS]] and [[Betamax]]. This 1977 release of the film was the original, unedited version and was one of the first 50 titles released to home video by [[Magnetic Video|Magnetic Video Corporation]] (''M*A*S*H'' was number #38).

[[Ster-Kinekor]] Video and [[20th Century Home Entertainment|Fox Video]] released the film with [[20th Century Fox]], which was released in 1992 in South Africa.

In the 1990s, Fox Video re-released a VHS version of the film as part of its "Selections" banner, which ran 116 minutes and was rated PG. However, this is not the alternate PG version that was released in 1973. It has the same run-time as the theatrical release; none of the aforementioned scenes or theme music was removed. The actual 1973 PG-edited version has never been issued on home video in the United States.<ref name=Variety>"Big Rental Films of 1973", ''Variety'', January 9, 1974, p. 19.</ref> The original cut was released on DVD on January 8, 2002,<ref>{{cite web |first1=John |last1=Jimenez |url=http://www.hive4media.com/news/html/product_article.cfm?article_id=1812 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20011101151400/http://www.hive4media.com/news/html/product_article.cfm?article_id=1812 |title=Fox Gives 'M*A*S*H' Five-Star Treatment |website=hive4media.com |archive-date=November 1, 2001 |date=September 21, 2001 |access-date=September 7, 2019 |url-status=live}}</ref> and on [[Blu-ray]] on September 1, 2009.<ref>https://www.amazon.com/M-S-Blu-ray-Donald-Sutherland/dp/B0024HH32A/ Retrieved March 27, 2024.</ref>

In 2010, the film was released on a triple feature DVD pack with ''[[The Full Monty]]'' and ''[[My Cousin Vinny]]''.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://movieweb.com/20th-century-fox-celebrates-its-75th-anniversary-with-brand-new-dvd-and-blu-ray-releases/|title=20th Century Fox Celebrates Its 75th Anniversary with Brand New DVD and Blu-ray Releases|date=April 14, 2010 }}</ref>

==Reception==

===Box office===
''M*A*S*H'' was a box-office hit; it was the third highest-grossing film released in [[1970 in film|1970]] (behind ''[[Love Story (1970 film)|Love Story]]'' and ''[[Airport (1970 film)|Airport]]'').<ref name="Block & Wilson">{{cite book |editor1-last=Block |editor1-first=Alex Ben |editor2-last=Wilson |editor2-first=Lucy Autrey |year=2010 |title=George Lucas's Blockbusting: A Decade-By-Decade Survey of Timeless Movies Including Untold Secrets of Their Financial and Cultural Success |publisher=[[HarperCollins]] |isbn=9780061778896 |url-access=registration |url=https://archive.org/details/georgelucassbloc00alex}}
* ''M*A*S*H'': p.527. $67.3 million (Initial Release Domestic Box office)</ref> The film opened January 25, 1970, at the Baronet Theatre in New York City and grossed $37,143 in its first week.<ref>{{cite magazine |title=Strength at Some N.Y. Situations; 'Patton' Spanky 51G; 'Zabriskie' OK Kickoff; 'Looking Glass,' In 2, Big |magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] |date=February 11, 1970 |page=9}}</ref> According to [[20th Century Fox]] records, the film required $6,550,000 in rentals to break even, and by December 11, 1970, had made $31,225,000, thus making a profit for the studio.<ref>{{cite book |page=[https://archive.org/details/foxthatgotawayt00silv/page/329 329] |title=The Fox That got Away: the Last Days of the Zanuck Dynasty at Twentieth Century-Fox |url=https://archive.org/details/foxthatgotawayt00silv |url-access=registration |last1=Silverman |first1=Stephen M |year=1988 |publisher=L. Stuart|isbn=9780818404856 }}</ref> Ultimately, the film made $81.6&nbsp;million<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://boxofficemojo.com/movies/?id=mash.htm |publisher=[[Box Office Mojo]] |title=M*A*S*H, Box Office Information |access-date=January 29, 2012 |archive-date=January 28, 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120128225219/http://www.boxofficemojo.com/movies/?id=mash.htm |url-status=live}}</ref> against a budget of $3&nbsp;million.<ref>Solomon, Aubrey. ''Twentieth Century Fox: A Corporate and Financial History (The Scarecrow Filmmakers Series)''. Lanham, Maryland: Scarecrow Press, 1989. {{ISBN|978-0-8108-4244-1}}. p. 256.</ref>

It was the sixth most popular film at the French box office in 1970.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://translate.googleusercontent.com/translate_c?depth=1&rurl=translate.google.com&sl=fr&sp=nmt4&tl=en&u=http://www.boxofficestory.com/france-1970-c22750529&usg=ALkJrhineDwQL3nWRzu5huL7fLnsBt_9Lg |website=Box Office Story |title=1970 Box Office in France |access-date=June 14, 2021 |archive-date=June 14, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210614161013/https://fonts.googleapis.com/icon?family=Material+Icons |url-status=live}}</ref>

Fox re-released the film to theaters in North America in late 1973. To attract audiences to the ''[[M*A*S*H (TV series)|M*A*S*H]]'' television series, which had struggled in the ratings in its first season, Fox reissued the film in a version running 112 minutes and bearing a [[Motion Picture Association of America film rating system|PG rating]]. Some of the more explicit content from the original R-rated cut was edited out, including segments of graphic surgical operations, Hot Lips' shower scene, and the use of the word "fuck" during the football game. According to film critic and historian [[Leonard Maltin]], the film's main theme song, "Suicide is Painless", was replaced with music by [[Ahmad Jamal]].<ref>Maltin, Leonard. ''Leonard Maltin's Movie & Video Guide'' (NAL).</ref> The re-release earned an estimated $3.5&nbsp;million at the box office.<ref name=Variety />

===Critical response===

====Contemporaneous====
Upon its release, ''M*A*S*H'' received acclaim from critics. In a rave review, John Mahoney of ''[[The Hollywood Reporter]]'' called the film "the finest American comedy since ''[[Some Like It Hot]]''", and "the ''[[Mister Roberts (1955 film)|Mister Roberts]]'' of the Korean War", as well as "''[[The Graduate]]'' of 1970".<ref>Mahoney, John. ''M*A*S*H'' review, ''The Hollywood Reporter'' (1970). [https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/review/mash-review-1970-movie-1078385 Archived on THR website] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190430132801/https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/review/mash-review-1970-movie-1078385 |date=April 30, 2019 }}. Retrieved September 18, 2019.</ref> ''[[Time (magazine)|Time]]'' magazine, in a review titled "''[[Catch-22]]'' Caliber", wrote of the film, "though it wears a dozen manic, libidinous masks, none quite covers the face of dread&nbsp;... ''M.A.S.H.'', one of America's funniest bloody films, is also one of its bloodiest funny films."<ref>[http://content.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,878756,00.html "Cinema: Catch-22 Caliber"] ({{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190430132200/http://content.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,878756,00.html |date=April 30, 2019 }}, ''Time'' (January 26, 1970).</ref> ''[[The New Yorker]]'' critic [[Pauline Kael]] wrote of the film: "I don't know when I've had such a good time at a movie. Many of the best recent American movies leave you feeling that there's nothing to do but get stoned and die, that that's your proper fate as an American. This movie heals a breach."<ref>Kael, Pauline. [https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1970/01/24/blessed-profanity "Blessed Profanity"] ({{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190920170112/https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1970/01/24/blessed-profanity |date=September 20, 2019 }}), ''The New Yorker'' (January 24, 1970), p. 74.</ref> [[John Simon (critic)|John Simon]] described ''M*A*S*H'' as an 'amusingly absurdist army satire'.<ref>{{cite book |title=John Simon on Film: Criticism 1982-2001|last1=Simon|first1=John |publisher=Applause Books |year=2005 |page=321}}</ref>

[[Roger Ebert]], in the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'', gave the film four (out of four) stars, writing

{{Blockquote|There is something about war that inspires practical jokes and the heroes&nbsp;... are inspired and utterly heartless&nbsp;... We laugh, not because ''M*A*S*H'' is [[The Phil Silvers Show|Sgt. Bilko]] for adults, but because it is so true to the unadmitted sadist in all of us. There is perhaps nothing so exquisite as achieving&nbsp;... sweet mental revenge against someone we hate with particular dedication. And it is the flat-out, poker-faced hatred in ''M*A*S*H'' that makes it work. Most comedies want us to laugh at things that aren't really funny; in this one we laugh precisely because they're not funny. We laugh, that we may not cry&nbsp;... We can take the unusually high gore-level in ''M*A*S*H'' because it is originally part of the movie's logic. If the surgeons didn't have to face the daily list of maimed and mutilated bodies, none of the rest of their lives would make any sense&nbsp;... But none of this philosophy comes close to the insane logic of ''M*A*S*H'', which is achieved through a peculiar marriage of cinematography, acting, directing, and writing. The movie depends upon timing and tone to be funny&nbsp;... One of the reasons ''M*A*S*H'' is so funny is that it's so desperate.<ref>Ebert, Roger. [https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/mash-1970 "M*A*S*H"] ({{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190924003516/https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/mash-1970 |date=2019-09-24 }}), ''Chicago Sun-Times'' (January 1, 1970).</ref>}}

In contrast, [[Roger Greenspun]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' wrote of ''M*A*S*H'': "To my knowledge [it] is the first major American movie openly to ridicule belief in God&nbsp;– not phony belief; real belief. It is also one of the few (though by no means the first) American screen comedies openly to admit the cruelty of its humor. And it is at pains to blend that humor with more operating room gore than I have ever seen in any movie from any place&nbsp;... Although it is impudent, bold, and often very funny, it lacks the sense of order (even in the midst of disorder) that seems the special province of successful comedy."<ref>Greenspun, Roger. [https://www.nytimes.com/1970/01/26/archives/mash-film-blends-atheism-gore-humor.html 'M*A*S*H' Film Blends Atheism, Gore, Humor"] ({{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190920170112/https://www.nytimes.com/1970/01/26/archives/mash-film-blends-atheism-gore-humor.html |date=September 20, 2019 }}), ''The New York Times'' (January 26, 1970).</ref>

====Retrospective====
In a retrospective review for the ''[[Chicago Reader]]'', [[Jonathan Rosenbaum]] noted that "the film&nbsp;... helped launch the careers of Elliott Gould, Donald Sutherland, Sally Kellerman, Robert Duvall, and subsequent Altman regulars Rene Auberjonois and John Schuck, and won screenwriter Ring Lardner Jr. an Oscar." Rosenbaum characterized the film as "a somewhat adolescent if stylish antiauthoritarian romp&nbsp;... But the [[misogyny]] and cruelty behind many of the gags are as striking as the black comedy and the original use of overlapping dialogue. This is still watchable for the verve of the ensemble acting and dovetailing direction, but some of the crassness leaves a sour aftertaste."<ref>Rosenbaum, Jonathan. [https://www.chicagoreader.com/chicago/mash/Film?oid=4573472 "M*A*S*H"] ({{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190430134117/https://www.chicagoreader.com/chicago/mash/Film?oid=4573472 |date=April 30, 2019 }}), ''Chicago Reader''. Retrieved September 20, 2019.</ref> Writing in ''[[The Guardian]]'' for the film's 50th anniversary, Noah Gittell also criticized it for having "a deep and unexamined misogyny", noting that the treatment of the Houlihan character in particular anticipated such later teen sex comedies as ''[[Animal House]]'', ''[[Porky's]]'' and ''[[Revenge of the Nerds]]''.<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2020/jan/21/mash-movie-robert-altman-misogyny |title=M*A*S*H at 50: the Robert Altman comedy that revels in cruel misogyny |last1=Gittell |first1=Noah |newspaper=The Guardian |date=January 22, 2020 |access-date=January 27, 2020 |archive-date=January 27, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200127050726/https://www.theguardian.com/film/2020/jan/21/mash-movie-robert-altman-misogyny |url-status=live}}</ref>

The Japanese filmmaker [[Akira Kurosawa]] cited this movie as one of his 100 favorite films.<ref name="farout">{{cite web |last1=Thomas-Mason |first1=Lee |title=From Stanley Kubrick to Martin Scorsese: Akira Kurosawa once named his top 100 favourite films of all time |url=https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/akira-kurosawa-100-favourite-films-list/ |website=Far Out Magazine |date=January 12, 2021 |access-date=January 23, 2023}}</ref>{{better source needed|date=May 2026}} The film holds an 86% approval rating on [[Rotten Tomatoes]], based on 58 reviews, with an average rating of 8.30/10. The website's consensus states: "Bold, timely, subversive, and above all, funny, ''M*A*S*H'' remains a high point in Robert Altman's distinguished filmography."<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/mash/ |title=M*A*S*H (1970) |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |access-date=January 3, 2022 |archive-date=November 17, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201117210027/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/mash |url-status=live}}</ref> The film also holds a score of 80 out of 100 on [[Metacritic]], based on 8 critics, indicating "generally favorable reviews".<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/mash |title=MASH Reviews |publisher=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=March 16, 2020 |archive-date=November 11, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201111163652/https://www.metacritic.com/movie/mash |url-status=live}}</ref>
