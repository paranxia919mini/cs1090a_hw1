'''''A Better Tomorrow''''' ({{Lang-zh|c=英雄本色|j=Jing1 Hung4 Bun2 Sik1|l=true colours of a hero}}) is a 1986 Hong Kong [[action film]] directed and produced by [[John Woo]], with a screenplay by Woo, Chan Hing-ka, and Leung Suk-wah. The film stars [[Ti Lung]], [[Leslie Cheung]], [[Chow Yun-fat]], [[Emily Chu]], and [[Waise Lee]]. Ti plays Ho, a [[Triad (organised crime)|triad]] gangster and money counterfeiter who is sent to prison after being betrayed by his subordinate, Shing (Lee). After his release, Ho tries to reform himself and reconcile with his estranged brother Kit (Cheung), a police officer who resents him for his criminal past. Ho becomes torn between trying and failing to make amends with Kit, and following the advice of his friend Mark (Chow) to seek revenge for Shing's treachery.

The plot was heavily influenced by ''[[The Story of a Discharged Prisoner]]'' (1967), a film admired by both Woo and producer [[Tsui Hark]]. After forming [[Film Workshop]] in 1984, Tsui asked Woo to write and direct a dramatic crime film. Woo saw this as his chance to break away from directing comedies for [[Cinema City Enterprises|Cinema City]] and developed the story based on ''Discharged Prisoner'', adding his own ideas about honour and loyalty inspired by ''[[wuxia]]'' and the work of [[Jean-Pierre Melville]]. After convincing Cinema City to co-produce, Woo and Tsui began filming in early 1986, working with a budget of under {{Currency|11 million|HKD|passthrough=yes|linked=yes}}. The majority of filming took place in Hong Kong, with some exterior shots filmed in Taiwan, and concluded after 80 to 100 days.

Released during a time when comedy films were popular in Hong Kong, Cinema City was expecting ''A Better Tomorrow'' to be a [[box-office bomb]] due to its dramatic tone and lack of a popular cast. However, it defied expectations and achieved immediate commercial success upon its release on 2 August 1986, earning over {{Currency|34 million|HKD|linked=no|passthrough=yes}} during its extended theatrical run. Critics praised the action sequences, Woo's direction, and the performances of its cast. The film was nominated for numerous accolades, winning two [[Hong Kong Film Award|Hong Kong Film Awards]] and three [[Golden Horse Film Festival and Awards|Golden Horse Awards]].

''A Better Tomorrow'' has been influential on action films since its release. Its stylised, balletic gunplay is considered the original source of [[gun fu]] fight sequences. The movie's popularity led Hong Kong film producers to introduce more melodramatic elements into their crime films, leading to the emergence of the [[heroic bloodshed]] film genre. ''A Better Tomorrow'' is considered by critics to be one of the greatest action movies, with a Hong Kong Film Awards panel ranking it second in its list of The Best 100 Chinese Motion Pictures in 2005. Its box office success spawned a direct sequel, ''[[A Better Tomorrow II]]'' (1987), a prequel entitled ''[[A Better Tomorrow III: Love & Death in Saigon]]'' (1989), and two official remakes: [[A Better Tomorrow (2010 film)|one in South Korea]] released in 2010 and [[A Better Tomorrow 2018|one in mainland China]] in 2018.

==Plot==

[[Triad (organised crime)|Triad]] member Ho{{Efn|Sung Tse-ho ({{Lang-zh|c=宋子豪|labels=no}}) and Sung Tse-kit ({{Lang-zh|c=宋子杰|labels=no}}) are the brothers' full names, with "Tse" being their [[generation name]].<ref name="SBS_NamingConventions">{{Cite web |last=Evason |first=Nina |year=2024 |title=Hong Kong Culture: Chinese Naming Conventions |url=http://culturalatlas.sbs.com.au/hong-kong-culture/hong-kong-culture-naming |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250725040902/https://culturalatlas.sbs.com.au/hong-kong-culture/hong-kong-culture-naming |archive-date=2025-07-25 |access-date=2025-10-01 |website=SBS Cultural Atlas |publisher=[[Special Broadcasting Service]] |language=en}}</ref> In the film, they are informally referred to by their friends and close acquaintances as "Ah Ho" ({{Lang-zh|c=阿豪|labels=no}}) and "Ah Kit" ({{Lang-zh|c=阿杰|labels=no}}), which reflects how Cantonese speakers use the prefix "ah" to show familiarity with someone.{{Sfn|Matthews|Yip|2011|p=43}} For brevity, these two characters will be referred to by their given names, Ho and Kit.|name=NamingConventions}} and his best friend, Mark Lee, run a [[Counterfeit money|money counterfeiting]] business for their boss, Mr. Yiu. This activity is kept secret from Ho's younger brother Kit,{{Efn|name=NamingConventions}} a police cadet. Out of respect for their ailing father, Ho promises to stop being a gangster after completing his next business deal. At Mr. Yiu's request, Ho lets a subordinate named Shing accompany him to [[Taiwan]] to conduct business with a local mob. However, the gang ambushes them, causing a shootout that alerts the Taiwanese police. Ho tells Shing to escape while he surrenders. To ensure Ho doesn't implicate anyone, the Taiwanese gang sends a kidnapper to abduct his father. In the scuffle between Kit, his girlfriend Jackie, and the kidnapper, Ho and Kit's father is fatally stabbed. Before he dies, the father begs Kit to forgive his brother, but a furious Kit blames Ho for their father's death. Mark travels to Taiwan to exact revenge on the gangsters, shooting them dead in a restaurant. However, he is shot twice in his right leg, leaving him crippled and needing a leg brace to walk.

Ho is released from prison after serving a three-year sentence. Determined to start anew, he gets a job driving for a taxi company run by ex-convict Ken. Ho finds out that Shing has climbed the ranks of the triad during his imprisonment, usurping Mark's position and status. He is devastated to learn that Mark, who had hidden the extent of his condition in his letters to Ho, has been cast aside by Shing after becoming disabled. Not wanting to be dragged back into crime, Ho refuses to confront his former protégé, opting to try and reconcile with Kit. However, Kit resents the fact that his familial connection to Ho is preventing his promotion at work. In an effort to prove to his superiors that he can be trusted, Kit becomes obsessed with bringing down Shing, despite Ho's warnings. 

Hoping to expand the triad's operations into [[Illegal drug trade|drug trafficking]], Shing tries to persuade Ho to rejoin the triad. He tells Ho that they stand to make a lot of money if Kit is turned into a [[Police corruption|corrupt policeman]]. When Ho refuses, Shing has his men raid the taxi company, beat Mark nearly to death, and shoot Kit after luring him into a trap. After Mark and Ho narrowly escape arrest, Mark pleads with Ho to stand up for himself and fight Shing. Fed up with his friend's unwillingness, Mark steals a [[Magnetic-tape data storage|computer tape]] containing printing plate data from the counterfeiting business. Having a change of heart, Ho shows up on a motorbike to collect the tape. After using it to blackmail Shing for {{Currency|2 million|linked=yes|passthrough=yes}} and an escape boat to leave Hong Kong on, Ho transfers the tape to Kit to use as proof of Shing's crimes.

Through a conversation with Mr. Yiu, Shing reveals that he was the one who set up the ambush in Taiwan. After lying to his boss about a negotiation with Ho, Shing kills Mr. Yiu, coercing the witnesses to frame Ho as the murderer. At the ransom exchange site, Ho and Mark take Shing hostage and drive to a pier, where the boat is guarded by Shing's men. Ho implores Mark to escape by himself in the boat. Kit arrives to arrest Shing, but is taken hostage by the gangster's men. The two parties try to exchange Shing for Kit, but it quickly devolves into a shootout. Hearing the sound of gunfire, Mark comes back to help, and the three of them kill most of Shing's henchmen. After seeing Kit rebuff his brother’s attempts at reconciliation again, Mark berates him, telling him that Ho's present actions have made up for his past sins. Mid-speech, Mark is shot from behind and killed by Shing and his remaining enforcer.

Ho kills Shing's last henchman, but runs out of ammunition as the police approach. Shing mocks the two brothers, stating that his money and influence will ensure his swift release from custody. Finally forgiving his brother, Kit hands Ho his revolver, and Ho shoots Shing dead. As redemption for his crimes, Ho handcuffs himself to Kit, and the two walk towards the gathered crowd of police together.

The primary inspiration for ''A Better Tomorrow'' was [[Patrick Lung]]'s ''[[The Story of a Discharged Prisoner]]'' (1967), a film which [[John Woo]] and [[Tsui Hark]] both greatly admired.{{Sfn|Rizov|2014}}{{Sfn|Pinkerton|2017}} According to film critic {{Interlanguage link|Law Kar|lt=Law Kar|zh|羅卡 (香港)}}, Woo had witnessed Lung directing the movie at {{Interlanguage link|Wader Film Studio|lt=Wader Film Studio|zh|華達製片廠}};{{Sfn|Tsang|Cunliffe|2022}} Tsui meanwhile, had seen it as a teenager and was impressed by the film's handling of social issues. Lung's movies eventually influenced Tsui to venture into filmmaking by creating short films.{{Sfn|Rizov|2014}}{{Sfn|Cunliffe|2021}} Throughout his career, Tsui kept ''Discharged Prisoner'' on his mind, and considered making a dramatic work inspired by it. He first suggested an adaptation of Lung's film in the mid-1970s while working at [[TVB]], but his idea was ignored.{{Sfn|Rizov|2014}} Years later, Tsui would propose a similar project to the studio he was contracted with, [[Cinema City Enterprises|Cinema City]]. Due to Cinema City's commercial success with formulaic comedies,{{Sfn|Teo|1997|p=153}}{{Sfn|Yang|2003|p=85}} they refused, stating that local audiences would not want to watch such a serious movie.{{Sfn|Morton|2016|p=63}}

Undeterred by the rejection, Tsui founded the production company [[Film Workshop]] in April 1984 with his wife, [[Nansun Shi]].{{Sfn|Heard|2000|p=37}} Tsui then approached his friend Woo, who had helped kickstart his directing career by getting him initial contract work with Cinema City, to come write and direct a gangster film.{{Sfn|Abrams|2023}}{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=10}}{{Sfn|Heard|2000|p=38}} Woo himself had been feeling burnt out while working in Taiwan for Cinema City. He had originally signed with the studio in hopes of getting more directorial freedom, but had instead been shunted into doing administrative work, stopping only to churn out a couple of company-mandated comedies for money. Woo was frustrated by his potential future, as he had desperately wanted to make a gangster film instead.{{Sfn|Yang|1997|pp=94, 96}}{{Sfn|Heard|2000|p=33}}{{Sfn|Elder|2005b|p=47}} In a 2025 interview with ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'', he stated, "Before ''A Better Tomorrow'', I didn't have much chance to try this kind of a style. Because I never got any support from the studio or anyone, I wasn't able to experiment."{{Sfn|Debruge|2025}} Intrigued by Tsui's offer, he came back to Hong Kong in 1985, but was surprised by a perceived cultural shift when he arrived. Woo noted that there had been "gangster infiltration into all kinds of businesses—even the film industry—and a widespread feeling that there were no morals left, that many people would do anything to get ahead."{{Sfn|McDonagh|1993|p=50}} Taken aback by this, Woo decided that he would direct a film that would bring back and highlight "true values, like honour and chivalry".{{Sfn|Yang|1997|p=96}}

=== Writing and development ===
Inspired by [[Jean-Pierre Melville]], Woo began writing ''A Better Tomorrow'' with the goal of making it his own version of ''[[Le Samouraï]]'' (1967).{{Sfn|Abrams|2023}}{{Sfn|Heard|2000|p=41}}<ref name="NYT_Yu2023">{{Cite news |last=Yu |first=Brandon |date=2023-12-01 |title=John Woo Has Seen a Lot in Hollywood. He's Finally Back for More. |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2023/12/01/movies/john-woo-silent-night.html |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231201152857/https://www.nytimes.com/2023/12/01/movies/john-woo-silent-night.html |archive-date=2023-12-01 |access-date=2023-12-01 |work=[[The New York Times]]|url-status=live}}</ref> Encouraged by Tsui to draw upon his own personal experiences to write the film's dialogue, Woo considered ''A Better Tomorrow'' to be his first auteur movie.<ref name="NYT_Yu2023" /><ref name="Vulture_Ebiri2023">{{Cite web |last=Ebiri |first=Bilge |author-link=Bilge Ebiri |date=2023-07-03 |title='My Films Had So Much Anger' |url=https://www.vulture.com/2023/07/john-woo-on-face-off-mission-impossible-2-and-more.html |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230703134643/https://www.vulture.com/2023/07/john-woo-on-face-off-mission-impossible-2-and-more.html |archive-date=2023-07-03 |access-date=2023-10-15 |website=[[Vulture (website)|Vulture]] |language=en|url-status=live}}</ref> He meant for the film to be a way for him to express his feelings about Hong Kong and human dignity, and didn't intend for it to be a political statement.{{Sfn|Abrams|2023}}{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=118}}  Woo also wanted it to showcase his friendship with Tsui, since both men had helped each other gain a foothold in their respective directing careers.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=124}}{{Sfn|Woo|2000|p=66}}

Woo was influenced by the gallant tales of ''[[wuxia]]'', remarking that films like ''A Better Tomorrow'' "are violent, but they also have an element of romance—not love, but chivalry—and there's always the dream of a better world."{{Sfn|McDonagh|1993|p=52}} He noted the impact that a previous directorial effort, ''[[Last Hurrah for Chivalry]]'' (1979), had on him, calling it a prequel to ''A Better Tomorrow''.{{Sfn|Teo|1997|p=175}} While Woo used the basic plot of ''The Story of a Discharged Prisoner'' as the foundation for his film, including copying the Chinese title directly,{{Sfn|Li|1994|p=176}} he made specific changes to the story's characters.{{Sfn|Elder|2005b|p=49}} Woo turned the younger brother into a police officer in order to heighten the drama between the siblings, and reworked the original film's social worker character to be Mark.{{Sfn|Elder|2005b|p=49}} Since Woo was a fan of [[Nicholas Ray]]'s ''[[Rebel Without a Cause]]'' (1955), he wrote Kit to be an homage to [[James Dean]]'s character.{{Sfn|Elder|2011|p=212}} More notably, most of the characters in the original were rewritten to be male roles, despite Tsui's request to include female protagonists.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=11}} Tsui admitted that there was friction between him and Woo, stating that from a production standpoint: "I get too involved in the project, and there is not enough room for some directors to breathe. John Woo is very much independent."{{Sfn|Heard|2000|pp=41–42}}{{Sfn|Dannen|Long|1997|p=41}} 

In addition to Woo, {{Interlanguage link|Chan Hing-ka|lt=Chan Hing-ka|zh|陳慶嘉}} and Leung Suk-wah ({{Lang-zh|c=梁淑華|labels=no}}) also received writing credits for the screenplay.{{Sfn|Elder|2005a|p=xxv}} Chan was enlisted by one of the film's production managers, who remembered his work co-writing the TVB television series ''[[The Battle Among the Clans]]'' (1985).<ref name="ChanHingKaInterview_HiringForTVBWork">{{Cite AV media |title=When Tomorrow Comes: An Interview with Screenwriter Chan Hing-ka |last=Chan |first=Hing-ka |type=Blu-ray |publisher=[[Shout! Studios]] |year=2025 |time=0:40}}</ref> His primary screenplay contribution was introducing a romantic relationship between Kit and Jackie, as Woo and Tsui wanted the younger characters in the film to get more screen time.<ref name="ChanHingKaInterview_KitJackieRomance">{{Cite AV media |title=When Tomorrow Comes: An Interview with Screenwriter Chan Hing-ka |last=Chan |first=Hing-ka |type=Blu-ray |language=en |publisher=[[Shout! Studios]] |year=2025 |time=15:25}}</ref> For the film's English title, Woo asked Nansun Shi for a phrase that better reflected the film's themes of redemption and courage, shortening her suggestion of "For a Better Tomorrow".{{Sfn|Fang|2004|pp=33–34}}

While Tsui had wanted to include women in the movie's leading roles—he expressed interest in getting [[Michelle Yeoh]] to play a female version of Mark—the film was [[Greenlight|greenlit]] based on the selection of three male leads: Chow Yun-fat, Ti Lung, and Leslie Cheung.{{Sfn|Short|2000}} In an interview with ''[[Apple Daily]]'', [[Raymond Wong Pak-ming]], a co-founder of Cinema City, said that the role of Mark had initially been offered to [[George Lam]]. === Filming and post-production ===
Film production began in early 1986 with a budget of under {{Currency|11 million|HKD|linked=no|passthrough=yes}}.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=11}} The film was shot in multiple locations across Hong Kong, with some exterior location filming in Taiwan.<ref name="ABTCommentary_ShotInNewTerritoriesAndTaiwan">{{Cite AV media |title=A Better Tomorrow |last=Bey |first=Logan |type=DVD |publisher=Optimum Releasing |time=15:50}}</ref> Due to the film's limited budget, cinematographer Wong Wing-hang had to shoot the nighttime scenes with minimal lighting, as the production could not afford to rent lights.{{Sfn|Hendrix|2015}}

The film shoot was at times emotional for Woo; Cheung recalled that the director would sometimes cry on set.<ref>{{Harvtxt|Havis|1998|p=15}} quoted in {{Harvtxt|Fang|2004|p=33}}</ref> Tsui remembered the production being subject to significant alterations, stating that "everything was on an unstable basis, sort of like walking on a boat—everything is moving, shaking, unbalanced."{{Sfn|Morton|2016|p=64}} When the actor they had cast as Inspector Wu struggled with delivering a suitable performance, Woo decided to step into the role himself, allowing both Chow and Cheung to direct him.<ref name="ChinaNewsService_Zhang2017">{{Cite news |last=Zhang |first=Yiling |author-mask=Zhang Yiling (张义凌) |date=2017-11-09 |title=吴宇森和31岁的《英雄本色》：他至今没有看第二遍 |trans-title=John Woo and the 31-year-old ''A Better Tomorrow'': He to this day has not watched it a second time |url=https://news.sina.com.cn/o/2017-11-09/doc-ifynrsrf3050858.shtml |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251001192157/https://news.sina.com.cn/o/2017-11-09/doc-ifynrsrf3050858.shtml |archive-date=2025-10-01 |access-date=2025-10-01 |work=[[Sina Corporation|Sina]] |language=zh |agency=[[China News Service]]}}</ref>{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=113}} While the director bemoaned his own acting abilities, he believed that the character needed to be in the film "to show that morally things are not always black and white."{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=113}}

One of the most notable changes during filming was Chow's involvement in the movie. The actor was initially slated to be on set for only 10 to 15 days as Mark.{{Sfn|Morton|2016|p=64}} However, as Woo and Chow bonded over their similar values and industry struggles, Woo gradually expanded the actor's supporting role after realizing how capable Chow was at playing Mark.{{Efn|Attributed to multiple sources.<ref name="Vulture_Ebiri2023" /><ref name="YuleSohu_Yi2008">{{Cite web |last=Yi |first=Bao |author-mask=Yi Bao (懿宝) |date=2008-12-19 |title=周润发曾拍片不要片酬 黄百鸣感慨港片衰弱(图)-搜狐娱乐 |trans-title=Chow Yun-fat once made a film without wanting remuneration; Raymond Wong laments the decline of Hong Kong films |url=https://yule.sohu.com/20081219/n261313149_1.shtml |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251001193834/https://yule.sohu.com/20081219/n261313149_1.shtml |archive-date=2025-10-01 |access-date=2025-10-01 |website=[[Sohu]] |language=zh}}</ref><ref name="SMP_Tsui2009">{{Cite web |last=Tsui |first=Clarence |author-link1=Clarence Tsui |date=2009-04-12 |title=Nansun Shi |url=https://www.scmp.com/article/676543/nansun-shi |url-access=subscription |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231105025047/https://www.scmp.com/article/676543/nansun-shi |archive-date=2023-11-05 |access-date=2025-10-04 |website=[[South China Morning Post]] |language=en}}</ref>{{Sfn|Fu|1994}}{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=12}}}} Chow, who had been emotionally connecting with the film's dialogue, was also similarly impressed by the production. Woo wanted to highlight the violence in the film to "make the audiences sense the invisible and widespread power of the underworld".{{Sfn|Heard|2000|p=50}}{{Sfn|Ho|2005|p=266}} Assistant art director Tim Yip used ''[[The Godfather (film series)|The Godfather]]''<nowiki/>'s suave portrayal of the mafia as inspiration.<ref name="TimYip_SCMP_Yau2021" /> The film's gunfights were partially inspired by the continual confrontations between good and evil in [[Western (genre)|Westerns]], as well as the martial arts choreography from ''wuxia'' films.<ref name=":1">{{Harvtxt|Woo|1998|p=55}} quoted in {{Harvtxt|Stokes|Hoover|1999|p=35}}</ref>{{Sfn|Szeto|2011|p=77}} Woo has stated that ''A Better Tomorrow'' was the first time he tried incorporating [[Dual wield|dual wielding]] pistols in a film. Woo wanted his hero to use a handgun because he felt they possessed a "great spirit" to them that was reminiscent of Chinese swords. === Music ===
[[Joseph Koo]] served as ''A Better Tomorrow''<nowiki/>'s composer. He primarily used synthesisers for the background score, writing more dramatic music in the lead up to the action sequences.{{Sfn|Leung|2011|p=50}} Koo also composed the film's theme song, "{{Interlanguage link|In the Sentimental Past|lt=In the Sentimental Past|zh|當年情}}", with lyrics by frequent collaborator [[James Wong Jim]], and sung by Cheung.{{Sfn|Spring|2015|loc=The Synthesizer Score in ''A Better Tomorrow''}} Written in the [[Relative key|relative keys]] of [[E major]] and [[C minor]], it is a melancholic musical piece featuring harmonica, strings, keyboard, drums, and guitar.<ref name="EDBHK_AnalysisOfJosephKoo">{{Cite web |title=Analysis of Joseph Koo's Cantonese Popular Songs |url=https://www.edb.gov.hk/attachment/en/curriculum-development/kla/arts-edu/resources/mus-curri/Joseph-koo-analysis-e.pdf |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250515023021/https://www.edb.gov.hk/attachment/en/curriculum-development/kla/arts-edu/resources/mus-curri/Joseph-koo-analysis-e.pdf |archive-date=2025-05-15 |access-date=2025-10-08 |website=[[Education Bureau]]}}</ref> Lyrically, "In the Sentimental Past" is about a warm reunion between individuals happy to re-express their feelings for each other.{{Sfn|Van den Troost|2024b|p=241}} Scholar Julian Stringer described "In the Sentimental Past" as having a "lilting, haunting melody" that "denotes pain, suffering and emotional trauma".{{Sfn|Stringer|1997|p=31}} While Koo used the song as a [[Motif (music)|musical motif]] at key emotional points throughout the movie, the full version with lyrics is played at the film's conclusion.{{Sfn|Stringer|1997|p=31}}{{Sfn|Leung|2011|pp=51–52}}

== Release ==

=== Context ===
[[Martial arts film|Martial arts films]] became popular throughout Hong Kong in the 1960s and 1970s, with the [[kung fu film]] eclipsing the popularity of the ''wuxia'' film throughout the 1970s.{{Sfn|Cheuk|2008|p=87}}{{Sfn|Szeto|2011|p=25}} However, by the early 1980s, local audiences began to grow tired of the traditional period pieces that were typical of the genre.<ref name="ABT_SCMP_Havis2021">{{Cite web |last=Havis |first=Richard James |date=2021-10-31 |title=Like Martial Arts Movies But with Guns: How John Woo's ''A Better Tomorrow'' Launched Action-Film Era in Hong Kong, and the ''Wuxia'' and Kung Fu Roots of Hits Like ''The Killer'' and ''City on Fire'' |url=https://www.scmp.com/lifestyle/entertainment/article/3154117/martial-arts-movies-guns-how-john-woos-better-tomorrow |url-access=subscription |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211030221956/https://www.scmp.com/lifestyle/entertainment/article/3154117/martial-arts-movies-guns-how-john-woos-better-tomorrow |archive-date=2021-10-30 |access-date=2022-04-21 |website=[[South China Morning Post]] |language=en}}</ref>{{Sfn|Yip|2017|p=187}} Instead, comedic movies saw mainstream success throughout the decade, often ranking in the top 10 of the highest-grossing films for that year.{{Sfn|Chu|2003|p=68}} By 1986, several popular comedy films, such as ''[[Mr. Vampire]]'' (1985) and ''[[Twinkle, Twinkle, Lucky Stars]]'' (1985) had been produced. While some movies like ''[[Yes, Madam (1985 film)|Yes, Madam]]'' (1985) and ''[[In the Line of Duty (film series)|Royal Warriors]]'' (1986) involved police work, their focus on [[girls with guns]] meant that they did not examine brotherly relationships like ''A Better Tomorrow'' did.{{Sfn|Hall|2012a|p=96}}

Because of those audience trends, Cinema City was not expecting the movie to perform well financially.{{Sfn|Aufderheide|1987|p=74}} Tsui recalled that unhappy studio executives asked him to "burn the film" midway through production.{{Sfn|Rizov|2014}} As a result, ''A Better Tomorrow'' was minimally advertised in Hong Kong.<ref name="BFI_Bittel2019">{{Cite web |last=Bitel |first=Anton |date=2019-07-10 |title=Heroic Bloodshed: How Hong Kong's Style Was Swiped by Hollywood |url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/heroic-bloodshed-hong-kong-hollywood-cycle-influence |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260303234204/https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/heroic-bloodshed-hong-kong-hollywood-cycle-influence |archive-date=2026-03-03 |access-date=2026-03-03 |website=[[British Film Institute]] |language=en}}</ref> The studio was also concerned with Chow's poor box office reputation: unlike the other actors, his portrait was not included in the film's publicity stills, and he received third billing in the credits behind Ti and Cheung.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=12}} Despite Cinema City's misgivings however, both action movies and crime dramas had risen in popularity with local audiences. The realistically gritty ''[[Long Arm of the Law (film)|Long Arm of the Law]]'' (1984) had won multiple [[Hong Kong Film Award|Hong Kong Film Awards]], and Cinema City's own action-oriented ''[[Aces Go Places (film series)|Aces Go Places]]'' films had performed well at the box office.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=10}} In addition, the kung fu film genre had already started to modernise to cater to audience tastes. As evidenced by the success of movies like [[Jackie Chan]]'s ''[[Police Story (1985 film)|Police Story]]'' (1985), directors were transitioning to making action films set in contemporary Hong Kong, with fight scenes containing gunplay and [[hand-to-hand combat]] instead of swordfighting.{{Sfn|Law|Bren|2004|p=286}}{{Sfn|Needham|2012|p= 25}}

''A Better Tomorrow'' was released in Hong Kong on 2 August 1986.{{Sfn|Morton|2016|p=60}} First shown at a midnight sneak preview, its initial screening received an extremely positive audience reception.{{Sfn|Dannen|Long|1997|p=42}} Woo recalled that "the people went crazy" while watching the film,{{Sfn|Dannen|Long|1997|p=42}} and Chow noted that "there was shouting and clapping of hands, which is not something that happens in Hong Kong movies."{{Sfn|Heard|2000|pp=209–210}} The film went on to become the number one movie of 1986, grossing {{HKDConvert|34651324|lk=on|year=1986|to=USD|force=yes|exact=on|showdate=no}} during its exhibition.{{Sfn|Morton|2016|p=62}} While the average theatrical run in Hong Kong is normally brief, lasting one to three weeks depending on a film's popularity, ''A Better Tomorrow'' ran for a then-unheard of sixty-one days in theatres.{{Sfn|Bordwell|2011|p=72}}

The film's release was rolled out across Asia over the course of the year. In Taiwan, it was released on 8 August 1986, selling 249,468 tickets across 128 screens until its last showing on 31 December of that year. == Reception ==

=== Critical response ===
''A Better Tomorrow'' was released in Hong Kong to general acclaim by critics. Karen Fang states that contemporary reviews praised the film for being "explosive but sentimental" and "full of masculinity",<ref>{{cite news |last=Sek |first=Kei |author-link=Sek Kei |date=1986-08-30 |others=Translated by Edward Leung |title=Film Teahouse |trans-title= |url= |access-date= |work=[[Ming Pao]] |language=zh}} Quoted in {{harvtxt|Fang|2004|pp=|p=37}}</ref> with [[David Bordwell]] remarking that most Hong Kong critics believed ''A Better Tomorrow'' to be Woo's best work.{{Sfn|Bordwell|2011|p=93}} According to Jenny Lau of ''[[Journal of Cinema and Media Studies|Cinema Journal]]'', local reviewers liked the film not necessarily for its violence, but more for its successful adaptation of traditional ''wuxia'' tropes and action sequences.{{Sfn|Lau|1998|p=21}} Hong Kong critic [[Li Cheuk-to]] credited ''A Better Tomorrow''<nowiki/>'s success to its more thoroughly developed story, consistent melodramatic tone, and stylised violence. He also suggested that the heroic protagonists resonated with audiences feeling politically anxious over the upcoming [[handover of Hong Kong]].{{Sfn|Lent|1990|p=114}}

Over the years, English-language critics were generally positive about the film. [[Glenn Kenny]] of ''[[Entertainment Weekly]]'' said that American audiences would find ''A Better Tomorrow'' a welcome respite from most of the generic action movies made by Hollywood.{{Sfn|Kenny|1994}} A reviewer for ''Variety'' described the movie as a "fine vehicle" for Woo, calling it a significant departure from the comedies that he had previously directed.{{Sfn|Variety|1985}} With many of the reviews being retrospectively written after the international success of Woo's later films, several critics chose to compare and contrast ''A Better Tomorrow'' with ''[[The Killer (1989 film)|The Killer]]'' (1989) and ''[[Hard Boiled]]'' (1992). Alan Morrison of ''[[Empire (magazine)|Empire]]'' said that many of Woo's stylistic hallmarks were present in the film, albeit in a rudimentary form.{{Sfn|Morrison|2000}} Both Keith Phipps of ''[[The A.V. Club]]'' and Chris Gramlich of ''[[Exclaim!]]'' preferred watching ''The Killer'' and ''Hard Boiled'',<ref name="AVClub_Phipps2002">{{Cite web |last=Phipps |first=Keith |date=2002-04-19 |title=''A Better Tomorrow'' |url=https://www.avclub.com/a-better-tomorrow-1798197349 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210730083903/https://www.avclub.com/a-better-tomorrow-1798197349 |archive-date=2021-07-30 |access-date=2022-05-04 |website=[[The A.V. Club]] |language=en-US}}</ref>{{Sfn|Gramlich|2004}} with Gramlich explaining that he felt Woo was still trying to refine his approach with ''A Better Tomorrow''.{{Sfn|Gramlich|2004}} ''[[The Austin Chronicle]]''<nowiki/>'s Marc Savlov said that while the movie was not as polished as ''The Killer'', nor as complex as ''[[Bullet in the Head]]'' (1990), it was still a good representation of Woo's filmmaking style. He also believed that ''A Better Tomorrow'' was essential viewing for anyone interested in Hong Kong cinema.<ref name="AustinChronicle_Savlov1993">{{Cite web |last=Savlov |first=Marc |date=1993-03-05 |title=Movie Review: ''A Better Tomorrow'' |url=https://www.austinchronicle.com/events/film/1993-03-05/a-better-tomorrow/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250816150654/https://www.austinchronicle.com/events/film/1993-03-05/a-better-tomorrow/ |archive-date=2025-08-16 |access-date=2025-10-02 |website=[[The Austin Chronicle]] |language=en-US}}</ref>

Johnathan Crow of ''[[AllMovie]]'' praised Woo's "baroque" visual style. He stated that the [[Tracking shot|tracking shots]], [[fast cutting]], and [[slow motion]] explosions helped deliver an emotional, melodramatic experience that elevated the film beyond traditional action cinema.<ref name="AllMovie_ABTReview_Crow">{{Cite web |last=Crow |first=Johnathan |title=''A Better Tomorrow'' Review |url=http://www.allmovie.com/movie/a-better-tomorrow-v5168/review |url-status=deviated |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230406150246/http://www.allmovie.com/movie/a-better-tomorrow-v5168/review |archive-date=2023-04-06 |access-date=2025-10-02 |website=[[AllMovie]]}}</ref> A ''[[TV Guide]]'' reviewer echoed this sentiment, commenting that Woo's clear, purposeful direction made each new [[plot point]] seem like an unavoidable outcome.<ref name="TVGuideReview">{{Cite web |title=''A Better Tomorrow'' |url=https://www.tvguide.com/movies/a-better-tomorrow/2030129630/ |url-status=deviated |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231105091350/https://www.tvguide.com/movies/a-better-tomorrow/review/2030129630/ |archive-date=2023-11-05 |access-date=2025-10-02 |website=[[TV Guide]] |language=en}}</ref> Both Morrison and Kenny liked the final shootout; Kenny said that it was "one of the most ridiculously exhilarating—or exhilaratingly ridiculous—sequences of its kind."{{Sfn|Kenny|1994}} Writing for the ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'', [[Kevin Thomas (film critic)|Kevin Thomas]] said that he enjoyed the action and likeable characters, believing the film was a hallmark of Hong Kong cinema.<ref name="LATimes_Thomas1993">{{Cite news |last=Thomas |first=Kevin |author-link=Kevin Thomas (film critic) |date=1993-11-24 |title=Movie Review: Woo's ''Tomorrow'' Balances Emotion and Violence |url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1993-11-24-ca-60201-story.html |url-access=subscription |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251002170544/https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1993-11-24-ca-60201-story.html |archive-date=2025-10-02 |access-date=2025-10-02 |website=[[Los Angeles Times]] |language=en-US}}</ref> Thomas also liked Koo's music, writing that the sentimental score was a great match for the film's visual energy.<ref name="LATimes_Thomas1993" /> In contrast, ''Variety'' criticised the melodrama, finding the movie "contrived" and full of "overdone violence".{{Sfn|Variety|1985}} Gramlich said that too much emphasis was placed on an overly-complex story containing shoddy dialogue.{{Sfn|Gramlich|2004}} 

Many critics praised Chow's performance as Mark. David Chute of ''[[Film Comment]]'' believed that Chow gave a "mesmerizing lead performance" that evoked [[Robert Mitchum]], Ken Takakura and Steve McQueen.{{Sfn|Chute|1987|p=71}} ''[[Rolling Stone]]''<nowiki/>'s Peter Kenis commended the film for making Chow "the ultimate cool-as-fuck Asian badass".{{Sfn|Kenis|2004}} Morrison said that the actor stole the show,{{Sfn|Morrison|2000}} a sentiment also shared by Phipps.<ref name="AVClub_Phipps2002" /> Conversely, ''Variety'' dismissed Chow and Cheung's performances as overacting. However, they praised Ti's "silent masculine presence" throughout the film, and appreciated the actor's understated vulnerability in his role as Ho.{{Sfn|Variety|1985}} ''TV Guide'' also believed Ti expressed the internal struggle of his character convincingly, and found Waise Lee's portrayal of Shing a standout.<ref name="TVGuideReview" /> Thomas singled out Woo's ability to get strong acting performances from his cast.<ref name="LATimes_Thomas1993" />
=== Accolades ===
''A Better Tomorrow'' won two awards at the [[6th Hong Kong Film Awards]]: [[Hong Kong Film Award for Best Film|Best Film]] and [[Hong Kong Film Award for Best Actor|Best Actor]] (Chow).{{Sfn|Morton|2016|p=62}} It also earned nine further nominations for [[Hong Kong Film Award for Best Director|Best Director]] (Woo), Best Actor (Ti), [[Hong Kong Film Award for Best Supporting Actor|Best Supporting Actor]] (Lee), [[Hong Kong Film Award for Best Screenplay|Best Screenplay]] (Woo), [[Hong Kong Film Award for Best Art Direction|Best Art Direction]] (Bennie Lui), [[Hong Kong Film Award for Best Cinematography|Best Cinematography]] (Wong Wing-hang), [[Hong Kong Film Award for Best Editing|Best Film Editing]] (Ma Kam), [[Hong Kong Film Award for Best New Performer|Best New Performer]] (Lee), and [[Hong Kong Film Award for Best Original Film Score|Best Original Film Score]] (Koo).<ref name="HKFAA_6thAwardsWinners">{{Cite web |title=香港電影金像獎 |trans-title=List of nominees and awardees of the 6th Hong Kong Film Awards |url=http://www.hkfaa.com/winnerlist06.html |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250813185547/https://www.hkfaa.com/winnerlist06.html |archive-date=2025-08-13 |access-date=2025-10-02 |website=[[Hong Kong Film Awards]] |language=zh}}</ref>

At the [[23rd Golden Horse Awards]], the film won the awards for [[Golden Horse Award for Best Director|Best Director]] (Woo), [[Golden Horse Award for Best Leading Actor|Best Leading Actor]] (Ti), [[Golden Horse Award for Best Cinematography|Best Cinematography]] (Wong), and [[Golden Horse Award for Best Sound Effects|Best Sound Effects]]. In addition to Chow also receiving a nomination for Best Leading Actor, the film was further nominated for [[Golden Horse Award for Best Narrative Feature|Best Feature Film]], [[Golden Horse Award for Best Supporting Actor|Best Supporting Actor]] (Lee), [[Golden Horse Award for Best Film Editing|Best Film Editing]] (Ma), and [[Golden Horse Award for Best Original Film Score|Best Original Music]] (Koo).<ref name="GoldenHorse_23rdAwardsWinners">{{Cite web |title=Taipei Golden Horse Film Festival Nominees and Winners |url=https://www.goldenhorse.org.tw/awards/nw/?serach_type=flim&sc=8&search_regist_year=1986&ins=22 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251002171121/https://www.goldenhorse.org.tw/awards/nw/?serach_type=flim&sc=8&search_regist_year=1986&ins=22 |archive-date=2025-10-02 |access-date=2025-10-02 |website=[[Golden Horse Film Festival and Awards|Taipei Golden Horse Film Festival]] |language=zh}}</ref>

''A Better Tomorrow'' is a modern adaptation of ''wuxia'' films, combining chivalric Chinese traditions with contemporary gunplay.{{Sfn|Fang|2010|p=562}}{{Sfn|Lam|2016}} Woo takes inspiration from the partially [[Confucianism|Confucian]] moral codes of ''wuxia''<nowiki/>'s swordsmen characters (''[[youxia]]''),{{Sfn|Soe|2019|p=464}} and the long-standing rituals of the triads, which are rooted in [[Taoism]].{{Sfn|Soe|2019|p=464}}{{Sfn|Nochimson|2007|p=72}} Ho and Mark are portrayed as heroic outlaws who operate outside of the boundaries of mainstream society, like their ''wuxia'' counterparts living in the ''[[jianghu]]''.{{Sfn|Ho|2014|pp=91–92}} They adhere to a conceptual "system of brotherhood, honour and justice" called ''[[Yi (philosophy)|yi]]'' ({{Lang-zh|c={{linktext|義}}|l=righteousness|labels=no|j=ji6}}), and swear by a personal code of loyalty called ''yi qi'' ({{Lang-zh|c={{linktext|義氣}}|l=breath of yi|labels=no|j=ji6 hei3}}).{{Sfn|Teo|1997|pp=175–176}}{{Sfn|Lam|2016}}{{Sfn|Stokes|Hoover|1999|p=42}} Conversely, Shing follows what Lisa Odham Stokes and Michael Hoover define as choosing ''jing'' ({{Lang-zh|c={{linktext|精}}|l=essence|labels=no|j=zing1}}) over ''qing'' ({{Lang-zh|c={{linktext|情}}|l=feeling|labels=no|j=cing4}}). Unlike Ho and Mark, who possess the chivalrous trait of having empathy for others (''qing''), Shing is willing to betray his brotherhood because he values caring for his physical being (''jing'') first.{{Sfn|Stokes|Hoover|1999|pp=40, 47–48}}
[[File:Accademia - Pietà Martinengo by Giovanni Bellini Cat.883.jpg|left|thumb|The ''[[Martinengo Pietà]]'' ({{Circa|1505}}) by [[Giovanni Bellini]]. Mark's death evokes this and other versions of the [[Pietà]], an artistic representation of the [[Lamentation of Christ]] featuring the [[Mary, mother of Jesus|Virgin Mary]].]]
To show the similarities between biological and triad brotherhood, Woo introduces mirrored relationships between the characters. Ho is the mature older brother of Kit, a [[blood brother]] to Mark, and a trusted mentor to Shing.{{Sfn|Hall|2012a|p=71}}{{Sfn|Chen|2022|p=223}} These parallels in kinship are reinforced through language: Mark is deferentially addressed with the honorific ''gor'' ({{Lang-zh|c={{linktext|哥}}|labels=no|l=older brother}}), and characters use the phrase ''dai lo'' ({{Lang-zh|c={{linktext|大佬}}|l=big brother|labels=no}}) to refer to their triad superiors.{{Sfn|Lam|2016}}{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=24}} Kenneth E. Hall said that the film's primary conflicts revolve around [[filial piety]]. Ho's elder brother status implies that he is not just required to respect his father, but also assume the position as the patriarch ({{Lang-zh|c={{linktext|家長}}|j=gaa1 zoeng2|labels=no|l=head of the household}}) if his father dies.{{Sfn|Hall|2008|p=246}} However, both Kit and Shing reject their own duty of filial piety towards their respective elder brothers. Kit's hostility stems from both personal resentment over Ho's presumed involvement in their father's death, as well as occupational frustration over being blocked from promotion.{{Sfn|Hall|2008|pp=246–247}} Shing meanwhile, double-crosses Ho for his own gain.{{Sfn|Lam|2016}} These parallel violations of ''yi qi'' are presented through repeated scenes and symmetrical [[Shot (filmmaking)|shot]] compositions, with Mark commonly used as the axis for [[Cut (transition)|cuts]] to highlight his importance as the film's emotional centre.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=15}} Stephen Teo wrote that Mark "represents an aestheticised model of the ''wuxia'' hero-cum-contemporary killer."{{Sfn|Teo|2011|p=162}} Despite becoming crippled, he draws upon the strength of his brotherly friendship with Ho to overcome his disability and transform into a heroic figure.{{Sfn|Nochimson|2007|pp=81–82}} Mark's devotion to the triad code compels him to return and help Ho in the final firefight, and he displays his heroism in his plea to Kit to remember his fraternal loyalty, pushing Kit out of the way of Shing's gunfire.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|pp=28–29}} When Mark dies, Woo employs the [[Christian symbolism|Christian imagery]] of a [[pietà]] to associate his death with [[Jesus]]. This religious reference highlights Mark as both a victim and hero who sacrifices themselves to rekindle Ho and Kit's relationship, and thus restore a world of traditional values and [[homosociality]].{{Sfn|Van den Troost|2024b|pp=239–241}}{{Sfn|Szeto|2011|p=87}} Likewise, Ho gives up his freedom by pressuring Kit to arrest him, knowing that his brother's career will be salvaged by bringing him to justice.{{Sfn|Gates|2001|p=64}}

Some authors have discussed the film's usage of [[Melodrama (film genre)|melodrama]], and how its portrayal of brotherhood has been interpreted by some Western audiences as [[Homoeroticism|homoerotic]].{{Sfn|Williams|1995|p=48}}{{Sfn|Reynaud|2000|p=62}}{{Sfn|Sandell|2001a}} According to Julian Stringer, Woo's combination of action and melodrama produces a depiction of masculinity distinct from American films.{{Efn|Stringer uses academic Geoffrey Nowell-Smith's suggestion that Hollywood  segregated its movie releases into two specific types. The masculine "doing" genres would encompass men taking action, such as the [[Western film]] and the [[War film|war film]]. Conversely, the feminine "suffering" genres, such as the [[Woman's film|woman's film]] and the [[Melodrama (film genre)|melodrama]], would depict female agency being impaired.<ref>{{Harvard citation text|Nowell-Smith|1987|pp=70–74}} quoted in {{Harvard citation text|Stringer|1997|p=29}}</ref>}} In Woo's "male melodramas", men participate in both violence and heroism, while also experiencing intense sadness and loss.{{Sfn|Stringer|1997|pp=29–30}}{{Sfn|Choi|2010a|pp=67⁠–68}} Ho's agency in ''A Better Tomorrow'' is impacted by familial duties; he is reluctant to seek revenge on Shing because he wants to regain Kit's trust, as well as to honour his promise to his father of leaving the triads.{{Sfn|Choi|2010a|pp=67⁠–68}} Woo also differs from American action films in that he avoids showing physically sculpted bodies. Instead, he prefers to showcase both "stylish clothes and accessories" as well as male emotional vulnerability, presented as intimate gazes between men.{{Sfn|Lu|2011|p=108}}{{Sfn|Stringer|1997|p=32}} Fang stated that this level of closeness, shown in Ho's post-prison reunion with Mark, is usually only reserved in Hollywood action films for impending character deaths.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=24}} Jillian Sandell wrote that these moments underscore the importance of male bonding within the triad code of honour, allowing Ho and Mark to foster a connection that is both "emotionally and materially fulfilling" despite their bleak circumstances.{{Sfn|Sandell|2001a}} Stringer argued that this depiction is not inherently homosexual, since it represses sexuality instead of foregrounding it.{{Sfn|Stringer|1997|p=32}} Mikel Koven believed that interpreting Woo's gangster films as homoerotic misreads how the Hong Kong audience sees the male relationships.{{Sfn|Koven|1997|p=55}} Woo has echoed this sentiment, stating, "In my culture there is no hiding. If we need to cry, we cry. If you need to hug someone you do it whether they are your lover or your friend."{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=124}}
=== Societal change ===
''A Better Tomorrow'' has been interpreted as commentary on the disappearance of tradition with societal change. Woo was spurred to direct the film out of concern that traditional values in Hong Kong were being eroded by modernisation.{{Sfn|Woo|2014|p=145}}{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=10}} Caleb Kelso-March commented that the film portrays "conditions of capitalist modernity" found in Hong Kong at the time, such as the spread of organised crime and financial uncertainty.{{Sfn|Kelso-March|2020|p=57}} Mark and Ho are depicted as men of humble beginnings who have risen the ranks of a capitalist society by becoming successful counterfeiters of American currency.{{Sfn|Ho|2014|pp=90–91}}{{Sfn|Vesia|2002}} {{ill|Sam Ho|zh|何思穎 (香港影評人)}} remarked that the code of brotherhood that Mark and Ho observe allows for illegal activities like counterfeiting, since mainstream Hong Kong society is not directly impacted by the forging of foreign currency. Shing's rise to power, however, hurts on multiple fronts: his betrayal erodes the traditional values of the criminal underworld, and his move into drug trafficking directly harms Hong Kong civilians.{{Sfn|Ho|2014|pp=93–94}}{{Sfn|Lam|2016}} According to Karen Fang, Shing's ability to use money and power to escape consequence highlights the "degradation of justice in modern society."{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=30}}

Jillian Sandell described the three protagonists as "loner heroes" who become marginalised from their respective organisations. Kit's policeman career is stalled by his brother's criminal affiliations, and Ho and Mark's position in the triad is upended when Shing usurps their power and status. Ho tries to legitimise himself by joining a taxi company run by ex-convicts, but he ultimately fails to "live outside the institutions available within Hong Kong capitalism" once Shing's subordinates ransack the company.{{Sfn|Sandell|2001a}} Sandell said that Mark represents the "supreme loner" whose code of honour causes him to reject any organisation; this lack of affiliation spells his inevitable death. According to her, Mark symbolically dies from fatigue instead of literal injury, since his kind of heroism is a "throwback to China's past with no place in contemporary Hong Kong".{{Sfn|Sandell|2001a}}
[[File:Guangdong 04780019 (8389262688).jpg|thumb|The [[Daya Bay Nuclear Power Plant]] in 2007]]
While Woo has maintained that ''A Better Tomorrow'' was not intended as political commentary,{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=118}} various scholars have viewed the film as a meditation on the anxieties surrounding the impending 1997 handover of Hong Kong.<ref name="HKFilmCritics_Chan2010">{{Cite journal |last=Chan |first=Ga-ming |author-mask=Chan Ga-ming (陳嘉銘) |date=November 2010 |title=十大香港電影 － 第二位：《英雄本色》（1986） |trans-title=Top 10 Hong Kong movies – #2: ''A Better Tomorrow'' (1986) |url=https://www.filmcritics.org.hk/film-review/node/2015/07/01/%E5%8D%81%E5%A4%A7%E9%A6%99%E6%B8%AF%E9%9B%BB%E5%BD%B1-%EF%BC%8D-%E7%AC%AC%E4%BA%8C%E4%BD%8D%EF%BC%9A%E3%80%8A%E8%8B%B1%E9%9B%84%E6%9C%AC%E8%89%B2%E3%80%8B%EF%BC%881986%EF%BC%89 |url-status=live |journal=HKinema |language=zh |publisher=[[Hong Kong Film Critics Society]] |issue=12 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251003214154/https://www.filmcritics.org.hk/film-review/node/2015/07/01/%E5%8D%81%E5%A4%A7%E9%A6%99%E6%B8%AF%E9%9B%BB%E5%BD%B1-%EF%BC%8D-%E7%AC%AC%E4%BA%8C%E4%BD%8D%EF%BC%9A%E3%80%8A%E8%8B%B1%E9%9B%84%E6%9C%AC%E8%89%B2%E3%80%8B%EF%BC%881986%EF%BC%89 |archive-date=2025-10-03 |access-date=2025-10-03}}</ref>{{Sfn|Abbas|2002|p=24}}{{Sfn|Sandell|2001a}} According to Tony Williams, Mark's dialogue about Hong Kong is an example of this uneasiness: after reflecting on the region's nighttime beauty with Ho, Mark grimly states, "Like most things, it won't last. That's for sure."{{Sfn|Williams|1997|pp=147–148}} Kin-yan Szeto said that the marginalised characters form bonds over this uncertainty, equating Ho's struggle between loyalty and the law with Hong Kong's sense of identity between the United Kingdom and China.{{Sfn|Szeto|2011|p=78}} Film critic Li Cheuk-to suggested that the film's popularity in Hong Kong might have stemmed from its role as an outlet for frustration over the August 1986 construction of the nearby [[Daya Bay Nuclear Power Plant]] in Guangdong. Li said that local audiences, concerned over the environmental impacts of nuclear power as well as Hong Kong's lack of autonomy over the plant's construction, empathised with Mark's feelings on personally fighting to "regain what we have lost".{{Sfn|Li|1994|p=174}}

=== Cinematic homage and meta-reference ===
According to Woo, the world in ''A Better Tomorrow'' is an amalgamation of both his personal memories and his love of cinema.{{Sfn|Ciecko|1997|p=223}} Film historian [[Po Fung]] said that the film's narrative structure contains similarities to Chang Cheh's ''[[The Duel (1971 film)|The Duel]]'' (1971),<ref name="HKFCS_Po2014">{{Cite web |last=Po |first=Fung |author-link=Po Fung |date=2014-04-15 |title=大決鬥和《英雄本色》的變奏 |trans-title=The Duel and variations of ''A Better Tomorrow'' |url=https://www.filmcritics.org.hk/film-review/node/2015/07/13/大決鬥和《英雄本色》的變奏 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251003214949/https://www.filmcritics.org.hk/film-review/node/2015/07/13/%E5%A4%A7%E6%B1%BA%E9%AC%A5%E5%92%8C%E3%80%8A%E8%8B%B1%E9%9B%84%E6%9C%AC%E8%89%B2%E3%80%8B%E7%9A%84%E8%AE%8A%E5%A5%8F |archive-date=2025-10-03 |access-date=2025-10-03 |website=[[Hong Kong Film Critics Society]] |language=zh}}</ref> with Magnan-Park observing that Mark's self-sacrifice mirrors the martyrdom endings in Chang's films.{{Sfn|Magnan-Park|2007|p=42}} Kenneth E. Hall also likened the movie to Chang's ''[[The One-Armed Swordsman|One Armed Swordsman]]'' series due to their shared focus on familial relations.{{Sfn|Hall|2008|p=244}} Within the Shaw Brothers catalogue, ''A Better Tomorrow'' bears some resemblance to ''[[The Brothers (1979 film)|The Brothers]]'' (1979) directed by Hua Shan ({{Lang-zh|c=華山|labels=no}}).<ref name="BFI_Bittel2019" /> Li Cheuk-to further identified ''wuxia'' films directed by [[Chor Yuen]] and written by [[Gu Long]] as additional possible references.{{Sfn|Li|1994|p=176}} Anne T. Ciecko said that the film shares a relationship with Japanese ''[[Samurai cinema|chambara]]'' films,{{Sfn|Ciecko|2006|p=171}} and Tony Williams highlighted the influence of [[Yakuza film|yakuza films]] on Woo's work, as they both involve "similar conflicts involving ancient loyalty and modern values".{{Sfn|Williams|1997|p=139}} Both Kristof Van den Troost and Karen Fang note that the film's iconic shot of Mark lighting a cigarette with a flaming bank note is similar to a sequence in [[Ng See-yuen]]'s ''[[Anti-Corruption (film)|Anti-Corruption]]'' (1975).{{Sfn|Van den Troost|2017|p=58}}{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=16}}

Woo also borrows liberally from non-Asian cinema. From [[French New Wave]] films, he adopted the costuming of Alain Delon in Jean-Pierre Melville's ''Le Samouraï''. Fang said that these cinematic inclusions "foreground the film's stylisation and demonstrate how [Woo's] own film self-consciously pursues the 'coolness' that Woo constantly praises."{{Sfn|Fang|2004|pp=14–15}} Woo also looked at American cinema for inspiration: Mark's slow-motion restaurant entrance was a nod to [[Martin Scorsese]]'s ''[[Mean Streets]]'' (1973).<ref name="ScreenAnarchy_Murray2016" /> He was also heavily influenced by [[Arthur Penn]]'s romanticised depiction of the deaths of the titular characters in ''[[Bonnie and Clyde (film)|Bonnie and Clyde]]'' (1967), and sought to recreate that feeling for Mark's death.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|pp=115–116}} Fang believed that the film superficially shares some of the aesthetics of [[film noir]], such as its [[low-key lighting]] and pessimistic outlook, but differs in its hopeful, redemptive ending where Ho handcuffs himself to Kit.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|pp=30–31}}

Hall said that ''A Better Tomorrow'' can be interpreted as an allegorical, semi-autobiographical work referencing Woo's own feelings and experiences.{{Sfn|Hall|2012a|pp=73–74}} Like Ho and Mark, whose good fortunes run out after travelling to Taiwan, Woo experienced major dissatisfaction over being sent there by Cinema City to oversee its regional office. He also stayed in Taiwan for roughly the same amount of time as Ho's three-year prison sentence.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=20}}{{Sfn|Hall|2012a|p=74}} Ho and Mark's friendship can thus be read as an allegory for Tsui Hark's support of Woo.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=10}} The inclusion of Ken, who helps Ho reintegrate into society by giving him work, parallels Tsui rescuing Woo's career by letting him return to Hong Kong to direct ''A Better Tomorrow''.{{Sfn|Hall|2012a|pp=74–75}}

In an idea mirroring Patrick Lung's own acting role in ''The Story of a Discharged Prisoner'', Woo casts himself as a police inspector. This cameo can also be viewed as self-reflexive, in that Woo's character is shown gradually understanding—and even admiring—the gangsters over time.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|pp=19–20}} In an interview, Woo stated, "I always believe that if you want to catch a thief you have to think like him and act like him, first. Then you can make your judgement."{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=113}} Fang drew similarities between the cameo appearances of Woo, [[Woody Allen]], and [[Alfred Hitchcock]] in their respective films; these acting roles indicate authorial control of their work.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=20}} This self-referential understanding is further deepened by Woo's decision to let the cast direct his acting; such a collaborative role reversal reinforces the film's themes of brotherhood and having empathy for others.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|pp=20–21}}

== Legacy ==
The film's box office success positively impacted the careers of many of its participants. It turned both Woo and Chow into superstars in Asia, and helped forge a partnership in which Chow would star in many of the director's following films.{{Sfn|Donovan|2014|p=181}}{{Sfn|Hampton|1998}} Woo was granted more creative freedom for his projects,{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=44}} and some of his shelved films, like ''[[Heroes Shed No Tears (1986 film)|Heroes Shed No Tears]]'' (1986), were released to capitalise on his newfound popularity.{{Sfn|Elder|2005b|p=43}} It would also lead to a decades-long work relationship between Woo and Terence Chang, who would produce several of Woo's films in the future.{{Sfn|Hall|2012b|p=103}}

Chow's performance in the film turned him into a local icon,<ref name="Sina_Wen2009">{{Cite web |last=Wen |first=Shenting |author-mask=Wen Shenting (文沈汀) |date=2009-08-31 |title=香港黑帮电影：审美化的江湖 |trans-title=Hong Kong gangster films: The aestheticization of the ''jianghu'' |url=https://news.sina.com.cn/c/sd/2009-08-31/114218547667_5.shtml |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090904015435/http://news.sina.com.cn/c/sd/2009-08-31/114218547667_5.shtml |archive-date=2009-09-04 |access-date=2022-05-13 |website=Southern Metropolis Weekly |publisher=Sina |page=5 |language=zh}}</ref>{{Sfn|Morton|2016|p=61}} with him becoming known as "Brother Mark" in Hong Kong for years.{{Sfn|Leung|2011|p=50}} As a result of the movie's success, Chow began to receive a barrage of acting opportunities, enabling him to be cast in almost three dozen films from 1986 to 1989. Many of these movies became Hong Kong box-office successes, such as ''[[Prison on Fire]]'', ''[[Tragic Hero (film)|Tragic Hero]]'', and ''[[An Autumn's Tale]]'' (all 1987 and the third, fourth, and fifth highest-grossing films that year respectively), ''[[The Eighth Happiness]]'' (highest-grossing film of 1988), and ''[[God of Gamblers]]'' (highest-grossing movie of 1989).{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=42}}

Ti and Cheung also benefited from the film's success. His career rejuvenated, Ti would star alongside Chow in other Cinema City films throughout the late 1980s, such as ''[[Tiger on the Beat]]'' and ''[[City War]]'' (both 1988).{{Sfn|Williams|2012|p=41}} === Cultural influence ===
Upon its release, the film sparked avid enthusiasm amongst Hong Kong audiences. Influenced by the movie's preoccupation with honour and loyalty, they began using the [[honorific]] "gor" ({{Lang-zh|c={{linktext|哥}}|labels=no}}) in casual slang to address each other.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|p=39}} Young men in Hong Kong began trying to emulate Mark by wearing long coats and sunglasses, despite the local humidity.{{Sfn|Donovan|2014|p=181}}{{Sfn|Bliss|2002|p=29}} This fashion trend caused Alain Delon's branded sunglasses to sell out in Asia, spurring the actor to write a thank-you letter to Woo.<ref name="SCMP_Chan2010">{{Cite news |last=Chan |first=Tessa |date=2010-09-10 |title=Bad Boy Charm |url=https://www.scmp.com/article/724321/bad-boy-charm |url-access=subscription |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250604085447/https://www.scmp.com/article/724321/bad-boy-charm |archive-date=2025-06-04 |access-date=2025-06-21 |work=[[South China Morning Post]]|url-status=live}}</ref> The long coat also subsequently began to be colloquially known as a "Mark coat" ({{Lang-zh|c=Mark哥褸|j=Mark go1 lau5}}) in reference to the character.{{Sfn|Donovan|2014|p=181}}{{Sfn|Bliss|2002|p=29}}

arts action films in the past."<ref name=":2">{{Cite news |last=Sek |first=Kei |author-link=Sek Kei |date=1986-08-30 |others=Translated by Edward Leung |title=Film Teahouse |work=[[Ming Pao]]}} Quoted == Sequels, prequels and spin-offs ==
The success of ''A Better Tomorrow'' spawned two more movies. ''[[A Better Tomorrow II]]'' (1987) was a narrative sequel that followed Ho and Kit as they investigate Ho's former boss, Lung.{{Sfn|Stokes|Hoover|1999|p=48}} To take advantage of Chow's newfound superstardom—despite his character's death in the first film—the actor was recast as Mark's twin brother, Ken.{{Sfn|Bordwell|2011|p=204}} Woo and Tsui's creative disagreements throughout its production badly strained their friendship.{{Sfn|Hall|2012a|p=77}} ''A Better Tomorrow II'' performed well at the box office, earning {{Currency|23 million|HKD|passthrough=yes|linked=no}} and becoming the sixth highest-grossing film that year.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|pp=44–45}}

The fractured partnership between Woo and Tsui led to Woo not returning as director for the next film in the series. After collaborating on ''The Killer'', Woo directed ''Bullet in the Head'' without Tsui's involvement, basing his movie off of a script he had intended as a prequel to ''A Better Tomorrow''.<ref name="ScreenAnarchy_Murray2016" />{{Sfn|Hall|2012a|p=77}} Tsui would instead direct his own prequel film, ''[[A Better Tomorrow III: Love & Death in Saigon]]'' (1989). This depicted Mark's trip to Saigon during the [[Vietnam War]] to bring his cousin and uncle back to Hong Kong.{{Sfn|Fang|2004|pp=45–46}}{{Sfn|Cheuk|2008|p=98}} For this movie, Tsui explored Mark's origin story, giving him a female mentor and love interest portrayed by [[Anita Mui]].{{Sfn|Bordwell|2011|p=120}}{{Sfn|Hughes|2005|p=258}} ''A Better Tomorrow III'' made {{Currency|18 million|HKD|passthrough=yes|linked=no}},{{Sfn|Lau|2000|p=174}} reaching eighth place at the box office and earning two nominations at the [[9th Hong Kong Film Awards]].{{Sfn|Morton|2016|p=170}}
