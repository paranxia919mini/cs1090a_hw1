'''''Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles'''''{{efn|{{IPA|fr|ʒan dilman vɛ̃ntʁwɑ kɛ dy kɔmɛʁs milkatʁəvɛ̃ bʁysɛl}}, [[literal translation|lit.]] "Jeanne Dielman, 23 Commerce Quay, 1080 Brussels", often referred to simply as '''''Jeanne Dielman'''''.}} is a 1975 [[art film]] written and directed by [[Chantal Akerman]], and starring [[Delphine Seyrig]]. Distinguished by its [[long take]]s, static camerawork, and [[slice-of-life]] depiction of domestic labor, the film follows the routine of a widowed mother (Seyrig) over three days. 

''Jeanne Dielman'' was shot over five weeks on location in [[Brussels]], and financed through a $120,000 grant awarded by the Belgian government.<ref>{{cite web|title=Jeanne Dielman, 23, Quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles|url=https://mubi.com/films/jeanne-dielman-23-quai-du-commerce-1080-bruxelles|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170911115455/https://mubi.com/films/jeanne-dielman-23-quai-du-commerce-1080-bruxelles|archive-date=11 September 2017|access-date=25 December 2020|website=[[Mubi (streaming service)|Mubi]]}}</ref><ref>{{cite news|last=Dean|first=Tacita|date=6 January 1999|title=The time of our lives|newspaper=[[The Guardian]]|url=http://www.theguardian.com/theguardian/1999/jan/06/features11.g21|url-status=live|access-date=25 December 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140507180723/http://www.theguardian.com/theguardian/1999/jan/06/features11.g21|archive-date=7 May 2014}}</ref> It had its world premiere at the [[Directors' Fortnight]] section of the [[1975 Cannes Film Festival]] on 22 May, and it was theatrically released in France on 21 January 1976, where it was met with polarized reviews. 

In the following decades, ''Jeanne Dielman'' gained exposure and reappraisal alongside Akerman’s body of work, becoming a [[Cult film|cult classic]]. Labelled as a [[feminist film]] and an early example of the [[slow cinema]] genre, it is now considered to be [[List of films voted the best|one of the greatest films ever made]]. In 2022, it placed first in the decennial [[The Sight & Sound Greatest Films of All Time 2022|critics' poll]] published by the [[British Film Institute]]'s magazine ''[[Sight and Sound]]''.<ref name="BFI">{{cite web|last=Mulvey|first=Laura|date=1 December 2022|title=The greatest film of all time: Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles|url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/features/greatest-film-all-time-jeanne-dielman-23-quai-du-commerce-1080-bruxelles|publisher=[[British Film Institute]]}}</ref>

==Plot==
Jeanne Dielman is a widowed woman living with her teenage son, Sylvain. Her days are filled with meticulous routine: cooking, cleaning, running errands, and taking care of her son. Their conversations are minimal, and their relationship appears distant but structured by habit.

Over the course of three afternoons, Jeanne receives a man in her home for a brief, silent sexual encounter. She hides the money that the men give her in a soup tureen, maintaining an almost ritualistic order to her life. Everything unfolds with quiet precision, reflecting her control over her environment.

After her second-day encounter, small disruptions begin to appear: overcooked potatoes, a dropped brush, an unbuttoned robe, a malfunctioning stamp machine. These seemingly minor hiccups in her routine signal an internal unraveling. Jeanne becomes increasingly unsettled, unable to maintain the precision that once defined her existence.{{efn|According to Chantal Akerman, the disruptions occur because Jeanne had an [[orgasm]], which disrupted her sense of order.<ref>{{cite web |last=Callahan |first=Dan |title=Jeanne Dielman, Three Decades Later: A Q & A with Chantal Akerman |website=[[Slant Magazine]] |date=22 January 2009 |url=https://www.slantmagazine.com/film/q-a-with-chantal-akerman-jeanne-dielman-three-decades-later/ |access-date=22 November 2025}}</ref>}}

On the final day, after a sexual encounter where she shows signs of emotional disturbance, Jeanne calmly stabs the client in the neck with a pair of scissors. She sits alone at the dining table, motionless, her blouse and hand stained with blood.

==Production==
After establishing herself as a major film director in 1974 with ''[[Je, tu, il, elle]]'', Akerman said that she "felt ready to make a feature with more money" and applied for a grant from the Belgian government for financial support, submitting a script that Jane Clarke described as portraying "a rigorous regimen [constructed] around food&nbsp;... and routine bought sex in the afternoon". This script would only be the rough basis for ''Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles'' because after Akerman received the government grant of $120,000 {{USDCY|120000|1975}} and began production, she threw the script out and began a new film instead.<ref name="Wakeman">{{cite book|editor-last=Wakeman|editor-first=John|url=https://archive.org/details/worldfilmdirecto0000unse_u4q2/page/4/mode/2up|title=World Film Directors, Volume Two: 1945–1985|year=1988|publisher=[[The H. W. Wilson Company]]|isbn=978-0-8242-0757-1|location=New York|pages=4–5|oclc=16925324}}</ref> Akerman also explained that she was able to make a female-centric film because "at that point everybody was talking about women" and that it was "the right time".<ref name="Wakeman"/>

Shooting ''Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles'' took five weeks and Akerman called it "a love film for my mother. It gives recognition to that kind of woman."<ref name="Wakeman"/> Akerman used an all-female crew for the film, which she later said "didn't work that well – not because they were women but because I didn't choose them. It was enough just to be a woman to work on my film&nbsp;... so the shooting was awful." Akerman further stated that the film is a reaction to "a hierarchy of images" in cinema that places a car accident or a kiss "higher in the hierarchy than washing up&nbsp;... And it's not by accident, but relates to the place of woman in the social hierarchy&nbsp;... Woman's work comes out of oppression and whatever comes out of oppression is more interesting. You have to be definite. You have to ''be''."<ref name="Wakeman"/>

The film depicts long moments of the life of Jeanne Dielman in real time, which Akerman said "was the only way to shoot the film – to avoid cutting the action in a hundred places, to look carefully and to be respectful. The framing was meant to respect her space, her, and her gestures within it."<ref name="Wakeman"/> The long static shots ensure that the viewer "always knows where I am".<ref name="margulies">{{cite web|last=Margulies|first=Ivone|date=17 August 2009|title=A Matter of Time: Jeanne Dielman, 23, quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles|url=http://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1215-a-matter-of-time-jeanne-dielman-23-quai-du-commerce-1080-bruxelles|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110706074518/http://www.criterion.com/current/posts/1215-a-matter-of-time-jeanne-dielman-23-quai-du-commerce-1080-bruxelles|archive-date=6 July 2011|access-date=15 December 2013|publisher=[[The Criterion Collection]]}}</ref>

==Reception and legacy==

===Initial release===
''Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles'' premiered in the [[Directors' Fortnight]] of the [[1975 Cannes Film Festival]]. It was initially met with mixed critical reception; many criticized it as a boring or meaningless exercise in [[Minimal art|minimalism]], while others praised its visuals and use of time.<ref name=encyclo>{{cite book |last1=Monaco |first1=James |last2=Pallot |first2=James |title=The encyclopedia of film |date=1991 |publisher=Perigree |isbn=9780399516047 |page=5}}</ref> The film's exposure and financial success in Europe helped Akerman to obtain funding for her 1978 film ''[[Les Rendez-vous d'Anna]]''.<ref name=encyclo/> ''Jeanne Dielman'' was not released in the United States until 1983.<ref name="Wakeman"/>

Upon its release, critic Louis Marcorelles called it the "first masterpiece of the feminine in the history of the cinema".<ref>{{cite book|last=Spaas|first=Lieve|url=https://archive.org/details/francophonefilms0000spaa/page/27|title=Francophone Film: A Struggle for Identity|year=2000|publisher=[[Manchester University Press]]|isbn=978-0-7190-5860-8|page=27|oclc=46332176|quote=In ''Le Monde'' Louis Marcorelles referred to the film as 'Undoubtedly the first masterpiece of the feminine in the history of cinema' (Marcorelles ''Le Monde'' 22 January 1976, trans.).}}</ref> [[Jonathan Rosenbaum]] defended the film's length and pace, saying that it "needs its running time, for its subject is an epic one, and the overall sweep&nbsp;... trains one to recognize and respond to fluctuations and nuances. If a radical cinema is something that goes to the roots of experience, this is at the very least a film that shows where and how some of these roots are buried".<ref name="Wakeman"/> Critic Gary Indiana said that "Akerman's brilliance is her ability to keep the viewer fascinated by everything normally left out of movies".<ref name="Wakeman"/> [[B. Ruby Rich]] said that "never before was the materiality of woman's time in the home rendered so viscerally&nbsp;... She invents a new language capable of transmitting truths previously unspoken".<ref name="Wakeman"/> [[Marsha Kinder]] called it "the best feature that I have ever seen made by a woman".<ref name="Wakeman"/> Akerman was reluctant to be seen as a feminist filmmaker, stating that "I don't think woman's cinema exists".<ref name="Wakeman"/>

The film has been characterized as an exemplar of the [[slow cinema]] genre, in which time is foregrounded and narrative action is downplayed.<ref name="dargis">{{cite news |last=Dargis |first=Manohla |author-link=Manohla Dargis |title='Jeanne Dielman': Where the Devil's in the Domestic Details |url=https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2015/08/20/arts/design/21art-of-slow.html |newspaper=[[The New York Times]] |date=20 August 2015 |access-date=6 December 2022 |url-access=subscription |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151015071115/http://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2015/08/20/arts/design/21art-of-slow.html |archive-date=15 October 2015 |url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="Bose">{{cite web |last=Bose |first=Swapnil Druv |title=10 essential films from the 'Slow Cinema' genre |url=https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/10-essential-films-from-slow-cinema-genre/ |website=Far Out |date=22 May 2022 |access-date=6 December 2022}}</ref> Critic [[Richard Brody]] called it a "tour de force of [[Modernist film|cinematic modernism]] [that] puts time onscreen as it was never seen before".<ref name="Brody">{{cite news |last=Brody |first=Richard |author-link=Richard Brody |title=Review: Jeanne Dielman, 23, quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles |url=https://www.newyorker.com/goings-on-about-town/movies/jeanne-dielman-23-quai-du-commerce-1080-bruxelles-2#intcid=inline_amp |newspaper=[[The New Yorker]] |access-date=6 December 2022}}</ref> Critic Jessica Winter wrote that "the film's strength derives in significant part from its austerity, patience, and extreme discipline", calling attention to its use of fixed shots, long takes, and absence of [[closeup]]s or [[reaction shot]]s.<ref name="Winter"/> Winter asserted that "as the minutes and hours pass, Akerman rewards the viewer's attention by recalibrating it".<ref name="Winter"/>  In a 2009 essay, Ivone Marguilies observed that the film was "fully in tune" with the European women's movement of the time, and that feminist critics welcomed its "rigorous alignment of sexual/gender politics with a formal economy—showing cooking and hiding sex—... as an impressive alternative to well-intentioned but conventional political documentaries and features".<ref name=margulies/>

===Accolades===
The film was named the 19th greatest film of the 20th century in a critics' poll conducted by ''[[The Village Voice]]'' in 2000.<ref>{{Cite book|last=Mathijs|first=Ernest|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=uPtuaV7IAt0C&pg=PT140|title=Cult Cinema: An Introduction|last2=Sexton|first2=Jamie|publisher=[[Wiley-Blackwell]]|year=2011|page=140|isbn=978-1-4051-7374-2|location=Chichester, West Sussex|oclc=692084802}}</ref><ref>{{cite news|last=Hoberman|first=James Lewis|author-link=J. Hoberman|year=2001|orig-year=4 January 2000|title=100 Best Films of the 20th Century: Village Voice Critics' Poll|work=[[The Village Voice]]|via=[[Filmsite]]|url=http://www.filmsite.org/villvoice.html|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140331174817/http://www.filmsite.org/villvoice.html|archive-date=31 March 2014}}</ref> It was rated 35th in the 2012 [[The Sight & Sound Greatest Films of All Time 2012|''Sight & Sound'' "Greatest Films of All Time"]] critics' poll, and not rated in the top 100 of the 2012 directors' list. In 2022, the film was given the distinction of being voted as ''[[Sight & Sound]]''{{'}}s "[[The Sight & Sound Greatest Films of All Time 2022|greatest film of all time]]".<ref>{{Cite news |last=Ugwu |first=Reggie |date=2022-12-01 |title=Chantal Akerman's 'Jeanne Dielman' Named Greatest Film of All Time in Sight and Sound Poll |newspaper=The New York Times |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2022/12/01/movies/jeanne-dielman-greatest-film-of-all-time-sight-and-sound-poll.html |access-date=2022-12-02 |url-access=subscription |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221201201633/https://www.nytimes.com/2022/12/01/movies/jeanne-dielman-greatest-film-of-all-time-sight-and-sound-poll.html |archive-date=1 December 2022 |url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="sight-and-sound">{{cite magazine |title=The Greatest Films of All Time |url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/greatest-films-all-time |magazine=[[Sight & Sound]] |date=1 December 2022 |access-date=1 December 2022}}</ref> The film ranked number 1 on the critics' poll, and tied for 4th place in the directors' poll.<ref>{{cite magazine |title=Directors' 100 Greatest Films of All Time |url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/directors-100-greatest-films-all-time |magazine=Sight & Sound |access-date=1 December 2022}}</ref> It is the fourth film to top the critics' poll after ''[[Bicycle Thieves]]'', ''[[Citizen Kane]]'', and ''[[Vertigo (film)|Vertigo]]'', and the first directed by a woman to do so.<ref name="sight-and-sound"/>
