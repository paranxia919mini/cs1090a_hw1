{{nihongo|'''''Kwaidan'''''|怪談|[[Kaidan]]|{{literal translation|Ghost Stories}}|lead=yes}} is a 1964 Japanese [[horror film|horror]] [[anthology film]] directed by [[Masaki Kobayashi]] from a screenplay by [[Yoko Mizuki]]. It consists of four separate stories adapted from [[Lafcadio Hearn]]'s collections of [[Japanese folktales]], including two from his 1904 book ''[[Kwaidan: Stories and Studies of Strange Things]]'', the film's namesake. Set mostly in feudal Japan, the segments feature vengeful spirits enforcing moral retribution, while portraying the supernatural realm as largely indifferent to human suffering.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2020-08-19 |title=Watch a cult classic #7: Kwaidan |url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/watch-cult-classic-7-kwaidan |access-date=2025-10-29 |website=BFI |language=en}}</ref> The cast includes [[Tatsuya Nakadai]], [[Tetsurō Tamba]], [[Yoichi Hayashi]], [[Katsuo Nakamura]], [[Rentarō Mikuni]], and [[Keiko Kishi]]; no actors appear in more than one segment.

The concept of adapting Hearn's ''Kwaidan'' into a film originated with producer [[Shigeru Wakatsuki]] during his student years. Upon the formation of [[Ninjin Club]]'s film production division, Wakatsuki pitched the idea to Kobayashi, who had once contemplated the same adaptation, prompting the project to advance. It subsequently languished in [[development hell]] for a decade; [[Shochiku]] initially planned to fund and distribute, but canceled. [[Toho]] then took over, accelerating progress. [[Principal photography]] began in March 1964, scheduled to end in July, but overran budget and schedule, [[Wrap (filmmaking)|wrapping]] in December.

''Kwaidan'' premiered at the {{Ill|Yurakuza|ja|有楽座 (昭和)}} theater on December 29, 1964, followed by a [[roadshow theatrical release]] in Japan beginning January 6, 1965, and a [[wide release]] on February 27, 1965. Escalating production costs pushed the final budget to approximately ¥318–380 million, making it one of the most expensive Japanese films of its time. Despite ranking as the ninth-highest-grossing Japanese film release of 1965, ''Kwaidan'' earned only ¥225 million at the domestic box office, resulting in a substantial financial loss. Thus, it exacerbated debts for the film's production company Ninjin Club, leading to its bankruptcy shortly after release. Receiving critical acclaim, the film won the [[Jury Prize (Cannes Film Festival)|Special Jury Prize]] at the [[1965 Cannes Film Festival]],<ref name="festival-cannes.com">{{cite web |url=http://www.festival-cannes.com/en/archives/ficheFilm/id/2895/year/1965.html |title=Festival de Cannes: Kwaidan |access-date=2009-03-04|work=festival-cannes.com}}</ref> and received an [[Academy Award]] nomination for [[Academy Award for Best International Feature Film|Best Foreign Language Film]].<ref name="Oscars1966">{{Cite web|url=http://www.oscars.org/oscars/ceremonies/1966 |title=The 38th Academy Awards (1966) Nominees and Winners |access-date=2011-11-06|work=oscars.org}}</ref><ref name=":10">{{Cite web |date=2016-05-26 |title=''Kwaidan'' |url=https://www.oscars.org/events/kwaidan |access-date=2025-11-09 |website=www.oscars.org |language=en}}</ref> It is now regarded as one of the greatest horror films and among the most visually beautiful films ever made.<ref name=":5">{{Cite web |last=Earl |first=Siddhant Adlakha,Peter Debruge,Owen Gleiberman,Courtney Howard,Tomris Laffly,Amy Nicholson,Rene Rodriguez,William |date=2024-10-09 |title=The 100 Best Horror Movies of All Time |url=https://variety.com/lists/best-horror-movies-of-all-time/ |access-date=2025-10-28 |website=Variety |language=en-US}}</ref>

==Plot==
==="The Black Hair"===
An impoverished swordsman in [[Kyoto]] divorces his wife, a weaver, and leaves her for a woman from a wealthy family to attain greater social status. However, despite his new wealthy status, the swordsman's second marriage proves to be unhappy. His new wife is shown to be callous and selfish. The swordsman regrets leaving his more devoted and patient ex-wife.

The second wife is furious when she realizes that the swordsman not only married her to obtain her family's wealth, but also still longs for his old life in Kyoto with his ex-wife. When he is told to go into the chambers to reconcile with her, the swordsman refuses, stating his intent to return home and reconcile with his ex-wife. He points out his foolish behavior and [[Poverty in Japan|poverty]] as the reasons why he reacted the way he did. The swordsman informs his lady-in-waiting to tell his second wife that their marriage is over and she can return to her parents in shame.

After a few years, the swordsman returns to find his home, and his wife, largely unchanged. He reconciles with his ex-wife, who refuses to let him punish himself. She mentions that Kyoto has "changed" and that they only have "a moment" together, but does not elaborate further. She assures him that she understood that he only left her in order to bring income to their home. The two happily exchange wonderful stories about the past and the future until the swordsman falls asleep. He wakes up the following day only to discover that he had been sleeping next to his ex-wife's rotted corpse, the corpse also rapidly decays into a skeleton on the next appearance. Rapidly aging, the swordsman stumbles through the house, finding that it actually is in ruins and overgrown with weeds. He manages to escape, only to be attacked by his ex-wife's black hair.

==="The Woman of the Snow"===
Two woodcutters from [[Musashi Province]], 18-year-old Minokichi and his elderly mentor Mosaku, take refuge in a boatman's hut during a snowstorm. Mosaku is killed by a {{transliteration|ja|[[yuki-onna]]}}. When the yuki-onna turns to Minokichi, she sympathetically remarks that he is a handsome boy and spares him because of his youth, warning him never to mention what happened or she will kill him. Minokichi returns home and never mentions that night.

One day, while cutting wood, Minokichi meets Yuki, a beautiful young woman traveling at sunset. She tells him she lost her family and that she has relatives in Edo who can secure her a job as a lady-in-waiting. Minokichi offers to let her spend the night at his house with his mother. The mother takes a liking to Yuki and asks her to stay. She never leaves for Edo, and Minokichi falls in love with her. The two marry and have children, living happily. The older women in the town are in awe over Yuki maintaining her youth even after having three children.

One night, Minokichi gives Yuki a set of sandals he has made. When she asks why he always gives her red ribbons on her sandals, he tells her of her youthful appearance. Yuki accepts the sandals and tries them on. She is stitching a kimono in the candlelight. In the light, Minokichi recalls the yuki-onna and sees a resemblance between them. He tells her about the strange encounter. It is then that Yuki reveals herself to be the yuki-onna, and a snowstorm comes over the home. Even though he broke his word, she cannot bring herself to kill him because of her love for him and their children. Yuki then spares Minokichi and leaves on the condition that he treats their children well. As Yuki disappears into the snowstorm, a heartbroken Minokichi places her sandals outside in the snow. After he goes back inside, the snow covers the sandals, implying that Yuki took them with her.

==="Hoichi the Earless"===
[[Hoichi the Earless|Hoichi]] is a young blind musician who plays the {{transliteration|ja|[[biwa]]}}. His specialty is singing the chant of ''[[The Tale of the Heike]]'' about the [[Battle of Dan-no-ura]] fought between the [[Taira clan|Taira]] and [[Minamoto clan|Minamoto]] clans during the last phase of the [[Genpei War]]. Hoichi is an attendant at a temple and is looked after by the others there. One night he hears a sound and decides to play his instrument in the garden courtyard. A spectral samurai appears and tells him that his lord wishes to have a performance at his house. The samurai leads Hoichi to a mysterious and ancient court. Another attendant notices that he went missing for the night as his dinner was not touched. The samurai re-appears on the next night to take Hoichi and affirms that he has not told anyone. Afterwards, the priest asks Hoichi why he goes out at night but Hoichi will not tell him. One night, Hoichi leaves in a storm and his friends follow him and discover he has been going to a graveyard and reciting the Tale of Heike to the court of the [[Emperor Antoku|dead Emperor]]. Hoichi informs the court that it takes many nights to chant the entire epic. They direct him to chant the final battle - the battle of Dan-no-ura. His friends drag him home as he refuses to leave before his performance is completed.

The priest tells Hoichi he is in great danger and that this was a vast illusion from the spirits of the dead, who plan to kill Hoichi if he obeys them again. Concerned for Hoichi's safety, a priest and his acolyte write the text of the [[Heart Sutra]] on his entire body including his face to make him invisible to the ghosts and instruct him to meditate. The samurai re-appears and calls out for Hoichi, who does not answer. Hoichi's ears are visible to the samurai as they forgot to write the text on his ears. The samurai, wanting to bring back as much of Hoichi as possible, rips his ears off to show his lord his commands have been obeyed.

The next morning, the priest and the attendants see a trail of blood leading from the temple. The priest and the acolyte realize their error and believe the ears were a trade for Hoichi's life. They believe the spirits will now leave him alone. A local lord arrives at the temple with a full retinue. They have heard the story of Hoichi the earless and wish to hear him play his biwa. Hoichi is brought before the lord and says he will play to console the sorrowful spirits. The narrator states that many wealthy nobles came to the temple with large gifts of money, making Hoichi wealthy.

==="In a Cup of Tea"===
Anticipating a visit from his publisher, a writer relates an old tale of an attendant of Lord Nakagawa Sadono named Kannai. While Lord Nakagawa is on his way to make a New Year's visit, he halts with his train at a [[Chashitsu|teahouse]] in [[Hakusan, Ishikawa|Hakusan]]. While the party is resting there, Kannai sees the face of a strange man in a cup of tea. Despite being perturbed, he drinks the cup.

Later, while Kannai is guarding his Lord, the man whose face appeared in the tea reappears, calling himself Heinai Shikibu. Kannai runs to tell the other attendants, but they laugh and tell him he is seeing things. Later that night at his own residence, Kannai is visited by three ghostly attendants of Heinai Shikibu. He duels them and is nearly defeated, but the author notes the tale ends before things are resolved and suggests that he could write a complete ending, but prefers to leave the ending to the reader's imagination.

The publisher soon arrives and asks the Madame for the author, who is nowhere to be found. They both flee the scene in terror when they discover the author trapped inside a large jar of water.

==Production==
===Development and pre-production===
[[File:Kinema-Junpo-1960-October-late-1.jpg|thumb|upright|Director [[Masaki Kobayashi]] in 1960]]
While he was a student, producer [[Shigeru Wakatsuki]], later the president of [[Ninjin Club]], had the idea of a film adaptation of [[Lafcadio Hearn]]'s ''[[Kwaidan: Stories and Studies of Strange Things]]''.{{sfn|Motoyama|Matsunomoto|Asai|Suzuki|2012|p=93}} Upon the formation of Ninjin Club's film production division in 1954, Wakatsuki pitched an adaptation of Lafcadio Hearn's ''Kwaidan'' to director [[Masaki Kobayashi]], who had also previously thought of adapting it. The project quickly gained momentum but soon languished in [[development hell]] for roughly a decade. [[Shochiku]] initially committed to funding and distribution but ultimately withdrew. [[Toho]] stepped in as partner and distributor, injecting the resources needed to push forward.{{sfn|Toho|1965|p=2}} Thus, Toho also began a three-film deal with director [[Masaki Kobayashi]] that concluded with the production of ''Kwaidan''.{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=451}}

Screenwriter [[Yoko Mizuki]] made the decision herself of which four of Hearn's stories to adapt.{{sfn|De Agostini|2012|p=12}} The opening segment, {{nihongo|"The Black Hair"|黒髪|Kurokami}}, is adapted from "{{Ill|The Reconciliation (novel)|lt=The Reconciliation|ja|和解 (小泉八雲の小説)}}", which appeared in Hearn's collection ''Shadowings'' (1900);{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:08:04}} the second and third segments, {{nihongo|"The Woman of the Snow"|雪女|Yukionna}}, and {{nihongo|"Hoichi the Earless"|耳無し芳一の話|Miminashi Hōichi no Hanashi}} are based on stories from Hearn's ''Kwaidan: Stories and Studies of Strange Things''.{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:39:57}}{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=01:22:17}} The fourth and final segment, {{nihongo|"{{Ill|In a Cup of Tea|ja|茶碗の中}}"|茶碗の中|Chawan no Naka}}, is adapted from Hearn's ''{{Ill|Kottō: Being Japanese Curios, with Sundry Cobwebs|ja|骨董 (小泉八雲)}}'' (1902).{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=02:37:46}}

Kobayashi pursued the project following the critical success of ''[[Harakiri (1962 film)|Harakiri]]'' (1962) at the [[Cannes Film Festival]], where he decided to advance further in stylization on his next film. ''Kwaidan'' also served as a personal homage to his late teacher [[Aizu Yaichi]], who had introduced Kobayashi to Hearn's writings.{{Sfn|Prince|2017|pp=204–205}}

During pre-production, Kobayashi discovered that the studio's sound stages were too small for the elaborate sets he envisioned. Therefore, he built the stages in an airplane hangar in Uji, Kyoto.<ref>{{Cite AV media |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Av-AQZ3iLQE |title=Interview with Masaki Kobayashi - Kwaidan (1964) |date=2024-09-15 |last=FilmArchive |access-date=2025-10-12 |via=YouTube}}</ref> Kobayashi worked with composer Toru Takemitsu for six months to produce the film's score.{{sfn|Kemp|2020|p=133}} The film exhausted its budget three-quarters of the way through production, which led Kobayashi to sell his house.{{sfn|Kemp|2020|p=133}}

Though known for hard-edged contemporary dramas, Kobayashi prioritized aesthetic reclamation with the film. During the 1930s–1940s, militarists had co-opted traditional arts for propaganda; ''Kwaidan'' sought to "reclaim the arts from this dark period", according to [[Stephen Prince]].{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:00:20}}

===Filming===
Filming began on March 22, 1964.{{sfn|Toho|1965|p=4}} Nearly all scenes were shot on vast indoor soundstages converted from a former military barracks—ironic given Kobayashi's pacifism and wartime trauma (drafted 1941, Kwantung Army near [[Unit 731]], brief POW detention). This enabled stylized, uncanny landscapes.{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:15:49}}

The film was one of the most expensive Japanese films ever at the time, with most money spent on the sets, nearly bankrupting Ninjin Club during filming; a loan from mentor [[Keisuke Kinoshita]] sustained it.{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:46:57}}<ref>{{Cite web |date=2021-10-27 |title=Kwaidan |url=https://www.deepfocusreview.com/definitives/kwaidan/ |access-date=2025-10-27 |website=Deep Focus Review |language=en-US}}</ref> The exact budget is disputed in reports, though it is generally agreed to have been over {{JPY|300 million}}. Some sources list the budget as {{JPY|320 million}},{{sfn|Imamura|1987|p=314}}{{sfn|Motoyama|Matsunomoto|Asai|Suzuki|2012|p=91}} while the film's trailer states it to have cost {{JPY|350 million}} ({{USD|970,000}}).<ref>{{cite web |title=Midnight Eye review: Kwaidan (Kaidan, 1965, Masaki KOBAYASHI) |url=http://www.midnighteye.com/reviews/kwaidan/ }}</ref>{{sfn|Barrett|2018|p=45}} According to Asaka Wakayama, widow of the film's composer, [[Tōru Takemitsu]], the initial budget was {{JPY|100 million}} with {{JPY|2 million}} set to be spent on Takemitsu's music, escalating to {{JPY|317 million}} for the film and {{JPY|3.36 million}} for the score by the end of production.{{sfn|Takemitsu|2006|p=101}} The ''[[Honolulu Star-Bulletin]]'' in September 1964 reported the budget as {{USD|361,000}}, though filming was still ongoing at that time.{{sfn|Gima|1964|p=9}} Wakatsuki provided a detailed description of the costs in the March 1965 issue of ''[[Kinema Junpo]]'', citing the exact production budget as {{JPY|318,661,704}} by January 20, 1965, excluding marketing.{{sfn|''Kinema Junpo''|1965|p=19}} The film's theater program stated that the total cost was {{JPY|380 million}} (just over {{USD|1 million|long=no}}) for the film.{{sfn|Toho|1965|p=2}} The [[New York Daily News|New York ''Daily News'']] claimed that the budget exceeded {{USD|1 million|long=no}}, adding that it was "peanuts for color and wide screen in Hollywood but the most expensive film ever made in Japan".{{sfn|Hale|1965|p=57}}

Cinematographer [[Yoshio Miyajima]] (a communist who made wartime propaganda before anti-militarist collaborations with Kobayashi) used fluid traveling shots, high-angle views (from Kobayashi's Otaru mountain climbs for solace, and 12th-century ''[[emakimono]]'' scrolls, explicitly cited in "Hoichi"), and oblique perspectives minimizing depth for two-dimensional surface design.{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:03:06}} Kobayashi rejected realism, honoring [[Zeami Motokiyo]]'s Noh theory despising "appearance."{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:06:19}} Color (his first film in color) was theatrical—gelled lights, painted backdrops, translights. Sets emphasized artificiality; outdoor archery in "The Black Hair" used exaggerated reverberant hoofbeats.{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=01:25:58}}

===Music and sound effects===
Tōru Takemitsu composed the film's score and co-created its sound effects, both of which are considered revolutionary. This marked his third of ten collaborations with Kobayashi. Kobayashi said his films had "too much music" until Takemitsu, who parsed to essentials.{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:02:30}} Takemitsu, a self-taught composer known for blending Eastern and Western musical traditions, was drawn to film scoring due to the medium's inherent "eroticism" and "violence," which he believed grounded it in physical reality.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Kwaidan |url=https://www.tcm.com/articles/145455/kwaidan |access-date=2025-11-18 |website=Turner Classic Movies |language=en-us}}</ref> Influenced by [[John Cage]], Takemitsu used prepared piano (screws/bolts/wool), electronic alteration, and Japanese ''[[Ma (negative space)|ma]]'' (charged silence). Sounds decoupled from visuals—creaking bamboo, ripping wood as metaphor, no ambient rustling.{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:10:06}}

==Thematic analysis==
''Kwaidan'' explores the uncanny (''kaidan''—Edo-period tales of strange encounters, not Western horror/morality).{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:05:06}} Central is ''[[mujō]]'' (transience)—life's brevity, inevitable decay, illusion of permanence.{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=01:06:32}}{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=02:13:20}}{{sfn|Eswaran|2021|p=34}} High angles evoke Buddha-like detachment; oblique compositions reference emakimono, flattening reality.{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=02:13:20}} Anti-militarism permeates subtly: angry ghosts unify episodes; eyes symbolize imperial surveillance ("thought police"); Heike suicide critiques ritualized death (echoing ''Harakiri''); biwa reclaimed from wartime propaganda.{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:45:46}} Kobayashi, a pacifist conscript, saw war as "culmination of human evil."{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=01:52:18}} Sound and image experiment—decoupled audio, prepared piano, silence—point to a spiritual domain beyond empiricism, confounding perception to reveal "force behind surfaces."{{sfn|Prince|2015|loc=00:05:42}}

Although ''Kwaidan'' is typically classified as a [[horror film]], Kobayashi rejected this interpretation. In a 1975 interview with [[Joan Mellen]], he called the horror label merely a "shorthand description" of the film. He emphasized his primary aim was to explore the opposition between humanity's material existence and the spiritual realm, including dreams and aspirations, and to focus on the profound spiritual meaning of life. He said he enjoyed portraying the beauty of [[Culture of Japan|traditional Japan]], but was dissatisfied with the horror classification, maintaining that the film's essence lay in its spiritual concerns.{{sfn|Mellen|1975|p=148}}

==Release==
[[File:Yūraku-za.jpg|thumb|The [[Toho]]-operated {{Ill|Yurakuza|ja|有楽座 (昭和)}} theater (pictured {{circa}} 1930s) in Tokyo's [[Yūrakuchō]] district, where ''Kwaidan'' premiered in December 1964.]]
''Kwaidan'' premiered at the {{Ill|Yurakuza|ja|有楽座 (昭和)}}, the most prestigious theater in central Tokyo, on December 29, 1964.{{sfn|Motoyama|Matsunomoto|Asai|Suzuki|2012|p=90}} The film opened in Japan as a [[roadshow theatrical release]] on 6 January 1965, distributed by [[Toho]],{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=217}}{{sfn|Gima|1965a|p=9}} with a simultaneous release in [[Taipei]].{{sfn|Huang|2008|p=111}} The Japanese general [[wide release]] followed on February 27, 1965.{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=218}} To promote the film, stars Nakadai and Aratama visited Heard's grave.{{sfn|Gima|1965a|p=9}}

Despite high expectations, ''Kwaidan'' underperformed commercially in Japan. Toho had projected at least ¥100 million in roadshow revenue, and early reports from the Tokyo Scala Theater noted attendance comparable to [[The Miracle Worker (1962 film)|''The Miracle Worker'']] and ''[[Mondo Cane]]'' (both 1962).{{sfn|''Engekikai''|1965|p=72}} The film ultimately earned ¥84.6 million during its Tokyo and Osaka roadshow engagements (January 6–February 12, 1965).{{sfn|''Kinema Junpo''|1965|p=19}} Despite this, its roadshow release alone was reportedly considered a success, leading Toho to start a new roadshow policy with the 1965 remake of [[Akira Kurosawa]]'s ''[[Sanshiro Sugata]]'' (1943).{{sfn|''Boxoffice''|1965|p=W-7}} In total, domestic rentals reached ¥225 million,{{sfn|''Kinema Junpo''|2012|p=210}} placing it ninth among Japanese-produced films released in 1965. With its final cost—including marketing—of ¥380 million, the film failed to break even. This shortfall, in turn, contributed heavily to the financial collapse of its production company, Ninjin Club, which declared bankruptcy shortly after release.{{sfn|Nikaido|2020|p=35}}

A 161-minute version screened in competition at the [[1965 Cannes Film Festival]], with Kobayashi and Takemitsu in attendance, giving them a sense of relief and accomplishment after the arduous production.{{Sfn|Prince|2017|p=224}} Upon returning from Cannes, Kobayashi was promptly dismissed by Shochiku president Kido Shiro, who had begun to dislike Kobayashi and attributed the decision to the film's excessive costs.{{Sfn|Prince|2017|p=224}} This ended Kobayashi's longstanding relationship with Shochiku and marked his transition to a more independent, "lone wolf" career.{{Sfn|Prince|2017|p=224}}

''Kwaidan'' then became one of the most popular and acclaimed foreign films ever released in the United States, according to [[The Union (newspaper)|''The Union'']].{{sfn|''The Union''|1982|p=8}} The film was released in the U.S. on 15 July 1965 by [[Continental Distributing]] in a 125-minute version that omitted the entire "The Woman of the Snow" segment after its Los Angeles premiere,{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=217}} reportedly with Kobayashi's approval.{{Sfn|Galbraith IV|1994|p=104}} A screening of ''Kwaidan'' was arranged for [[Grace Kelly|Princess Grace Kelly]] around November 1965 at her request.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Wilson |first=Earl |author-link=Earl Wilson (columnist) |date=November 26, 1965 |title=Earl Wilson's New York |url=https://www.newspapers.com/image/1133626611/ |url-access=subscription |work=[[Grand Forks Herald]] |page=6 |via=[[Newspapers.com]]}}</ref> The film, with all Its four segments intact, was also shown at Toho's [[La Brea Theatre]] in [[Los Angeles]] in July 1965;{{sfn|Barrin|1965|p=10}}{{sfn|Thomas|1965|p=49}} and in [[San Francisco]] from November 29-December 1, 1970.{{sfn|''San Francisco Chronicle''|1970|p=233}}

Some contemporary sources claim that the excised "Woman of the Snow" segment was later released overseas as a standalone short subject. However, film historian [[Stuart Galbraith IV]] found no evidence of a standalone theatrical release in the United States or elsewhere.{{Sfn|Galbraith IV|1994|p=104}} Nevertheless, there are indications of its exhibition in England. ''Kwaidan'' was re-released theatrically in Japan on November 29, 1982, as part of Toho's fiftieth anniversary.{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=332}} For several decades the original 183-minute Japanese cut was believed lost, and home-video releases (including laserdisc and early DVDs) were sourced from the 161-minute Cannes print. Toho later rediscovered the 183-minute cut and released it on [[DVD]] in 2003.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Kwaidan |url=https://www.tohokingdom.com/movies/kwaidan.htm |access-date=2025-11-09 |website=Toho Kingdom}}</ref>

==Reception==

=== Critical response ===
Japanese critics considered ''Kwaidan'' one of the best Japanese films of 1964 or 1965, but the latter year was mostly disregarded, as the film premiered in 1964 and was thus considered a 1964 film; only a few votes in a 1965 survey of Japanese film critics mentioned it as such.{{sfn|''Film Society Review''|1966|p=6}} ''[[Kinema Junpo]]'' ranked it the second best Japanese film of 1964, after ''[[Woman in the Dunes]]'',{{sfn|Gima|1965b|p=9}} and ''[[The Japan Times]]'' placed it sixth.{{sfn|Gima|1965c|p=13}} [[Yoko Mizuki]] also won the ''Kinema Junpo'' award for Best Screenplay. It also won awards for Best Cinematography and Best Art Direction at the [[Mainichi Film Concours]].{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=217}} The film won international awards, including [[Jury Prize (Cannes Film Festival)|Special Jury Prize]] at the [[Cannes Film Festival]] and was nominated for the [[Academy Award for Best International Feature Film|Best Foreign Language Film]] at the [[Academy Awards]].{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=215}} Despite its success at Cannes, its initial reception there was reportedly mixed.{{sfn|''Guam Daily News''|1965|p=14}} To meet international distribution demands, the film underwent significant running-time edits following its initial Japanese premiere. While the original Japanese roadshow version ran 182 minutes and the Japanese general release ran 163 minutes, American distributor Continental Distributing re-edited the film down to 125 minutes following its Los Angeles premiere by entirely removing "The Woman of the Snow" segment.<ref name="Galbraith217">Galbraith IV, Stuart (2008). ''The Toho Studios Story: A History and Complete Filmography''. Scarecrow Press. p. 217. ISBN 978-0810860049.</ref> Despite being omitted from initial Western theatrical prints, "The Woman of the Snow" was reinstated in subsequent reissues and home video releases, where it received widespread acclaim as one of the film's standout episodes.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Ehrenstein |first=David |title=Kwaidan |url=https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/91-kwaidan |access-date=2026-07-23 |website=The Criterion Collection |language=en}}</ref> It also remains the only horror movie ever to be nominated for the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film.<ref name=":10" />

In a 1967 review, the ''[[Monthly Film Bulletin]]'' commented on the use of colors in the film, stating that "it is not so much that the colour in ''Kwaidan'' is ravishing...as the way Kobayashi uses it to give these stories something of the quality of a legend."{{sfn|''Monthly Film Bulletin''|1967|pp=135–136}} The review concluded that the ''Kwaidan'' was a film "whose details stay on in the mind long after one has seen it."{{sfn|''Monthly Film Bulletin''|1967|pp=135–136}} [[Bosley Crowther]], in a 1965 ''[[The New York Times|New York Times]]'' review, stated that director Kobayashi "merits excited acclaim for his distinctly oriental cinematic artistry. So do the many designers and cameramen who worked with him. ''Kwaidan'' is a symphony of color and sound that is truly past compare."<ref name=":12" /> Furthermore, Crowther singled out [[Katsuo Nakamura]]'s performance as the "most conspicuous and memorable."<ref name=":12">{{cite web|url=https://www.nytimes.com/movie/review?res=9A07E4DB1639E33ABC4B51DFB767838E679EDE|work=[[New York Times]]|title=Screen: 'Kwaidan,' a Trio of Subtle Horror Tales:Fine Arts Theater Has Japanese Thriller|last=Crowther|first=Bosley|access-date=September 28, 2016|date=November 23, 1965|author-link=Bosley Crowther|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160609032227/http://www.nytimes.com/movie/review?res=9A07E4DB1639E33ABC4B51DFB767838E679EDE|archive-date=June 9, 2016}}</ref> He later listed it among the Top 10 films released in 1965 and recognized Katsuo's performance as one of the best in a film of that year.<ref>{{Cite web |date=1965-12-26 |title=The Ten Best Films of 1965; Summing Up: The Year's Ten Best Movies (Published 1965) |website=[[The New York Times]] |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1965/12/26/archives/the-ten-best-films-of-1965-summing-up-the-years-ten-best-movies.html |access-date=2025-11-09 |language=en}}</ref> ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' described the film as "done in measured cadence and intense feeling" and that it was "a visually impressive tour-de-force."{{sfn|Galbraith IV|1994|p=100}}

== Legacy ==

=== Modern reception ===
''Kwaidan'' is now considered one of the greatest horror films and among the "most beautiful" movies of all time.<ref name=":5" /><ref>{{Cite web |last=Bolt |first=Neil |date=2023-09-21 |title=Hideo Kojima's Criterion Closet Picks Include 4 Classic Japanese Horror Movies |url=https://www.comingsoon.net/movies/news/1346898-kojima-criterion-picks |access-date=2025-10-27 |website=ComingSoon.net - [[Mike Patton]] of [[Faith No More]] selected ''Kwaidan''<nowiki/>'s soundtrack as one of his favorite horror scores.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Staff |first=Revolver |title=Mike Patton Picks the 5 Most Spine-Tingling Horror Soundtracks |url=https://www.revolvermag.com/music/mike-patton-picks-5-most-spine-tingling-horror-soundtracks/ |access-date=2025-10-27 |website=Revolver |language=en-US}}</ref> Theatre director [[Ping Chong]] cited the film as inspiring his adaptation of Hearn's ''Kwaidan'' for the stage.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Cheng |first=Scarlet |date=2000-11-19 |title=Haunted by Such Human Tales |url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2000-nov-19-ca-54062-story.html |access-date=2025-11-11 |website=Los Angeles Times |language=en-US}}</ref>

The "Woman of the Snow" segment from the film was remade as the final story, "Lover's Vow", in the 1990 horror anthology ''[[Tales from the Darkside: The Movie]]''.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Miska |first=Brad |date=2017-03-24 |title=James Remar Tells Us His Favorite Satanic Horror Movies! |url=https://bloody-disgusting.com/editorials/3429477/james-remar-tells-us-favorite-satanic-horror-movies/ |access-date=2025-11-01 |website=Bloody Disgusting! |language=en-US}}</ref>{{sfn|Newman|2011|p=445}} This made ''Kwaidan'' the first Japanese horror film to be remade in the United States.{{sfn|Newman|2011|p=445}}

Stephen Prince suggests that the giant cosmic eyes in the skies of "The Woman of the Snow" episode influenced [[Akira Kurosawa]]'s depiction of the [[Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki|Nagasaki atomic bombing]] in ''[[Rhapsody in August]]'' (1991), where a massive eye opens in the sky as a surreal representation of the blast.{{Sfn|Prince|2017|p=213}}

In 2024, filmmaker Quan Zhang released the [[video essay]] ''kwAIdan'' for the [[academic journal]] ''MAI: Feminism & Visual Culture'', reinterpreting the film's "Black Hair" segment using [[Generative AI|AI-generated]] female figures to explore guilt, subvert female madness tropes, and critique AI objectification of women. Two reviewers in ''[[Sight and Sound]]'' praised it as a standout AI reworking and one of 2024's best video essays.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2024-12-18 |title=The best video essays of 2024 |url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/polls/best-video-essays-2024 |access-date=2025-11-01 |website=BFI |language=en}}</ref>

The Asian-owned luxury womenswear fashion label Kwaidan Editions is named after the film.<ref>{{Cite magazine |last=Neises |first=Jessica |date=2021-03-19 |title=26 Asian-Owned Fashion Businesses to Support Now and Always |url=https://www.vanityfair.com/style/photos/2021/03/25-asian-owned-brands-to-support-now-and-always |access-date=2025-11-01 |magazine=Vanity Fair |language=en-US}}</ref>
