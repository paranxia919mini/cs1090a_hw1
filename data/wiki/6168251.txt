'''''Downhill Racer''''' is a 1969 American [[sports film|sports]] [[drama (film and television)|drama film]] starring [[Robert Redford]], [[Gene Hackman]], and [[Camilla Sparv]];<ref name="dvd">{{Cite AV media |people=Michael Ritchie (director) |title=Downhill Racer |medium=DVD |publisher=The Criterion Collection |location=New York |year=2009 |orig-year=1977 AFI audio seminar}}</ref> it was the [[directorial debut]] of [[Michael Ritchie (filmmaker)|Michael Ritchie]].<ref>{{cite encyclopedia |title=Michael Ritchie |encyclopedia=Encyclopædia Britannica |url=https://www.britannica.com/biography/Michael-Ritchie-American-film-director |access-date=June 12, 2016 |date= |archive-date=September 27, 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160927224926/https://www.britannica.com/biography/Michael-Ritchie-American-film-director |url-status=live}}</ref> Written by [[James Salter]], based on [[Oakley Hall]]'s 1963 novel ''The Downhill Racers'', the film is about talented American [[Downhill (ski competition)|downhill]] racer David Chappellet, who joins the [[United States Ski Team|U.S. Ski Team]] in Europe to compete in [[FIS Alpine Ski World Cup|international skiing competitions]].

''Downhill Racer'' was filmed on location in [[Streif|Kitzbühel]] and [[Karl-Schranz-Piste|St. Anton]], Austria; [[Lauberhorn (downhill ski course)|Wengen]], Switzerland; [[Megève]] and [[Grenoble]], France; and [[Boulder, Colorado|Boulder]] and [[Idaho Springs, Colorado|Idaho Springs]], [[Colorado]], United States.<ref name="dvd" /> The film received positive reviews upon its theatrical release;<ref name="sidj1169">{{cite magazine |url=https://www.si.com/vault/1969/11/24/611073/how-to-succeed-at-racing-without-really-racing |magazine=[[Sports Illustrated]] |last=Jenkins |first=Dan |author-link=Dan Jenkins |title=How to succeed in racing without really racing |date=November 24, 1969 |page=66 |access-date=December 11, 2017 |archive-date=October 28, 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161028084353/http://www.si.com/vault/1969/11/24/611073/how-to-succeed-at-racing-without-really-racing |url-status=live}}</ref> [[Roger Ebert]] called it "the best movie ever made about sports—without really being about sports at all".<ref name="ebert" />

==Plot==
American downhill skiers David Chappellet and D.K. Bryan are summoned to Wengen, Switzerland, by U.S. ski team coach Eugene Claire. The newcomers are there to replace Tommy Herb, a top skier who was recently injured during an [[FIS Alpine World Ski Championships|FIS]] competition. Raised in the small town of [[Idaho Springs, Colorado]], Chappellet is a loner focused only on becoming a skiing champion. After refusing to race at the [[Lauberhorn ski races|Lauberhorn]] because of a late starting position, he makes his European skiing debut at the [[Arlberg-Kandahar]] in Austria, where he finishes fourth. In the final race of the season at the [[Hahnenkamm, Kitzbühel#Hahnenkamm race|Hahnenkamm-Rennen]] in [[Kitzbühel]], Austria, he crashes.

That summer, Chappellet joins the team in Oregon for offseason training. He visits his father at the family homestead, but they have little to say to one another. Chappellet drives into town and picks up Lena, an old flame, but after they have sex in the back seat of his father's old car, he shows little interest in her feelings. Later, when his father asks him why he is wasting his life on skiing, Chappellet reveals that he is racing as an amateur to become an Olympic champion. His father observes, "The world's full of 'em."<ref name="sidj1169" />

Back in France that winter, Chappellet wins the Grand Prix de [[Megève]] in France and attracts the attention of Machet, a ski manufacturer who wants him to use his skis to promote the brand, but Chappellet is more interested in Machet's attractive assistant Carole Stahl. After a chance encounter at a bakery, he and Carole spend some time together. They meet up again in [[Wengen]], ski the slopes together, and have sex.

At Kitzbühel, Chappellet wins the Hahnenkamm, but his cockiness alienates his teammates and coach, who feel that he is only concerned about himself. Chappellet finishes the season with several victories, ensuring his place on next season's [[Winter Olympic Games|Olympic]] team.

At the start of the third season, David invites Carole to spend Christmas with him, but she doesn't come. He travels to [[Zurich]] to Machet's office to find her and learns that she is spending Christmas with her family. The next week, Chappellet sees Carole in Wengen with another man. After a brief confrontation, he realizes that their relationship is over.

Two weeks before the Olympics, Chappellet challenges top skier Jimmy Creech to a race from the summit to the bottom of the slope, as the coaches look on in horror. On the way down, Creech runs out of room on the  trail and nearly collides with a stone wall. The next day, during the Lauberhorn race Creech is seriously injured, leaving Chappellet as the team's best hope for an [[Olympic medal|Olympic gold medal]].

At the Winter Olympics, Chappellet bests Austrian champion Max Meier, and is carried off in victory.

==Production==

===Development and writing===
The screenplay for ''Downhill Racer'' is loosely based on [[Oakley Hall]]'s 1963 novel ''The Downhill Racers''. In February 1966, [[Paramount Pictures]] acquired the book's film rights from director [[Mark Robson (film director)|Mark Robson]] for $15,000. The project was given to producer Steve Alexander<ref name="suit" /> and screenwriter Graham Ferguson. After attempts to develop it stalled, the new head of Paramount production, [[Robert Evans]], used the property to entice director [[Roman Polanski]], a skiing enthusiast, to make the film ''[[Rosemary's Baby (film)|Rosemary's Baby]]'' for the studio. Although Robert Redford passed on Polanski's offer to star in both films, he soon attached himself to the skiing film, taking it on as a pet project.<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.rogerebert.com/interviews/interview-with-robert-redford |last=Ebert |first=Roger |author-link=Roger Ebert |via=[[RogerEbert.com]] |newspaper=[[Chicago Sun-Times]] |title=INTERVIEW WITH ROBERT REDFORD |date=June 15, 1969 |access-date=March 28, 2018 |archive-date=March 29, 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180329054458/https://www.rogerebert.com/interviews/interview-with-robert-redford |url-status=live}}</ref> Redford also persuaded novelist [[James Salter]] to write a screenplay for the film, introducing him to Polanski and his partner, [[Gene Gutowski]], who agreed to work on the film.<ref name="mccarthy" />

Salter prepared notes for the story, which did not resemble Hall's novel, as he had not even read the book. Salter's starting point for the story was provided by Polanski, who told him the film should be a modern-day ''[[High Noon]]'', where the sheriff is killed and someone is called in to replace him. In the film, the lead U.S. national ski team racer breaks his leg, and David Chappellet is one of two skiers called in to replace him. Focused on directing ''Rosemary's Baby'', Polanski soon left the project, and the studio sued Redford for walking away from the starring role. Redford later revived the project by pitching the story to [[Gulf and Western Industries|Gulf+Western]] owner [[Charles Bluhdorn]]. Then Redford found his director and decided to make the film cheaply in Europe. Charles Bluhdorn, who was Austrian, may have influenced the film locations; he created the production company Wildwood.<ref name="mccarthy" />

In January 1968, Redford was named to produce and star and traveled with Salter to [[Grenoble]], France, and accompanied the U. S. ski team— traveling on buses, sleeping in hallways, taking in the atmosphere, and observing the athletes. One night in Grenoble, they discussed the central character, David Chappellet. Salter's inspiration for Chappellet was the [[Alpine skiing at the 1964 Winter Olympics – Men's slalom|1964 Olympic silver medal]] winner [[Billy Kidd]] of [[Vermont]], who conveyed an "arrogant and aloof" quality.<ref name="mccarthy" /> But Redford saw Chappellet as more like [[Spider Sabich]], the dynamic skier from [[Kyburz, California]], who finished fifth that year in the [[Alpine skiing at the 1968 Winter Olympics – Men's slalom|slalom]]. Chappellet ultimately took on aspects of both models, and Salter's original scenes of tense dynamics between Chappellet and the coach survived the writing process.<ref name="mccarthy" />

{{more citations needed|paragraph|date=July 2023}}
{{original research|paragraph|date=June 2023}}
The original inspiration for the character is said to be [[Colorado]]'s [[Buddy Werner]] of [[Steamboat Springs, Colorado|Steamboat Springs]]. Chappellet was said to be from [[Idaho Springs, Colorado]]. Werner burst upon the European downhill racing scene in the late 1950s and was the first American to win a F.I.S. downhill race. He was known for a reckless style but broke his leg in training two months before the [[Alpine skiing at the 1960 Winter Olympics|1960 Winter Olympics]] at [[Squaw Valley Ski Resort|Squaw Valley]], in which he would have been a favorite for a medal in the downhill. Werner died in an avalanche in Switzerland in 1964, while performing for a film crew.

In late December 1968, [[Michael Ritchie (filmmaker)|Michael Ritchie]] signed on as director.<ref name="suit" />

In 1969, Buck Holland and Jan Schimmel filed a $6 million lawsuit claiming that the film was based on their story ''Devil On His Hills'', which they gave to Redford, and asking Paramount to stop saying the film was based on Hall's book. Before the lawsuit, Paramount had already deleted reference to Hall in advertising and on screen and sought an injunction against Hall interfering with distribution and claiming the film was based on his book. Despite Paramount's intention, some studio publicity was released crediting Hall. Hall filed a counterclaim against Paramount. Alexander was also in a legal dispute to gain executive producer credit.<ref name="suit">{{cite magazine |magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] |date=December 24, 1969 |page=7 |title='Downhill' Quarrels Make Uphill Puzzle |last=Setlowe |first=Rick}}</ref><ref name="AFI" />

===Crew===
Ritchie admired the work of British director [[Ken Loach]] and hired Brian Probyn and Kevin Sutton, the cameraman and soundman from Loach's film ''[[Poor Cow]]''.<ref name="dvd" />

===Filming===
Three weeks before the film was due to begin, Paramount canceled it due to its $3.5 million budget; after the budget was reduced, filming proceeded.<ref name="suit" /> ''Downhill Racer'' was filmed on location in [[Wengen]] and [[Unterseen]], Switzerland; [[Kitzbühel]] and [[Sankt Anton am Arlberg|St.&nbsp;Anton]], Austria; [[Megève]] and [[Grenoble]], France; and [[Boulder, Colorado|Boulder]] and [[Idaho Springs, Colorado|Idaho Springs]], Colorado, United States.<ref name="dvd" /> Other scenes were shot at the Timp Haven ski area in [[Utah]] (acquired by Redford in 1968, now [[Sundance Resort]]).<ref>{{cite book |last1=D'Arc |first1=James V. |title=When Hollywood came to town: a history of moviemaking in Utah |date=2010 |publisher=Gibbs Smith |location=Layton, Utah |isbn=9781423605874 |edition=1st}}</ref> Most of the skiing footage was shot from January 11 to February 1, [[1968–69 FIS Alpine Ski World Cup|1969]], during four [[FIS Alpine Ski World Cup|World Cup]] races: Internationales [[Lauberhorn ski races|Lauberhorn]] in Wengen, Internationales [[Streif|Hahnenkamm]]-Rennen in Kitzbühel, Grand Prix de [[Megève]], and [[Arlberg-Kandahar]] in St. Anton.<ref name="dvd" /> The off-season scenes were filmed in Colorado: the track scene at [[Colorado Buffaloes#Facilities|Potts Field]] on the east campus of the [[University of Colorado Boulder|University of Colorado]] in Boulder,<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.cubuffs.com/ViewArticle.dbml?DB_OEM_ID=600&ATCLID=325144 |publisher=University of Colorado Athletics |title=Potts Field |access-date=April 29, 2015 |archive-date=April 18, 2015 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150418010617/http://www.cubuffs.com/ViewArticle.dbml?DB_OEM_ID=600&ATCLID=325144 |url-status=live}}</ref> and the hometown street scenes in Idaho Springs, {{convert|30|mi|-1|spell=in}} west of [[Denver]].<ref name="dvd" /> The interior scenes of Chappellet's Idaho Springs house were filmed at [[Paramount Studios]] in [[Southern California|southern]] [[California]].<ref name="dvd" />

[[Sylvester Stallone]], age 22, appears as an extra in the restaurant scene.<ref>{{cite news |last1=McKenzie-Murray |first1=Martin |title=Downhill Racer: sports films' unsentimental outlier |url=https://www.thesaturdaypaper.com.au/sport/2024/10/05/downhill-racer-sports-films-unsentimental-outlier |access-date=February 10, 2026 |newspaper=[[The Saturday Paper]] |date=October 5–11, 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241004141910/https://www.thesaturdaypaper.com.au/sport/2024/10/05/downhill-racer-sports-films-unsentimental-outlier |archive-date=October 4, 2024 |language=en |quote=Downhill Racer also happens to feature ... Sylvester Stallone ... as an uncredited extra.}}</ref>

==Release==
===Theatrical===
''Downhill Racer'' premiered at the Granada Theatre in [[Reno, Nevada]], on October 28, 1969.<ref name="AFI">{{AFI film|23973}}</ref><ref>{{cite news |title=Downhill Racer (1969) |url=https://www.nytimes.com/movies/movie/14515/Downhill-Racer/overview |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131015004542/http://www.nytimes.com/movies/movie/14515/Downhill-Racer/overview |url-status=dead |archive-date=October 15, 2013 |department=Movies & TV Dept. |newspaper=[[The New York Times]] |first=Hal |last=Erickson |author-link=Hal Erickson (author) |date=2013 |access-date=October 29, 2013}}</ref> The film was released one month after ''[[Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid]]'', also starring Redford. It was re-released in the U.S. in July 1984 at the [[Filmex]] (Los Angeles International Film Exposition) for a 50-hour Sports Movie Marathon on July 5–20.<ref>{{cite web |title=Downhill Racer Miscellaneous Notes |work=Turner Classic Movies |url=https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/4533/downhill-racer#notes |access-date=October 29, 2013 |archive-date=February 1, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140201202953/http://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/4533/Downhill-Racer/misc-notes.html |url-status=dead}}</ref> The British premiere was at [[Romaine Hart]]'s [[The Screen On The Green|Screen on the Green]] known as the "coolest cinema in [[London]]" and the audience included [[Richard Attenborough]], [[Laurence Olivier]], and [[Brian Forbes]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=January 3, 2022 |title=Romaine Hart obituary |url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2022/jan/03/romaine-hart-obituary |access-date=November 29, 2022 |newspaper=[[The Guardian]] |language=en}}</ref>

==Reception==
===Critical response===
In his contemporary review for the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'', [[Roger Ebert]] gave the film four out of four stars, calling it "the best movie ever made about sports—without really being about sports at all."<ref name="ebert">{{cite news |last=Ebert |first=Roger |author-link=Roger Ebert |title=Downhill Racer |newspaper=[[Chicago Sun-Times]] |via=[[RogerEbert.com]] |date=December 22, 1969 |url=http://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/downhill-racer-1969 |access-date=October 29, 2013 |archive-date=October 31, 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131031135556/http://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/downhill-racer-1969 |url-status=live}}</ref> In addition to praising the performances of Redford and [[Gene Hackman]], Ebert noted how well the film balances exciting action sequences and the less glamorous aspects of an athlete's life.<ref name="ebert" /> He wrote:
{{blockquote|Without bothering to explain much of the technical aspect of skiing, ''Downhill Racer'' tells us more about the sport than we imagined a movie could. The joy of these action sequences is counterpointed by the daily life of the ski amateur. There are the anonymous hotel rooms, one after another, and the deadening continual contact with the team members, and the efforts of the coach ... to hold the team together and placate its financial backers in New York. And there is Chappellet's casual affair with a girl [...] who seems to be a sort of ski groupie. She wants to make love to him, and does, but he is so limited, so incapable of understanding her or anything beyond his own image, that she drops him. He never does quite understand why. The movie balances nicely between this level, and the exuberance of its outdoor location photography. And it does a skillful job of involving us in the competition without really being a movie about competition. In the end, ''Downhill Racer'' succeeds so well that instead of wondering whether the hero will win the Olympic race, we want to see what will happen to him if he does.<ref name="ebert" />}}

In his review for ''[[The New York Times]]'', [[Roger Greenspun]] called ''Downhill Racer'' "a very good movie".<ref name="greenspun">{{cite news |last=Greenspun |first=Roger |title=For 'Downhill Racer,' Time Is the Master |newspaper=The New York Times |date=November 7, 1969 |url=https://www.nytimes.com/movie/review?res=9E04E2DB153DEF34BC4F53DFB7678382679EDE |access-date=October 29, 2013 |archive-date=March 6, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140306140328/http://www.nytimes.com/movie/review?res=9E04E2DB153DEF34BC4F53DFB7678382679EDE |url-status=live}}</ref> Of the lead character, Greenspun wrote, "His world is that international society of the well-exercised inarticulate where the good is known as 'really great,' and the bad is signified by silence. In appreciating that world, its pathos, its narcissism, its tensions, and its sufficient moments of glory, ''Downhill Racer'' succeeds with sometimes chilling efficiency."<ref name="greenspun" />

In his review for ''[[Life (magazine)|Life]]'' magazine, [[Richard Schickel]] praised the film for both its esthetic beauty and its depiction of the brutal realities of competition.<ref name="schickel">{{cite magazine |last=Schickel |first=Richard |title=The Brutal Beauty of Ski Racing |magazine=[[Life (magazine)|Life]] |volume=67 |number=23 |page=18 |date=December 5, 1969 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=mFAEAAAAMBAJ |access-date=September 27, 2016 |archive-date=October 9, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141009105032/http://books.google.com/books?id=MFAEAAAAMBAJ |url-status=live}}</ref> Schickel wrote:

{{blockquote|''Downhill Racer'' is precisely what we have waited so long to see—a small, tense, expertly made (and, on occasion, surprisingly funny) film about a newly chic form of athletic competition—[[Alpine skiing]]. ... That insistence on the heart of the matter, winning or losing, is not the least of ''Downhill'''s virtues, but there are others. Quite obviously, they include capturing on color film the sheer beauty of the white world in which racers live. Here the director, Michael Ritchie, splendidly exploits a couple of paradoxes. From a distance, the skiers seem to have the effortless natural grace of birds in flight. Close up, though, he makes us see they are engaged in a brutal, breath-stealing ordeal, and the contrast gripped me as strongly as anything I have recently seen on the screen. Then there's the other paradox—that this agonizing effort takes place, unlike that of any other sport, with the competitor alone in a sea of silence.<ref name="schickel" />}}

On review aggregator [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film holds an approval rating of 82% based on 28 reviews. The website's critics consensus reads, "''Downhill Racer'' plunges the viewer thrillingly into the action of the sport—and continues to hold the attention as a thoughtful drama."<ref>{{cite web |title=Downhill Racer (1969) |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/downhill_racer |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |access-date=April 14, 2026 |archive-date=October 6, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201006073406/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/downhill_racer |url-status=live}}</ref> On [[Metacritic]], the film has a [[weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average score]] of 89 out of 100, based on 15 critics, indicating "universal acclaim".<ref>{{cite web |title=Downhill Racer Reviews |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/downhill-racer |website=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=September 10, 2020 |archive-date=February 24, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210224233005/https://www.metacritic.com/movie/downhill-racer |url-status=live}}</ref>
