'''''The Funhouse''''' is a 1981 American [[slasher film]]{{sfn|Martin|2007|page=158}}{{sfn|Rockoff|2002|page=110}} directed by [[Tobe Hooper]], written by Larry Block and starring [[Elizabeth Berridge (actress)|Elizabeth Berridge]], [[Kevin Conway (actor)|Kevin Conway]], [[William Finley (actor)|William Finley]], [[Cooper Huckabee]], [[Miles Chapin]], [[Largo Woodruff]], Wayne Doba, and [[Sylvia Miles]]. The film's plot follows four [[Midwestern United States|Midwestern]] teenagers who become trapped in a [[dark ride]] at a traveling carnival and are stalked by a [[mentally handicapped]] [[albino]] killer.

A [[Universal Pictures]] production, ''The Funhouse'' was director Hooper's first major studio film after ''[[The Texas Chain Saw Massacre]]'' (1974) and ''[[Eaten Alive]]'' (1976). Its producers were inspired to produce a successful teenage-themed horror film following the major financial success of [[Paramount Pictures|Paramount]]'s slasher ''[[Friday the 13th (1980 film)|Friday the 13th]]'' (1980). Though the film is set in Iowa, principal photography took place on backlots at Norin Studios in [[Miami|Miami, Florida]]. Hooper was stylistically influenced by ''[[Nightmare Alley (1947 film)|Nightmare Alley]]'' (1947), a [[film noir]] also set in a traveling carnival.

An [[independent film|independent]] production,<ref name=Times/> ''The Funhouse'' was acquired by [[Universal Pictures]], who theatrically released it in the United States on March 13, 1981. Though a commercial disappointment, the film received mixed to positive reviews from critics with praise for its atmosphere, cinematography and Kevin Conway's performance but criticisms for its pacing and comparisons to Tobe Hooper's other works. Some critics favorably cited its references to several classic horror films, among them works by [[Alfred Hitchcock]], [[James Whale]], and [[Tod Browning]].{{efn-lr|Attributed to multiple sources.<ref name=Keneas/><ref name=Mahar/>}} Contemporary film scholars and critics have noted that the film continues Hooper's recurring theme of [[dysfunctional family|family dysfunction]] as seen in his previous films. 

A [[novelization]] of the film by [[Dean Koontz]] was released prior to its release, with Koontz using the pseudonym Owen West.

==Plot==
In small-town Iowa, a masked intruder attacks teenager Amy as she showers. The attacker turns out to be her younger brother Joey, a [[horror film]] fan, and his weapon is a fake rubber prop knife.

Against her father's wishes, Amy visits a sleazy [[traveling carnival]] with her new boyfriend Buzz, her best friend Liz, and Liz's irresponsible boyfriend Richie. At the carnival, the four teens smoke [[marijuana]], peep into a 21-and-over [[strip show]], heckle [[fortune teller]] Madame Zena, visit the [[freak show|freaks-of-nature]] exhibit, and view a [[illusionist|magic show]].

Richie dares the group to spend the night in "The Funhouse," which is a [[dark ride]]. After the carnival closes, the teenagers settle down inside the funhouse. Through a grate to a room below the attraction, the teenagers witness the ride assistant, a silent man in a [[Frankenstein's monster|Frankenstein's Monster]] mask, engaging Zena as a [[prostitute]]. He experiences [[premature ejaculation]], but despite his request, Zena will not return her $100 fee; he then murders her in a violent rage.

The teenagers try to leave but find themselves locked inside the funhouse. As they attempt to escape, Richie secretly steals the money from the [[safe]] from which the masked assistant took Zena's fee. The funhouse's [[Barker (occupation)|barker]], Conrad Straker, discovers what his son Gunther Twibunt (the masked assistant) has done to Zena. Conrad also realizes that the money is missing. Thinking Gunther took it, he attacks him. Gunther's face is revealed to be gruesomely deformed via [[albinism]] and [[frontonasal dysplasia]] with sharp protruding teeth, long white thinning hair, red eyes, and a cleft running up the bridge of his nose.

The teens see this, and Conrad realizes someone is watching when Richie's lighter falls from the ceiling where he and the others were hiding onto the floor. Buzz concludes that Richie has the money. Richie insists that he would have split the money with the others. Despite Liz wanting to return the money, Buzz knows it is too late since they are now in danger. Conrad stalks the funhouse to eliminate any witnesses and heckles Gunther into a murderous rage. The teens arm themselves with the various funhouse props as weapons.

Richie is hanged with a rope by Conrad, and the remaining three witness his corpse being carried through on a cart. Liz, hysterical, falls through a [[trapdoor]] and is confronted by Gunther. She stabs him with a dagger before he kills her by pushing her head through an [[industrial fan|industrial exhaust fan]]. Buzz stabs Conrad to death when he confronts him and Amy, but is then killed by Gunther. During a showdown between [[final girl]] Amy and Gunther in the funhouse's maintenance area, Gunther is electrocuted and crushed to death between two spinning gears.

As dawn breaks, the traumatized [[final girl|sole survivor]] Amy emerges from the funhouse and heads home as the [[animatronics|animatronic]] fat lady perched atop the entrance laughs mockingly at her.

==Themes==
In his 1997 book ''Hearths of Darkness'', author Tony Williams argues that ''The Funhouse'' "continues [Hooper's] exploration of the American family's repressive nature", a theme previously explored in ''[[The Texas Chain Saw Massacre]]'' (1974).{{sfn|Williams|1996|p=201}} Williams further posits that the "carnival world" which Amy enters acts as a liberating, unrepressed counter to the restricted nature of her home life with her family.{{sfn|Williams|1996|p=201}} The theme of family was similarly noted by critic Cynthia Rose of ''[[The Monthly Film Bulletin]]'', who wrote of the film during its 1981 release: "Again as in ''[The Texas] Chain Saw [Massacre]'', the film’s real focus is on the family and, through it, on the 'permissive' society. Over and over, we see how the 'monstrous' (and once more all-male) family—the source of the evil—is sustained and regenerated by exterminating (in ''Chain Saw'', actually devouring) the threat of the outsider."<ref name=bfi/>

''The Funhouse'' contains [[metafiction]] elements, including in its opening scene, which explicitly parodies the opening sequence of the 1978 slasher film ''[[Halloween (1978 film)|Halloween]]'', as well as the shower scene in ''[[Psycho (1960 film)|Psycho]]'' (1960).{{sfn|Nowell|2010}}{{sfn|Roche|2022|p=}} Hooper said that the opening scene "immediately [lets] you know you're watching a genre picture. In particular, too, it helped make the film a little safe. And I wanted that. Because I wanted the color and the fantasy, to build up to the moment where this person [who] is wearing a Frankenstein mask is actually the strange anomaly".{{sfn|Williams|2017|p=63}}

In the 2021 book ''American Twilight: The Cinema of Tobe Hooper'', writers Kristopher Woofter and Will Dodson note a recurring theme throughout ''The Funhouse'' of a world in which adults mistreat and look down upon teenagers, characterizing them as "a corrupt [[cabal]] disenfranchising the young."{{sfn|Woofter|Dodson|2021|p=33}}

==Production==
===Development===
''The Funhouse'' was written by Larry Block, and the script was optioned by BNB Productions who got a negative pickup deal with Universal Studios who were looking to produce a teen-aimed horror film after the success of [[Paramount Pictures|Paramount]]'s ''[[Friday the 13th (1980 film)|Friday the 13th]]'' (1980).{{sfn|Nowell|2010|p=174}} [[Tobe Hooper]], who had recently completed the miniseries ''[[Salem's Lot (1979 miniseries)|Salem's Lot]]'' (1979) for [[Warner Bros.]], was offered to direct ''The Funhouse''.{{sfn|Gregory|2007|loc=1:02}} Hooper agreed to sign on as director as he saw "a lot of potential" in its carnival setting; Hooper was an admirer of ''[[Nightmare Alley (1947 film)|Nightmare Alley]]'' (1947), a film noir set in a carnival, and had always wanted to make a film of his own set in the same locale.{{sfn|Gregory|2007|loc=1:16}}

In 2017, screenwriter Lawrence J. Block recaptured the U.S. copyright to The Funhouse through the termination provisions of U.S. copyright law.{{cite web |title=Copyright Record – The Funhouse |url=https://publicrecords.copyright.gov/detailed-record/voyager_29176950 |publisher=United States Copyright Office |access-date=23 June 2026}}

===Casting===
Elizabeth Berridge, who was cast in the lead role of Amy Harper, is given an "introducing" credit, though she had previously appeared in the film ''[[Natural Enemies]]'' (1979).<ref name=afi/> Largo Woodruff was cast in the role of Amy's best friend, Liz, after auditioning and screen testing for the part with Hooper in New York City.<ref name=carnivalofblood>{{cite AV media|title=Carnival of Blood: Largo Woodruff on The Funhouse|work=The Funhouse|medium=[[Blu-ray]] documentary short|publisher=[[Shout! Studios|Scream Factory]]|oclc= 1391643820}}</ref>

Commenting to [[Roger Ebert]] at the time, Sylvia Miles said of her casting: "[It]'s fabulous, I’m playing a fortuneteller. Madame Zena. I have a phony accent and a great scene where I lose the accent gradually as I’m being murdered in the fun house. Of course, there are people who have asked why I want to be in a horror picture. You know what I always say: Better a horror film than a horrible film. Besides, the people who see them, I think they remember the horror films better than the others."<ref>{{cite web|work=[[RogerEbert.com]]|url=https://www.rogerebert.com/interviews/interview-with-sylvia-miles|title=Interview with Sylvia Miles|date=December 14, 2012|last=Ebert|first=Roger|author-link=Roger Ebert|orig-year=1980|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201126163139/https://www.rogerebert.com/interviews/interview-with-sylvia-miles|archive-date=November 26, 2020}}</ref>

===Filming===
Though set in the [[Midwestern United States|American Midwest]] in Iowa,<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.desmoinesregister.com/story/news/2014/04/21/ultimate-list-of-iowa-movies/7913481/|work=[[Des Moines Register]]|title=The ultimate list of Iowa movies|date=April 24, 2014|last=West|first=Adam|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260525080645/https://www.desmoinesregister.com/story/news/2014/04/21/ultimate-list-of-iowa-movies/7913481/|archive-date=May 25, 2026}}</ref> ''The Funhouse'' was shot on the backlots of the [[Ivan Tors]] Studios in [[Miami|Miami, Florida]],<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.blumhouse.com/2016/02/16/slashback-something-not-quite-human-is-waiting-in-the-funhouse-1981/|website=[[Blumhouse Productions|Blumhouse]]|title=Slashback! Something Not Quite Human is Waiting in THE FUNHOUSE (1981)|first=Gregory|last=Burkart|date=February 16, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160918092604/http://www.blumhouse.com/2016/02/16/slashback-something-not-quite-human-is-waiting-in-the-funhouse-1981|archive-date=September 18, 2016|url-status=dead|access-date=July 3, 2018}}</ref> over approximately ten weeks.<ref name=carnivalofblood/> [[Principal photography]] began in March 1980, on a budget of $3.7 million.<ref name=Lovell/>{{sfn|Gregory|2007|loc=3:59}} The film was [[independent film|independent]]ly financed.<ref name=Times/> The production originally intended to shoot the film on the [[Universal Studios, Inc.|Universal Studios]] lots, but opted instead to film on the east coast, as they were unable to obtain a waiver in the state of California allowing for child actor Shawn Carson—who played a significant role in the film—to work overnight due to [[child labour law|child labor law]]s.{{sfn|Gregory|2007|loc=4:34}} Filming in Florida also allowed Hooper and the production to hire real carnies to appear in the film, as many traveling carnival workers settle in the state during the winter season when principal photography occurred.{{sfn|Gregory|2007|loc=5:16}} Filming was completed by May 1980.<ref name=Times>{{cite news|work=[[The Times (Shreveport, Louisiana)|The Times]]|date=May 17, 1980|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-times/198264475/|title=Rights acquired|page=7-B|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref>

The amusement rides and attractions featured in the film, which date from the 1940s and 1950s, were acquired from a defunct carnival in [[Akron, Ohio]].<ref name=Lovell/><ref name=afi>{{Cite web|title=The Funhouse|url=https://catalog.afi.com/Film/67053-THE-FUNHOUSE?sid=c50f45b6-7afe-4ce6-9f5a-db8096c942c3&sr=6.9997983&cp=1&pos=0|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241201204718/https://catalog.afi.com/Film/67053-THE-FUNHOUSE?sid=c50f45b6-7afe-4ce6-9f5a-db8096c942c3&sr=6.9997983&cp=1&pos=0|archive-date=December 1, 2024|url-status=live|website=[[AFI Catalog of Feature Films]]|publisher=[[American Film Institute]]}}</ref> The "freakshow" animals seen in the film—including one cow with a [[cleft palate]] and another with two heads{{sfn|Williams|2017|p=63}}{{sfn|Cutler-Broyles|2020|p=70}}—were real animals that belonged to a traveling carnival.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.shocktillyoudrop.com/news/articlenews.php?id=15603|title=The Funhouse Retrospective: An Interview With Tobe Hooper Page 2|last=Turek|first=Ryan|date=June 22, 2010|website=Shock Till You Drop|access-date=November 6, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100626112247/http://www.shocktillyoudrop.com/news/articlenews.php?id=15603|archive-date=June 26, 2010|url-status=dead}}</ref> Production designer Mort Rabinowitz designed the carnival set pieces based on his own recollections of visiting carnivals as a child: "I picked most of these concepts out of my memory—and old [[Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer|MGM]] carnival research stills."<ref name=Lovell/> Rabinowitz said he took the job because it was "a set designer's dream project."<ref name=Lovell/>

===Special effects===
The deformed facial appearance of the Monster--who was dubbed "cow-man" by Hooper and other members of the crew{{sfn|Martin|1981|p=53}}—was designed by makeup artist [[Rick Baker]] and executed by makeup artist Craig Reardon.{{sfn|Martin|1981|p=52}} The film's screenplay described the Monster's appearance as a hulking troll-like creature with large red animal eyes. Baker took some creative freedom during the design process.{{sfn|Martin|1981|p=52}} In an interview with ''[[Fangoria]]'', Baker said:
<blockquote>It's a birth-defect type monster. After I started thinking about it for a while, I felt real guilty about making that deformity a monster. It's so easy to take horror straight from nature, because there are some pretty horrifying real things. I just didn't feel right about making it a straight freak, so I added a little more to it. I ''hope'' it comes across that way, because it still has a lot of the birth defect aspect to it.{{sfn|Martin|1981|p=52}}</blockquote>
Twibunt's hands were designed by Reardon.{{sfn|Martin|1981|p=53}} The character was portrayed by Wayne Doba, a [[mime]] from the San Francisco area.{{sfn|Martin|1981|p=52}}

Additionally, Reardon designed Twibunt's brother, a preserved infant exhibiting similar facial deformities seen on display at the carnival in the film.{{sfn|Martin|1981|p=53}} A vinyl cast of a baby from a medical supply house was used as a base for the prop.{{sfn|Martin|1981|p=53}}

==Release==
===Theatrical run===
Distribution rights for ''The Funhouse'' were acquired by [[Universal Pictures]] shortly before filming completed in mid-1980.<ref name=Times/> Universal released the film in 814 theaters in the United States on March 13, 1981.<ref name=BOM/> It was released in some locations with the subtitle ''Carnival of Terror''.{{efn-lr|[[John Kenneth Muir]] notes that the film was sometimes known as ''Carnival of Terror''.{{sfn|Muir|2012|p=168}} During its theatrical release in the [[Tampa, Florida]] area, the film was billed with ''Carnival of Terror'' as a subtitle.<ref>{{cite news|work=[[Tampa Bay Times]]|date=March 17, 1981|title=Theater time clock|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/109770507/|via=Newspapers.com|page=4D}}</ref>}}

==Reception==
=== Box office===
''The Funhouse'' earned $2,765,456 in the United States during its opening weekend and went on to gross $7,886,857 in total.<ref name=BOM>{{cite web|url=https://boxofficemojo.com/movies/?id=funhouse.htm|work=[[Box Office Mojo]]| title=The Funhouse | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250219205030/https://www.boxofficemojo.com/release/rl2069202433/weekend/|archive-date=February 19, 2025|url-status=live}}</ref> Though it earned a profit, the film was a commercial disappointment.{{sfn|Rockoff|2002|page=110}} In his book ''Going to Pieces: The Rise and Fall of the Slasher Film'', Adam Rockoff posits that the film's combining of elements from slasher films and monster movies "never struck a chord with audiences" at the time of its release.{{sfn|Rockoff|2002|page=110}}

===Critical response===
{{Rotten Tomatoes prose|68||22|consensus=|access-date=May 25, 2026|ref=y}} [[Metacritic]], which uses a [[Weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average]], assigned the {{#switch:  | episode = episode | series = series | season = season | film }} a score of 56 out of 100, based on 7 critics, indicating "mixed or average" reviews.<ref name="Metacritic">{{cite web |title=''The Funhouse'' |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-funhouse |website=[[Metacritic]] |publisher=[[Fandom (website)#Fandom, Inc.|Fandom, Inc.]] |access-date=May 25, 2026 |archive-url= |archive-date= |url-status=}}</ref> [[Tobe Hooper]] was specifically praised for bringing style and suspense to what could have been a standard early-1980s blood and gore-focused horror film, and his work here was largely responsible for him getting the job of directing the [[Poltergeist (1982 film)|original ''Poltergeist'' movie]]. 

John Corry of ''[[The New York Times]]'' gave the film a middling review, noting: "At times, in fact, Mr. Hooper almost persuades us that he is up to more than just gore, creepiness and trauma. He has photographed a carnival - freak show, girly show, grifters and geeks -with a sense of style. The carnival is a small vision of middle-America gone sour, reveling in mean gaiety, and it is not bad while it lasts. Then the monster comes in and drools."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1981/03/14/movies/funhouse-by-tobe-hooper.html|work=[[The New York Times]]|first=John |last=Corry| title='Funhouse' by Tobe Hooper| date=March 14, 1981|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231111144516/https://www.nytimes.com/1981/03/14/movies/funhouse-by-tobe-hooper.html|archive-date=November 11, 2023|url-status=live| page=11}}</ref> ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]''{{'}}s review of the film was similarly mixed: "For all the elegance of photography, [the] pic has nothing in particular up its sleeves, and devotees of director Tobe Hooper’s ''[[The Texas Chain Saw Massacre]]'' will be particularly disappointed with the almost total lack of shocks and mayhem."<ref>{{cite news|title=Review: 'The Funhouse'|date=March 18, 1981| url=https://variety.com/1980/film/reviews/the-funhouse-1200424846/| work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231117112957/https://variety.com/1980/film/reviews/the-funhouse-1200424846/|archive-date=November 17, 2023| page=133| author=''Variety'' Staff}}</ref>

Film critic [[Gene Siskel]] of the ''[[Chicago Tribune]]'' liked the film and gave it a positive review.<ref name=Siskel>{{cite magazine|work=[[Fangoria]]|issue=15|date=1981|title=Taste Will Tell...|last=Reboy|first=Joseph|pages=39–43|url=https://archive.org/details/fangoria-015-halloween-2-1981}}</ref> He also cited it as one of his "guilty pleasures" in a 1987 ''[[At the Movies (1986 TV program)|Siskel & Ebert At the Movies]]'' special, giving the film credit for having an interesting story, creative direction, and even a somewhat sympathetic villain.<ref>{{cite episode|title=Guilty Pleasures|series=[[At the Movies (1986 TV program)|Siskel & Ebert and the Movies]]|publisher=[[WBBM-TV]]|date=May 16, 1987}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|work=[[Collider (website)|Collider]]|url=https://collider.com/the-funhouse-1981/|title=Quentin Tarantino, Roger Ebert, and Gene Siskel All Agree on This '80s Horror Movie|last=Van Horn|first=Shawn|date=September 28, 2024|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250817220734/https://collider.com/the-funhouse-1981/|archive-date=August 17, 2025}}</ref> In a review published in ''[[People (magazine)|People]]'', the film was praised: "While the director, Tobe (''The Texas Chainsaw Massacre'') Hooper, ought to have moved on to better things, he is the master of this gore-and-sadism genre...&nbsp;The film features an excruciatingly tense final confrontation.<ref>{{cite news|title=Picks and Pans Review: The Funhouse|work=[[People (magazine)|People]]|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180704035636/https://people.com/archive/picks-and-pans-review-the-funhouse-vol-15-no-16/|archive-date=July 4, 2018|date=April 27, 1981|author=''People'' Staff|url=https://people.com/archive/picks-and-pans-review-the-funhouse-vol-15-no-16/}}</ref> Alex Keneas of ''[[Newsday]]'' also gave the film a positive review: "''The Funhouse'' doesn't trade on gratuitous and graphic gore, but it doesn't have to. In little ways and using the traditional tried and true devices of the genre ... it skillfully heightens expectations [and] nicely evokes the chiller of a bygone era as it pays respect to [[Alfred Hitchcock|Hitchcock]] and [[James Whale]]."<ref name=Keneas>{{Cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/109767105/|work=[[Newsday]]|last=Keneas|first=Alex|date=March 13, 1981|page=II-7|title=Movie reviews/'The Funhouse'|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> Ted Mahar of ''[[The Oregonian]]'' heralded the film as a "superior gory horror movie...&nbsp;Hooper maintains the fever pitch of tension and growing terror with rare skill."<ref name=Mahar/> He also praised its references to classic horror films such as ''[[Frankenstein (1931 film)|Frankenstein]]'' (1931) and ''[[Freaks (1932 film)|Freaks]]'' (1932).<ref name=Mahar>{{cite news|work=[[The Oregonian]]|title='The Funhouse' terror classic|last=Mahar|first=Ted|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-oregonian/198227753/|date=March 26, 1981|page=D1|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> 

William Whitaker of the ''[[Abilene Reporter-News]]'' commended the film's performances, cinematography, musical score, screenplay, and pacing, summarizing it as a "stylish" horror film.<ref name=Whitaker>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/abilene-reporter-news/198262006/|work=[[Abilene Reporter-News]]|date=March 15, 1981|last=Whitaker|first=William|title='Funhouse' Shows Style|page=2-B|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref>

===Modern appraisal===
Writer [[John Kenneth Muir]] characterizes ''The Funhouse'' as an example of a self-reflexive horror film "before such things were popular with audiences" and cites it as a "masterpiece" of self-reflexive horror films: "''The Funhouse''...&nbsp;knowingly and cleverly dances on two levels of meaning. On its basic "surface" level, ''The Funhouse'' is a dead teenager movie like ''Halloween'' (1978), ''[[Scream (1996 film)|Scream]]'' (1996), and ''[[I Know What You Did Last Summer]]'' (1997). On a deeper and more meaningful thematic level, however, this is a film about horror films in general and in particular about the glut of "dead teenagers" inhabiting American cinemas in the early eighties."{{sfn|Muir|2015|page=77}}

In his 2022 book ''[[Cinema Speculation]]'', filmmaker [[Quentin Tarantino]] wrote that a rewatch of ''[[The Funhouse]]'' led him to reassess the film, praising its cinematography, direction, atmosphere, and characterizations. He described Lawrence J. Block's screenplay as "the big surprise," writing that what initially appeared "simple and obvious" ultimately revealed "both a level of depth and even sophistication" that "forced me to reconsider the whole film."<ref>{{cite book |last=Tarantino |first=Quentin |title=Cinema Speculation |publisher=Harper |year=2022 |isbn=978-0-06-311258-2}}</ref>

==Novelization==
{{Main|The Funhouse (novel){{!}}''The Funhouse'' (novel)}}
A [[novelization]] of the screenplay was written by [[Dean Koontz]], under the pseudonym Owen West.<ref>{{cite web|work=[[Screen Rant]]|url=https://screenrant.com/dark-rivers-heart-show-updates-release-date-story/|title=Dark Rivers Of The Heart Updates: Is The Dean Koontz Adaptation Still Happening?|last=Cotter|first=Padraig|date=June 2, 2020|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241127090008/https://screenrant.com/dark-rivers-heart-show-updates-release-date-story/|archive-date=November 27, 2024}}</ref> As the film production took longer than expected, the book was released before the film. The novel contains a great deal of backstory and characterization which was not used in the film, including a [[Satanism|Satanic]] subplot and several religious themes culled from Koontz's own [[Catholic Church|Catholic]] background.<ref name=Le>{{cite web|work=[[Bloody Disgusting]]|url=https://bloody-disgusting.com/editorials/3802540/the-funhouse-revisiting-tobe-hoopers-1981-slasher-and-dean-koontzs-novelization/|last=Lê|first=Paul|date=March 13, 2024|title=‘The Funhouse’ – Revisiting Tobe Hooper’s 1981 Slasher and Dean Koontz’s Novelization|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241115230637/https://bloody-disgusting.com/editorials/3802540/the-funhouse-revisiting-tobe-hoopers-1981-slasher-and-dean-koontzs-novelization/|archive-date=November 15, 2024}}</ref>
