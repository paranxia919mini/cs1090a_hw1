'''''Safe''''' is a 1995 [[Psychological drama|psychological drama film]] written and directed by [[Todd Haynes]]. The film stars [[Julianne Moore]], [[Peter Friedman]], [[Xander Berkeley]], and [[James LeGros]]. Set in [[Los Angeles]] in 1987, it follows Carol White (Moore), a suburban housewife whose monotonous life abruptly changes when she becomes sick with a mysterious illness which she believes is caused by the environment around her. [[Principal photography]] began in Los Angeles on January 3, 1994, and concluded on February 13.

''Safe'' premiered at the [[Sundance Film Festival]] on January 25, 1995, and was theatrically released in the United States on June 30. Despite underperforming commercially, the film received critical acclaim, with particular praise for Moore's performance. It received four nominations at the [[11th Independent Spirit Awards]], [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Film|Best Feature]], [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Female Lead|Best Female Lead]] for Moore, and [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Director|Best Director]] and [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Screenplay|Best Screenplay]] for Haynes.

''Safe'' topped the "best film of the 1990s" poll by ''[[The Village Voice]]'',<ref name=":13">{{Cite web|url=https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCOzFilLNcgrGzAeECAbUFCQ|title="Todd Haynes Q&A {{!}} Safe", Retrospective of Todd Haynes films|date=25 November 2015|website=Film at Lincoln Center, YouTube|access-date=30 October 2019}}</ref><ref>[https://www.criterion.com/films/28548-safe Criterion Collection]</ref> and was described by critics as "the scariest film of the year",<ref name=":13" /> "a mesmerizing horror movie",<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/longterm/movies/videos/saferkempley_c026c9.htm|title=Safe|last=Kempley|first=Rita|date=4 August 1995|newspaper=The Washington Post|access-date=30 October 2019}}</ref> and "a work of [[feminist cinema|feminist]] counter-cinema".<ref>Geller, Theresa L. "The Hardest, The Most Difficult Film: Todd Haynes' Safe as Feminist Film Praxis or what the fuck is this" or "An Indelible Mark: Women and the Work of Todd Haynes," a presentation, Grinnell College, 2013. page 6.</ref> In 2015, Haynes said that the themes explored throughout the film, disease and [[Immunity (medicine)|immunity]] in a post-industrial landscape and how recovery is a burden often placed on victims of illness, were even more relevant than they were at the time of its release.

==Plot==
In 1987, Carol White is a housewife living in an affluent suburb of Los Angeles with her husband Greg and stepson Rory. She spends her days gardening, doing [[aerobics]], and meeting friends. Her marriage and family life appear stable but sterile, and her friends are polite but distant. After the family's home is renovated, Carol begins experiencing physical symptoms in everyday situations: She coughs uncontrollably when exposed to exhaust fumes from a nearby truck while driving, suffers a panic attack at a [[baby shower]], and has a [[nosebleed]] while getting a perm at a hair salon. As her symptoms worsen, she becomes convinced that they are triggered by exposure to chemicals. Finally, she collapses while at her [[Dry cleaning|dry cleaners]], which is being fumigated with [[pesticide]]s.

Doctors are unable to diagnose or treat Carol and state that she is physically healthy. She attends [[psychotherapy]] sessions, but her symptoms do not improve. She finds herself very alone with her condition, as her community remains indifferent to her suffering. While hospitalized, Carol watches an advertisement for Wrenwood, a [[New Age|new-age]] desert community for people with "[[Environmental disease|environmental illnesses]]." The commune is led by Peter Dunning, an author who encourages residents to use self-help techniques. Realizing that she can no longer function in her current life, she leaves everything behind and moves to Wrenwood.

Even in a community of people who are friendly towards her and suffer from similar health problems, Carol becomes increasingly isolated despite claiming that she is getting better. Lesions appear on her face and she increasingly relies on an oxygen tank. Eventually, she moves into an insulated dome separated from the rest of the community. There, as a "treatment" suggested by others in the commune, she looks into a mirror and repeats, "I love you" to herself.

==Production==
Haynes first heard about "environmental illness" in 1991 on a [[News magazine|TV magazine program]] that referred to it as "20th Century Disease".<ref>{{Cite episode |title=Safe: Todd Haynes |episode-link= |url=https://www.sbs.com.au/movies/video/11700291765/Safe-Todd-Haynes |access-date=August 1, 2021 |series=[[The Movie Show]] |network=[[SBS (Australian TV channel)|SBS TV]] |date=May 31, 1995 |number=14 |time=1:30 }}</ref> He and producer Vachon interviewed organizations that advocate for people who have environmental illness, such as Response Team for the Chemically Injured in Atascadero, California and The Chemical Connection in Wimberley, Texas.<ref>{{cite AV media |people=Issa Clubb |date=2014 |title=Safe |chapter=Christine Vachon |medium=DVD |time=4:53 |publisher=The Criterion Collection |isbn=9781604659313 |oclc=950047934}}</ref><ref name="MacLean" /> The monologue that Carol gives during her birthday party was based on transcripts of one of the interviews from Wimberley.<ref>{{cite AV media |people=Issa Clubb |date=2014 |title=Safe |chapter=Todd Haynes and Julianne Moore |medium=DVD |time=31:49 |publisher=The Criterion Collection |isbn=9781604659313 |oclc=950047934}}</ref> Haynes also did research into [[New Age]] healing practices, and was especially interested in the work of [[Louise Hay]], whose books became popular among gay men during the [[HIV/AIDS in the United States|AIDS epidemic]] by telling them that self-love would heal their illness.<ref name="MacLean">{{cite web |last=MacLean |first=Alison |title=Todd Haynes |url=https://bombmagazine.org/articles/todd-haynes/ |website=[[BOMB (magazine)|BOMB]] |date=July 1, 1995 |accessdate=August 1, 2021}}</ref> The fictional Wrenwood was inspired by a yoga retreat at [[Kripalu Center]].<ref name="MacLean" /> Haynes also drew inspiration from films such as ''[[Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles]]'', ''[[2001: A Space Odyssey (film)|2001: A Space Odyssey]]'' and ''[[The Boy in the Plastic Bubble]]''.<ref name="Filmmaker"/>

For the script, Haynes has said that the conceptual origin involved setting up barriers that prevent the audience from getting emotionally close to the character of Carol, which was a concept he explored again in ''[[Far from Heaven]]''.<ref>{{cite AV media |people=Issa Clubb |date=2014 |title=Safe |chapter=Todd Haynes and Julianne Moore |medium=DVD |time=5:03 |publisher=The Criterion Collection |isbn=9781604659313 |oclc=950047934}}</ref> He used political red herrings to make the audience trust certain characters at first, such as the leader of Wrenwood being a gay man. Since Haynes himself is gay, he thought the audience would expect the gay character to be trustworthy.<ref>{{Cite episode |title=Writer and Director Todd Haynes Doesn't Play It "Safe" |url=https://freshairarchive.org/segments/writer-and-director-todd-haynes-doesnt-play-it-safe |access-date=August 1, 2021 |series=[[Fresh Air]] |network=WHYY-FM |date=June 28, 1995 |time=9:28 }}</ref>

For the role of Carol, Haynes was initially interested in Susan Norman—who was later cast as Linda instead—but Julianne Moore's agent reached out to Vachon and insisted on an interview with her for the role.<ref>{{cite AV media |people=Issa Clubb |date=2014 |title=Safe |chapter=Christine Vachon |medium=DVD |time=2:45 |publisher=The Criterion Collection |isbn=9781604659313 |oclc=950047934}}</ref> Moore knew exactly how to play the character of Carol as soon as she read the script: "I wanted the character to not put any weight on her [[larynx]] at all", so that's what she did in the audition that won her the part.<ref>{{cite AV media |people=Issa Clubb |date=2014 |title=Safe |chapter=Todd Haynes and Julianne Moore |medium=DVD |time=1:35 |publisher=The Criterion Collection |isbn=9781604659313 |oclc=950047934}}</ref>

Cinematographer [[Alex Nepomniaschy]] suggested the look of ''[[Red Desert (film)|Red Desert]]'' to Haynes after reading the script, and together they decided never to let the camera get very close to any of the characters as a way to keep emotional distance.<ref name="Filmmaker"/>

Potential investors wanted him to replicate the elements that had worked well in Haynes' previous film ''[[Poison (1991 film)|Poison]]'', but since this film was different in so many ways, it took a long time to find the funding for it.<ref>{{cite web|last=Clarke |first=Roger |title=The bad boy and the bubble |url=https://www.independent.co.uk/arts-entertainment/the-bad-boy-and-the-bubble-1305267.html |website=[[The Independent]] |date=April 16, 1996 |accessdate=August 2, 2021}}</ref> The budget was around $1 million.<ref name="Filmmaker"/> Finally, filming began on January 1, 1994, in Los Angeles, and lasted 6 weeks.<ref name="Filmmaker">{{cite web |last=Gross |first=Larry |title=Antibodies |url=https://www.filmmakermagazine.com/archives/issues/summer1995/antibodies.php |website=[[Filmmaker Magazine]] |date=1995 |accessdate=August 1, 2021}}</ref>

For the film's premiere at Sundance, Haynes had removed the shot of Peter's mansion, but put it back in after hearing audience feedback because he wanted to emphasize Peter's hypocrisy.<ref name="Filmmaker"/>

Ultimately, the film presents no answer for her illness or predicament. Her condition is given no name in the film, but director Haynes confirmed that it is a depiction of [[multiple chemical sensitivity]].<ref name=":13" /><ref name=":14">{{Cite web|url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EZmjaC8cN10 |archive-url=https://ghostarchive.org/varchive/youtube/20211213/EZmjaC8cN10 |archive-date=December 13, 2021 |url-status=live|title=Todd Haynes and Julianne Moore on Safe|website=CriterionCollection, YouTube|date=December 12, 2014 |access-date=October 30, 2019}}{{cbignore}}</ref> He also said that Carol's isolation was both the answer and the problem for her.<ref name=":13" />

==Release==
The film had its world premiere at the [[Sundance Film Festival]] on January 25, 1995.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://variety.com/1995/film/reviews/safe-3-1200439942/|title=Review: 'Safe'|website=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|first=Todd|last=McCarthy|date=January 26, 1995|access-date=October 24, 2016}}</ref> There were reports of people walking out of the theater because they didn't understand the movie.<ref>{{cite AV media |people=Issa Clubb |date=2014 |title=Safe |chapter=Todd Haynes and Julianne Moore |medium=DVD |time=25:50 |publisher=The Criterion Collection |isbn=9781604659313 |oclc=950047934}}</ref> Vachon has said that critics at the premiere were mixed about the film, but by the end of the decade many had come around to it and placed the film among the decade's best.<ref>{{cite AV media |people=Issa Clubb |date=2014 |title=Safe |chapter=Christine Vachon |medium=DVD |time=11:54 |publisher=The Criterion Collection |isbn=9781604659313 |oclc=950047934}}</ref>

In May of 1995, it played at the [[48th Cannes Film Festival]] in the [[Directors' Fortnight]] section.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.quinzaine-cineastes.fr/fr/edition/1995|title=1995 Edition|website=[[Directors' Fortnight]]|access-date=October 29, 2024}}</ref>

[[Sony Pictures Classics]] acquired distribution rights to the film and released the film in a [[limited release]] on June 23, 1995.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.sonyclassics.com/safe/safe.html|title=Safe|website=[[Sony Pictures Classics]]|access-date=October 24, 2016}}</ref>

==Reception and legacy==
===Critical response===
''Safe'' received positive reviews from critics. [[Rotten Tomatoes]] reports 88% approval based on 67 reviews. The website's critics consensus reads, "''Safe''{{'s}} eerie social satire and somewhat sterile stylization is balanced by comedic undertones and an impressive, understated performance from Julianne Moore."<ref>{{Citation|last = Maslin|first = Janet|title = Life of a Hollow Woman|work = [[The New York Times]]|date = June 23, 1995|url = https://www.nytimes.com/movie/review?res=990CE1D71F3AF930A15755C0A963958260|access-date = January 4, 2015}}</ref><ref>{{Citation|title = ''Safe''|publisher = Fandango Media|year = 2015|url = https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/safe/|access-date = March 8, 2026}}</ref> The film also holds a score of 76/100 on [[Metacritic]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.metacritic.com/movie/safe-1995 |title=Safe Reviews - Metacritic |publisher=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=January 8, 2016}}</ref> [[Janet Maslin]], writing in ''[[The New York Times]]'', lauds the first half of the film, but concludes that, as "brilliantly as it begins, ''Safe'' eventually succumbs to its own modern malady, as the film maker insists on a chilly ambiguity that breeds more detachment than interest ... Mr. Haynes makes fools of ... [the film's]  New Agers while possibly embracing some of their views." Another problem, according to Maslin, is that "the shadow of AIDS implicitly hangs over …[Carol's] decline, but it doesn't help bring ''Safe'' to a conclusion worthy of its inspired beginning."<ref>Maslin, ''[[Op. cit.]]''</ref>

The ending of the film is highly ambiguous, and has created considerable debate among critics and audiences as to whether Carol has emancipated herself, or simply traded one form of suffocation for an equally constricting identity as a reclusive invalid.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://thefilmstage.com/features/todd-haynes-discusses-safe-letting-go-of-the-past-working-with-julianne-moore-and-carol/|title=Todd Haynes Discusses 'Safe,' Letting Go of the Past, Working With Julianne Moore, and 'Carol'|date=December 15, 2014}}</ref> Julie Grossman argues in her article "The Trouble with Carol" that Haynes concludes the film as a challenge to traditional Hollywood film narratives of the heroine taking charge of her life, and that Haynes sets Carol up as the victim both of a male-dominated society, and also of an equally debilitating [[self-help]] culture that encourages patients to take sole responsibility for their illness and recovery.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.othervoices.org/2.3/jgrossman/|title=The Trouble with Carol: The Costs of Feeling Good in Todd Haynes's [Safe] and the American Cultural Landscape|website=Other Voices|publisher=[[University of Pennsylvania]]|first=Julie|last=Grossman|date=January 2005|access-date=July 11, 2015}}</ref>

Carol's illness, although unidentified, has been seen as an analogy for the 1980s [[HIV/AIDS|AIDS]] crisis, a similarly uncomfortable and largely unspoken "threat" during the [[Reagan presidency]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://thedissolve.com/features/interview/856-todd-haynes-on-the-unsafe-world-of-safe/|title=Todd Haynes on the unsafe world of Safe|website=The Dissolve|access-date=May 27, 2018|archive-date=January 21, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210121114249/https://thedissolve.com/features/interview/856-todd-haynes-on-the-unsafe-world-of-safe/|url-status=dead}}</ref>

===Accolades===
''Safe'' received seven votes in the [[British Film Institute]]'s 2012 ''[[Sight & Sound]]'' poll of the greatest films – with five votes from critics and two from directors – ranking it 323rd and 322nd, respectively.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://explore.bfi.org.uk/sightandsoundpolls/2012/film/4ce2b7c99ed38 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120822003917/http://explore.bfi.org.uk/sightandsoundpolls/2012/film/4ce2b7c99ed38 |url-status=dead |archive-date=August 22, 2012 |title=''Safe'' (1995) |publisher=[[British Film Institute]] |access-date=January 8, 2016}}</ref> 

The movie was widely critically acclaimed. It gave Moore her first leading role in a feature film and gave Haynes a measure of mainstream critical recognition.
