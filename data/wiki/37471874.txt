{{nihongo|'''''The Tale of the Princess Kaguya'''''|かぐや姫の物語|Kaguya-hime no Monogatari|lead=yes}} is a 2013 Japanese animated [[historical fantasy]]<ref>{{cite web |last1=Ross |first1=Carlos |title=''The Tale of Princess Kaguya'' |url=https://www.themanime.org/viewreview.php?id=1876 |website=[[THEM Anime Reviews]] |access-date=21 January 2021 |archive-date=27 January 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210127075109/https://www.themanime.org/viewreview.php?id=1876 |url-status=live }}</ref> [[Drama (film and television)|drama]] film directed by [[Isao Takahata]] from a screenplay he co-wrote with Riko Sakaguchi. Based on ''[[The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter]]'', a 10th-century [[Japanese literature|Japanese literary]] tale, the film follows a bamboo cutter who works with his wife, whose life changes when he meets a living nymph that turns into a beautiful young woman. Themes of [[feminism]], [[freedom]], [[social alienation]], [[impermanence]], and responsibility are explored throughout.

It was produced by [[Studio Ghibli]] for [[Nippon Television Network]], [[Dentsu]], [[Hakuhodo DY Music & Pictures|Hakuhodo DYMP]], [[Walt Disney Studios Home Entertainment|Walt Disney Japan]], [[Mitsubishi Corporation|Mitsubishi]], [[Toho]] and [[KDDI]]. With a budget of [[US$]]49.3 million, it was [[List of most expensive non-English-language films|the most expensive Japanese film]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.filmbiz.asia/news/pokemon-defeats-ghibli-at-japan-box-office|title=Pokemon defeats Ghibli at Japan box office|first=Kevin|last=Ma|date=23 July 2014|access-date=25 July 2014|website=[[Film Business Asia]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140819003920/http://www.filmbiz.asia/news/pokemon-defeats-ghibli-at-japan-box-office|archive-date=19 August 2014|url-status=dead}}</ref> It received critical acclaim and was nominated for the [[Academy Award for Best Animated Feature|Best Animated Feature]] at the [[87th Academy Awards]]. Its production was the subject of the feature-length documentary ''Isao Takahata and His Tale of the Princess Kaguya''.<ref>{{cite web|title=''Isao Takahata and His Tale of the Princess Kaguya''|url=https://www.wildbunch.biz/movie/isao-takahata-and-his-tale-of-the-princess-kaguya/|publisher=[[Wild Bunch (company)|Wild Bunch International Sales]]|access-date=27 February 2019|date=2015|archive-date=26 February 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190226142031/http://www.wildbunch.biz/movie/isao-takahata-and-his-tale-of-the-princess-kaguya/|url-status=live}}</ref>

==Plot==
<!-- Per WP:FILMPLOT, plot summaries for feature films should be between 400 to 700 words only. Please check the word count before making any additions. Please discuss any major changes on the talk page. -->
A bamboo cutter discovers a small scale girl inside a glowing [[bamboo shoot]]. Believing her to be a [[divine presence]], he and his wife decide to raise her as their own, calling her "Princess". The girl grows rapidly, earning her the nickname {{nihongo|"Takenoko"|タケノコ||"Little Bamboo"}} from the village children. {{nihongo|Sutemaru|捨丸}}, the oldest among Takenoko's friends, develops a close relationship with her.

The bamboo cutter comes upon gold and fine cloth in the bamboo grove the same way he found the Princess. He takes these as proof of her divine royalty and begins planning to make her a "noble princess". He relocates the family to the capital, forcing the girl to leave her friends behind, and the family moves into a mansion replete with servants. The girl is saddled with a [[governess]] who is tasked with taming her into a noblewoman. The girl struggles with the restraints of [[nobility]], yearning for her prior life in the countryside.

When she comes of age, she is granted the formal name {{nihongo|"Princess Kaguya"|かぐや姫||"The Shining Princess"}}. The bamboo cutter holds a celebration, where Kaguya overhears partygoers ridiculing his attempts to turn a peasant girl into a noble through money. Kaguya flees the capital in despair and runs back to the mountains, seeking Sutemaru and her friends, but discovers that they have all moved away. She passes out in the snow and awakens back at the party.

Kaguya grows in beauty, attracting suitors. Five noblemen attempt to court her, comparing her to mythical treasures. Kaguya tells them she will only marry whoever can bring her the mythical treasure mentioned. As spring arrives, the Princess wishes to see the [[Cherry blossom|cherry blossoms]] bloom. Her mother and a [[handmaiden]] take her back to the country where a blooming tree stands not far from her old hut. Reveling in its bursting forth with life, she happily whirls amid its petals but stumbles into a child. The child's family beg for her forgiveness and leave, reminding Kaguya of her new identity and status.

Returning to the capital, the cart the group is in finds itself stuck, when a ruckus is heard outside. Kaguya notices several men running through the streets, and upon seeing one of them, recognizes Sutemaru, who is stealing a chicken. Unable to contain herself, she cries out to him, but upon realizing her station in life, quickly retreats into the carriage as it drives off. Sutemaru attempts to go after her, but is beaten by those pursuing him, as Kaguya cries.

Two suitors attempt to persuade her with counterfeits, the third abandons his quest, and the fourth gives Kaguya a flower instead of his treasure, but is found by his wife before Kaguya can accept. When the last suitor dies in his quest, Kaguya becomes depressed. The [[Emperor of Japan]] takes notice of Kaguya's beauty and tries to forcefully bring her to his palace but she convinces him to leave.

Kaguya reveals to her parents her origin. Once a resident of the [[Moon]], she broke its laws, hoping to be exiled to Earth so that she could experience mortal life. When the Emperor made his advances, she silently begged the Moon to help her. Having heard her prayer, the Moon restored her memories and promised reclamation at the next full moon. Kaguya expresses her attachment to Earth and her reluctance to leave; the bamboo cutter swears to protect her and begins turning the mansion into a fortress.

Kaguya returns to her home village and finds Sutemaru. The two profess their love, and in their joy, they fly over the countryside, only to encounter the Moon and fall. Sutemaru wakes up alone and reunites with his wife and child, interpreting the experience as a dream.

On the night of the full moon, a procession of celestial beings led by the [[Buddha]] descends from the Moon, and the fortress is unable to stop it. An attendant offers Kaguya the robe that will erase her memories of Earth. She embraces her family one last time before the robe embraces her. They leave, and the bamboo cutter and his wife are distraught. As Kaguya looks back at Earth, tears fill her eyes.

==Production==

As a child, Takahata read ''The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter''. He recalled that he struggled to relate and sympathize with the protagonist; to him, the "heroine's transformation was enigmatic" and that it "didn't evoke any empathy from [him]".<ref name=":0">{{Cite web|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2014/12/immersed-in-movies-isao-takahata-talks-the-tale-of-the-princess-kaguya-swan-song-123737/|title=Immersed in Movies: Isao Takahata Talks 'The Tale of the Princess Kaguya' Swan Song|last=Desowitz|first=Bill|date=23 December 2014|website=IndieWire|language=en|access-date=8 April 2019|archive-date=13 June 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180613114523/http://www.indiewire.com/2014/12/immersed-in-movies-isao-takahata-talks-the-tale-of-the-princess-kaguya-swan-song-123737/|url-status=live}}</ref> In 1960, Takahata was preparing for a potential adaptation for his employer [[Toei Animation]], which eventually was abandoned.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.smh.com.au/entertainment/movies/the-tale-of-princess-kaguya-is-isao-takahatas-first-film-in-14-years-is-it-also-his-last-20141008-10rvka.html|title=The Tale of Princess Kaguya is Isao Takahata's first film in 14 years; is it also his last?|last=Hawker|first=Philippa|date=8 October 2014|website=The Sydney Morning Herald|language=en|access-date=8 April 2019|archive-date=24 October 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201024163944/https://www.smh.com.au/entertainment/movies/the-tale-of-princess-kaguya-is-isao-takahatas-first-film-in-14-years-is-it-also-his-last-20141008-10rvka.html|url-status=live}}</ref> After rereading the tale, he realized the story's potential to be entertaining, as long as an adaptation allowed the audience to understand how Princess Kaguya felt.<ref name=":0" /><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.gamesradar.com/isao-takahata-interview/|title=Isao Takahata Talks The Tale of Princess Kaguya|date=27 August 2015|website=gamesradar|language=EN-US|access-date=8 April 2019|archive-date=21 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200621011655/https://www.gamesradar.com/isao-takahata-interview/|url-status=live}}</ref>

Studio Ghibli revealed that Isao Takahata was working on a feature-length film in 2008.<ref name="ann20080101">{{cite web|url=https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2009-08-12/isao-takahata-to-base-next-film-on-taketori-monogatari|title=Ghibli's Takahata, Goro Miyazaki Developing New Works|date=1 February 2008|work=[[Anime News Network]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121216054346/http://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2009-08-12/isao-takahata-to-base-next-film-on-taketori-monogatari|archive-date=16 December 2012|url-status=live|access-date=27 December 2012}}</ref> Takahata announced at the 62nd [[Locarno International Film Festival]] in 2009 that he intended to direct a film based on the anonymous Japanese literary tale ''[[The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter]]''.<ref name="ann20090812">{{cite web|url=https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2009-08-12/isao-takahata-to-base-next-film-on-taketori-monogatari|title=Isao Takahata to Base Next Film on ''Taketori Monogatari''|date=12 August 2009|work=[[Anime News Network]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121112213837/http://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2009-08-12/isao-takahata-to-base-next-film-on-taketori-monogatari|archive-date=12 November 2012|url-status=live|access-date=28 October 2012}}</ref>

To make sure the audience emotionally connected with the film, it was important to Takahata that viewers were able to "imagine or recall the reality deep within the drawings", rather than be distracted by a realistic art style.<ref>{{Cite magazine|url=https://www.wired.co.uk/article/isao-takahata-interview|title=Studio Ghibli's Isao Takahata on animating his final film|last=Kamen|first=Matt|date=19 March 2015|magazine=Wired UK|access-date=9 April 2019|issn=1357-0978|archive-date=7 April 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180407115854/http://www.wired.co.uk/article/isao-takahata-interview|url-status=live}}</ref> He wanted to have people "recollect the realities of this life by sketching ordinary human qualities with simple props".<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.latimes.com/entertainment/movies/la-et-mn-isao-takahata-appreciation-20180409-story.html|title=Studio Ghibli co-founder Isao Takahata was a true poet of Japanese animation|last=Solomon|first=Charles|date=9 April 2018|website=[[Los Angeles Times]]|access-date=8 April 2019|archive-date=3 October 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181003215358/http://www.latimes.com/entertainment/movies/la-et-mn-isao-takahata-appreciation-20180409-story.html|url-status=live}}</ref> To assist with this vision, Osamu Tanabe provided the character designs and animation, and [[Kazuo Oga]] drew the watercolor backgrounds.<ref name=":1" /> 

The film uses more experimental animation aesthetics where "imperfections" remain in the image (such as stray pencil lines or color bleed).<ref name=":2">{{Cite journal |last=Carroll |first=William |date=2026 |title=Perfected Imperfection: Experimental Aesthetics in Takahata Isao's My Neighbors the Yamadas and The Tale of the Princess Kaguya |url=https://muse.jhu.edu/article/997356 |journal=Mechademia: Second Arc |volume=18 |issue=2 |pages=116 |via=Project MUSE}}</ref> Takahata cited experimental French Canadian animator Frédéric Back as an influence on the film, as well as ''[[My Neighbors the Yamadas]].''<ref name=":2" />

== Themes ==

=== Feminism ===
''The Tale of the Princess Kaguya'' has been noted for its [[Feminism|feminist]] themes. Kaguya's move to the capital and her father's insistence that she adopt the life of a noblewoman run counter to her wishes, while her mother's limited role in decision-making highlights the lack of female agency in the household. Yalcinkaya points to a [[dream sequence]] in which Kaguya breaks through a series of doors as a metaphor for the barriers imposed by family and society.<ref name="Yalcinkaya">{{Cite web |last=Yalcinkaya |first=Gunseli |date=12 April 2018 |title=why the themes isao takahata explored were so important |url=https://i-d.co/article/why-the-themes-isao-takahata-explored-were-so-important/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220611103853/https://mb.moatads.com/s/v2?url=https%3A%2F%2Fi-d.vice.com%2Fen_uk%2Farticle%2F8xkkxa%2Fwhy-the-themes-isao-takahata-explored-were-so-important&pcode=viceheader426995670975&ord=1654943928127&jv=386447513&callback=BrandSafetyNadoscallback_51019374 |archive-date=11 June 2022 |access-date=3 October 2020 |website=i-D |language=en}}</ref>

The film also critiques ideals of beauty and propriety during the [[Heian period]]. According to Monji, Kaguya's training in court etiquette, which required her to suppress her emotions, amounts to a painful renunciation of humanity. Her decision to set impossible tasks for her suitors has further been read as a depiction of female resistance within a male-dominated hierarchy.<ref name="Monji">{{Cite web |last=Monji |first=Jana J. |date=17 December 2014 |title=Interpreting Isao Takahata's 'The Tale of Princess Kaguya' |url=https://ageofthegeek.org/2014/12/17/interpreting-isao-takahatas-the-tale-of-princess-kaguya/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201024093405/https://ageofthegeek.org/2014/12/17/interpreting-isao-takahatas-the-tale-of-princess-kaguya/ |archive-date=24 October 2020 |access-date=3 October 2020 |website=Pasadena Art & Science Beat |language=en}}</ref>

=== Nature and freedom ===
The contrast between rural life and the palace is a central motif. The countryside is shown through muted colors and soft lines that emphasize simplicity and joy, while the capital is rendered with bolder hues suggesting indulgence and confinement.<ref name="Yalcinkaya" /> In one sequence, Kaguya flees the palace in a style animated with rapid, unrestrained brushstrokes, visually expressing her frustration and despair. She observes that her vitality fades as the story progresses, culminating in a scene where she overhears men mocking her origins; her shrinking figure against a backdrop of darkness underscores her sense of isolation.<ref name="catchinglight.org">{{Cite web |last=Shi |first=Stephanie |date=14 September 2017 |title=The Tale of Princess Kaguya: The Power of Animation as a Medium |url=http://www.catchinglight.org/tale-princess-kaguya-animation-medium/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200220211808/http://www.catchinglight.org/tale-princess-kaguya-animation-medium/ |archive-date=20 February 2020 |access-date=3 October 2020 |website=Catching Light |language=en-US}}</ref>

Both rural and aristocratic life are portrayed as tragic: peasants are constrained by poverty, while aristocrats are bound by the rigidity of class expectations.<ref name="Yalcinkaya" /><ref name="catchinglight.org" />

=== Life, suffering and responsibility ===
The ending frames earthly life as inseparable from grief but also from beauty. The deities who descend to return Kaguya to the Moon are depicted in bright hues and accompanied by joyful music, contrasting with the darker tones of Earth and the sorrow of her family. She interprets this juxtaposition as suggesting that, despite suffering, earthly existence holds wonder and meaning; a point Kaguya affirms before her departure.<ref name="catchinglight.org" />

The film also draws on the concept of [[mono no aware]], the fleeting beauty of existence. The figure who arrives to take Kaguya back to the Moon resembles a Buddha accompanied by heavenly attendants, evoking the [[Pure Land]] tradition in which practitioners believe they are guided to a realm of peace after death. The Moon, which Kaguya recalls as a place free from earthly pain and longing, parallels this vision of divine solitude. Her return can thus be interpreted as a metaphor for death and the impermanence of human life.<ref>{{Cite web |title=The Tale of Princess Kaguya |url=https://www.eagle.pitt.edu/sites/default/files/Princess%20Kaguya%20Culture%20Notes.pdf |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210815000312/https://www.eagle.pitt.edu/sites/default/files/Princess%20Kaguya%20Culture%20Notes.pdf |archive-date=15 August 2021 |access-date=9 August 2021 |website=East Asia Gateway}}</ref>

The film also reflects on adulthood and responsibility. Monji reads Kaguya's reluctant acceptance of her social duties as an allegory for the necessity of living within society's restrictions. This theme extends to her adoptive parents, whose actions, though sometimes misguided, are framed as motivated by love and duty toward their daughter.<ref name="Yalcinkaya" />

Club]]'' gave the film an A, deeming it "the best animated movie of the year", adding that it is "destined to be remembered as one of the revered Studio Ghibli's finest achievements."<ref>{{cite web|url= https://www.avclub.com/review/studio-ghibli-delivers-new-masterpiece-tale-prince-210452|title= Studio Ghibli delivers a new masterpiece with The Tale of Princess Kaguya|date= 16 October 2014|access-date= 16 October 2014|work= [[The A.V. Club]]|archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20141017213935/http://www.avclub.com/review/studio-ghibli-delivers-new-masterpiece-tale-prince-210452|archive-date= 17 October 2014|url-status= live|df= dmy-all}}</ref> Nicolas Rapold of ''The New York Times'' praised the artwork calling it "exquisitely drawn with both watercolor delicacy and a brisk sense of line".<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2014/10/17/movies/the-tale-of-the-princess-kaguya-from-isao-takahata.html?ref=movies|title='The Tale of the Princess Kaguya,' From Isao Takahata|last=Rapold|first=Nicolas|date=16 October 2014|work=The New York Times|access-date=18 January 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141027083840/http://www.nytimes.com/2014/10/17/movies/the-tale-of-the-princess-kaguya-from-isao-takahata.html?ref=movies|archive-date=27 October 2014|url-status=live}}</ref> For [[IndieWire]]'s 2018 list of the best Japanese films of the 21st century, Carlos Aguilar expressed agreement with the common view that ''[[Spirited Away]]'' is the greatest, but still chose ''The Tale of the Princess Kaguya'' for the list and referred to the latter as "a work of nearly identical caliber{{nbsp}}... an artistic triumph that delights with exuberant handcraft where each pencil stroke comes alive on screen. Takahata made something at once pastoral, timeless, and epic in proportion with an emotional depth rarely seen in films – animated or not."<ref>{{Cite web|last=Ehrlich|first=David|date=26 March 2018|title=The Best Japanese Films of the 21st Century – IndieWire Critics Survey|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2018/03/best-japanese-movies-21st-century-1201943800/|access-date=10 January 2021|website=IndieWire|language=en|archive-date=27 November 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201127072105/https://www.indiewire.com/2018/03/best-japanese-movies-21st-century-1201943800/|url-status=live}}</ref>

In July 2025, it was one of the films voted for the "Readers' Choice" edition of ''[[The New York Times]]''{{'}} list of "The 100 Best Movies of the 21st Century," finishing at number 279.<ref>{{cite web |title=Readers Choose Their Top Movies of the 21st Century |url=https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2025/movies/readers-movies-21st-century.html |website=The New York Times |access-date=2 July 2025}}</ref>
