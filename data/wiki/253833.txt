'''''The Mummy''''' is a 1932 American [[Pre-Code Hollywood|pre-Code]] [[supernatural horror film]] directed by [[Karl Freund]], and starring [[Boris Karloff]], [[Zita Johann]], [[David Manners]], [[Edward Van Sloan]], and [[Arthur Byron]]. The screenplay by [[John L. Balderston]] was adapted from a treatment written by [[Nina Wilcox Putnam]] and [[Richard Schayer]]. Released by [[Universal Pictures|Universal Studios]] as a part of the [[Universal Monsters]] franchise, the film features Karloff as [[Imhotep (The Mummy)|Imhotep]], an ancient Egyptian [[mummy (undead)|mummy]] who was killed for attempting to resurrect his dead lover, [[Ankhesenamun|Anck-es-en-Amon]]. After being discovered and accidentally brought to life by a team of archaeologists, he disguises himself as a modern Egyptian named Ardeth Bey and searches for Anck-es-en-Amon, who he believes has been reincarnated in the modern world.

Universal released ''The Mummy'' theatrically in the United States on December 22, 1932. While less profitable than its predecessors ''[[Dracula (1931 English-language film)|Dracula]]'' and ''[[Frankenstein (1931 film)|Frankenstein]]'', ''The Mummy'' was still a commercial and critical success, becoming culturally influential and spawning [[The Mummy (franchise)|several sequels, spin-offs, remakes, and reimaginings]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=March 21, 2024|title=The Mummy {{!}} Horror Classic, Universal Pictures, Karl Freund|url=https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Mummy |archive-url=https://archive.today/20251005181429/https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Mummy|archive-date=October 5, 2025|url-status=live |website=[[Encyclopædia Britannica]] |language=en}}</ref> The film and its sequels cemented the [[Mummy (undead)|mummy archetype]] as a staple of the horror genre and [[Halloween]] festivities.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Schmitt |first=Morgan |date=October 28, 2020|title='The Mummy,' now on Prime, remains Halloween classic. |url=https://go.gale.com/ps/i.do?id=GALE%7CA639680197&sid=sitemap&v=2.1&it=r&p=AONE&sw=w&userGroupName=anon~46f52644&aty=open-web-entry |journal=UWIRE Text |language=English |p=1|url-access=subscription}}</ref>

==Plot==
In 1921, an [[archaeology|archaeological]] expedition led by Sir Joseph Whemple finds the mummy of an [[ancient Egypt]]ian high priest named [[Imhotep (The Mummy)|Imhotep]]. An inspection of the mummy by Whemple's friend Dr. Muller reveals that the mummy's [[viscera]] were not removed, and from the signs of struggling, Muller deduces that although Imhotep had been wrapped like a traditional mummy, he had been buried alive. Also buried with Imhotep is a casket bearing a [[Curse of the pharaohs|curse]]. Despite Muller's warning, Sir Joseph's assistant, Ralph Norton, opens it and finds an ancient, life-giving [[Book of the Dead|scroll]], the "Scroll of [[Thoth]]". He translates the symbols and then reads the words aloud, causing Imhotep to rise from the dead. Norton is horrified and laughs hysterically as Imhotep shuffles off with the scroll.

Ten years later, Imhotep has assimilated into modern society, taking the identity of an eccentric Egyptian historian named Ardath Bey. He calls upon Sir Joseph's son Frank and Professor Pearson and shows them where to dig to find the tomb of the princess [[Ankhesenamun|Ankh-es-en-Amon]]. After locating the tomb, the archaeologists present its treasures to the [[Egyptian Museum|Cairo Museum]], after which Ardath disappears. The professor recalls that Sir Joseph's assistant Norton went insane and died laughing shortly afterward.

Ardath soon meets Helen Grosvenor, a half-Egyptian woman who bears a striking resemblance to the princess, and she stays with Muller. Ardath falls in love with her, but so does Frank. After it is discovered that Ardath is the mummy Imhotep, Muller urges Joseph to burn the Scroll of Thoth, but when Joseph tries to do so, Ardath uses his magical powers to kill him and then hypnotizes Joseph's [[Nubians|Nubian]] servant to be his slave and bring the Scroll to him, as the Nubian's Egyptian-related bloodline is susceptible to Imhotep's magic. After the servant does so, he hypnotizes Helen to come to his place and there, reveals to her that his horrific death was punishment for [[sacrilege]], as he attempted to resurrect his lover, Princess Ankh-es-en-Amon.

Believing her to be the princess's [[reincarnation]], he attempts to make her his immortal bride by killing, mummifying, and resurrecting her. Frank and Muller come to her rescue and distract Ardath long enough for Helen, now possessed by Ankh-es-en-Amon's spirit, to appeal to [[Isis (goddess)|Isis]] to come to her aid. The statue of Isis raises its arm and emits a flash that sets the Scroll of Thoth on fire, breaking the spell that had given Imhotep his immortality as his body dies and turns to dust. In Frank's arms, Ankh-es-en-Amon's spirit leaves Helen's body, as Helen awakens as herself.

==Production==
===Development===
[[File:Cagliostro (1932 Universal Pictures ad sheet).jpg|thumb|Concept art for ''Cagliostro'', a film project based on the historical occultist [[Alessandro Cagliostro]], which served as the basis for what became ''The Mummy''.]]
Inspired by the opening of [[Tutankhamun]]'s tomb in 1922 and the alleged "[[curse of the pharaohs]]",<ref>{{cite web |last=Miller|first=Frank|title=The Mummy (1932)|work=[[Turner Classic Movies]]| url=https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/84111/the-mummy/#articles-reviews?articleId=18613 |date=December 29, 2002|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221031190330/https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/84111/the-mummy/#articles-reviews?articleId=18613|archive-date=October 31, 2022|url-status=dead}}</ref> producer [[Carl Laemmle Jr.]] commissioned story editor [[Richard Schayer]] to find a novel to form a basis for an Egyptian-themed horror film, just as the novels ''[[Dracula]]'' and ''[[Frankenstein]]'' inspired their 1931 films ''[[Dracula (1931 English-language film)|Dracula]]'' and ''[[Frankenstein (1931 film)|Frankenstein]]''.{{sfn|Robson|2012|p=11}} Schayer found none,{{cn|date=June 2026}} although the plot bears a strong resemblance{{cn|date=June 2026}} to a short story by [[Arthur Conan Doyle]] entitled "[[Arthur Conan Doyle bibliography#Publications in periodicals|The Ring of Thoth]]".<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/index.php/The_Ring_of_Thoth | title=The Ring of Thoth | access-date=October 12, 2019}}</ref> Schayer and writer [[Nina Wilcox Putnam]] learned about [[Alessandro Cagliostro]] and wrote a nine-page treatment entitled ''Cagliostro''.{{sfn|Robson|2012|p=11}}  The story, set in San Francisco, was about a 4,000-year-old magician who survives by injecting [[nitrate]]s.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Weaver |first=Tom |title=Universal horrors: the studio's classic films, 1931-1946 |last2=Brunas |first2=Michael |last3=Brunas |first3=John |date=2011 |publisher=McFarland & Co |isbn=978-0-7864-2974-5 |edition=2nd |location=Jefferson, N.C |pages=64}}</ref>

Pleased with the Cagliostro concept, Laemmle hired [[John L. Balderston]] to write the script.{{sfn|Robson|2012|p=11}} Balderston had contributed to ''[[Dracula (1931 English-language film)|Dracula]]'' and ''[[Frankenstein (1931 film)|Frankenstein]]'', and had covered the opening of Tutankhamun's tomb for the ''[[New York World]]'' when he was a journalist so he was more than familiar with the well publicised tomb unearthing. Balderston moved the story to Egypt, renaming the film to ''The Mummy'' and its title character [[Imhotep]], after the historical architect.{{sfn|Vieira|2003|pages=55–58}}

He also changed the story from one of revenge upon all the women who resembled the main character's ex-lover to one where the main character is determined to revive his old love by killing and mummifying her reincarnated self before resurrecting her with the spell of the Scroll of Thoth.<ref name="ejas.revues.org">{{cite journal|title=THE MUMMY in context|first=Richard|last=Freeman|journal=European Journal of American Studies|volume=4|date=March 18, 2009|issue=1|doi=10.4000/ejas.7566|doi-access=free}}</ref> Balderston invented the Scroll of Thoth, which gave an aura of authenticity to the story. [[Thoth]] was the wisest of the Egyptian gods who, when [[Osiris]] died, helped [[Isis]] bring her love back from the dead. Thoth is believed to have authored ''[[Book of the Dead|The Book of the Dead]]'', which may have been the inspiration for Balderston's Scroll of Thoth. Another likely source of inspiration is the fictional ''[[Book of Thoth]]'' that appeared in several ancient Egyptian stories.{{sfn|Smith|2007|pages=20–21}}

===Filming===
[[Karl Freund]], the cinematographer on ''Dracula'', was hired to direct, making this his first film in the United States as a director.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/84111/the-mummy#articles-reviews |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121016150751/http://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/84111/The-Mummy/#articles-reviews |url-status=dead |archive-date=October 16, 2012 |title=Articles: Turner Classic Movies|accessdate=November 4, 2022}}</ref> Freund cast [[Zita Johann]] as 'Anck-es-en-Amon', named after the only wife of Pharaoh Tutankhamun [[Ankhesenamun]], whose body was missing from the pharaoh's tomb. 

Filming began in September 1932 and was scheduled for three weeks, with a budget of $196,000.{{sfn|Robson|2012|page=11-12}} Karloff's first day was spent shooting the Mummy's awakening from his [[sarcophagus]]. Make-up artist [[Jack Pierce (make-up artist)|Jack Pierce]] had studied photos of [[Seti I]]'s mummy to design Imhotep. Pierce began transforming Karloff at 11 a.m., applying [[cotton]], [[collodion]] and [[spirit gum]] to his face; [[clay]] to his hair; and wrapping him in [[linen]] bandages treated with acid and burnt in an oven, finishing the job at 7 p.m. Karloff finished his scenes at 2 a.m., and another two hours were spent removing the make-up. Karloff found the removal of gum from his face painful, and overall found the day "the most trying ordeal I [had] ever endured."{{sfn|Vieira|2003|pages=55–58}} Although the images of Karloff wrapped in bandages are the most iconic taken from the film, Karloff appears on screen in this make-up only for the opening vignette; the rest of the film sees him wearing less elaborate make-up.{{sfn|Mank|2009|pages=126–128}}

A lengthy and detailed flashback sequence was longer than now exists.{{sfn|Mank|2009|p=134}} This sequence showed the various forms Anck-es-en-Amon was reincarnated in over the centuries:{{sfn|Mank|2009|pages=126, 131, 133}} [[Henry Victor]] is credited in the film as "Saxon Warrior", despite his performance having been deleted.{{sfn|Mank|2009|p=133}} Stills exist of those sequences, but the footage (save for Karloff's appearance and the sacrilegious events leading up to his mummification in ancient Egypt) is lost.{{sfn|Mank|2009|p=134}}

===Music===
The piece of classical music heard during the opening credits, taken from the [[Tchaikovsky]] ballet ''[[Swan Lake]]'', was previously also used (in the same arrangement) for the opening credits of Universal's ''[[Dracula (1931 English-language film)|Dracula]]'' (1931) and ''[[Murders in the Rue Morgue (1932 film)|Murders in the Rue Morgue]]'' (1932); it would be re-used as the title music of the same studio's ''[[Secret of the Blue Room]]'' (1933).{{citation needed|date=July 2023}}

==Historical accuracy==
While the Scroll of Thoth is a fictional artifact, it was likely based on the ''[[Book of the Dead]]'', a real-world ancient Egyptian funerary text that was said to contain magic spells that would aid a soul in going to the afterlife. The eponymous [[Thoth]] was the Egyptian god of knowledge, and was said to be the inventor of hieroglyphs, which were thought to contain a sort of magic power. The film also makes reference to the Egyptian myth of the goddess [[Isis]] resurrecting [[Osiris]] after his murder by his brother [[Set (deity)|Set]].

Conspirators were caught in a plot to assassinate Pharaoh [[Ramesses III]] around 1151 B.C. [[Judicial Papyrus of Turin|Papyrus trial transcripts]] reveal that the conspirators were prescribed 'great punishments of death', and archaeological evidence led to the suggestion that at least one of them may have been buried alive. Although such punishment was not common practice, this discovery probably influenced the film's screenplay.{{sfn|Smith|2007|pages=20–21}}

There is no evidence to suggest that the ancient Egyptians believed in or considered the possibility of reanimated mummies. Mummification was a sacred process meant to prepare a dead body to carry the soul through the afterlife, not for being reincarnated and living again on Earth. While it is possible that some individuals were mummified by being buried alive, it is unlikely that ancient Egyptians would think resurrection was possible because they were very aware of the fact that all the necessary organs had been removed and the body would be of little use on Earth anymore.<ref name="ejas.revues.org"/> Egyptologist [[Stuart Tyson Smith]] notes that 'the idea of mobile mummies was not entirely alien to ancient Egypt', citing [[Khaemweset#Khaemweset in Ancient Egyptian fiction|one of the Hellenistic-era stories of Setna Khaemwas]], which features both the animated mummy of Naneferkaptah and a fictional ''[[Book of Thoth]]'' and may have inspired screenwriter John Balderston.{{sfn|Smith|2007|pages=20–21}}

==Reception==
===Box office===
''The Mummy'' was not as profitable in the United States as previously released Universal horror films such as ''[[Dracula (1931 English-language film)|Dracula]]'' and ''[[Frankenstein (1931 film)|Frankenstein]]''.{{sfn|Best|2023|p=75}} In January 1933, after its first three weeks in release, the film's ticket sales were noted as "dropping down markedly in some cities, but holding steady in others" despite it receiving favorable reviews.{{sfn|Best|2023|p=76}} The film was a major box-office hit in Australia.{{sfn|Best|2023|p=76}}

===Critical response===
====Contemporary====
The ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' was positive, although the film received varied critical reviews despite being a modest box office success.{{sfn|Robson|2012|page=13}} When the film opened at New York's RKO Mayfair theater, a reviewer for ''[[The New York Times]]'' was ultimately unimpressed:{{blockquote|For purposes of terror there are two scenes in ''The Mummy'' that are weird enough in all conscience. In the first the mummy comes alive and a young archaeologist, going quite mad, laughs in a way that raises the hair on the scalp. In the second Im-Ho-Tep is embalmed alive, and that moment when the tape is drawn across the man's mouth and nose, leaving only his wild eyes staring out of the coffin, is one of decided horror. But most of ''The Mummy'' is costume melodrama for the children.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1933/01/07/archives/life-after-3700-years.html|title=Life After 3,700 Years|work=[[The New York Times]]|date=January 7, 1933|archive-url=https://archive.today/20251005215942/https://www.nytimes.com/1933/01/07/archives/life-after-3700-years.html|archive-date=October 5, 2025|url-status=live}}</ref>}}

====Modern assessment====
Modern reviews have been much more positive overall. {{Rotten Tomatoes prose|90|7.9|48|consensus=Relying more on mood and atmosphere than the thrills typical of modern horror fare, Universal's ''The Mummy'' sets a masterful template for mummy-themed films to follow.|access-date=October 5, 2025|ref=y}}

[[James Lowder]] reviewed ''The Mummy'' in ''[[White Wolf Inphobia]]'' #53 (March, 1995), rating it a 4 out of 5 and stated that "Despite being a blatant reworking of Universal's earlier supernatural romance ''Dracula'', 1932's ''The Mummy'' manages to be an effective and chilling horror film. Two people deserve most of the credit for its success: Star Boris Karloff and Director Karl Freund."<ref name="WW53">{{Cite magazine |last=Lowder |first=James |authorlink=James Lowder |date=March 1995|title=Into the Dark |magazine= [[White Wolf Inphobia]] |number=53 |pages=56–57 |url=https://imgur.com/a/bHo2OkF}}</ref>

Cliff Aliperti at ''Immortal Ephemera'' opines: "The perfect explanation of ''The Mummy''{{'}}s quality comes from film historian [[William K. Everson]]'s closing comment in the brief Mummy section of his 1974 Classics of the Horror Film: 'If one accepts ''[[The Bride of Frankenstein]]'' for its theatre and ''[[The Body Snatcher (1945 film)|The Body Snatcher]]'' for its literacy, then one must regard ''The Mummy'' as the closest that Hollywood ever came to creating a poem out of horror'."<ref>{{cite web | url=https://immortalephemera.com/7971/boris-karloff-in-the-mummy-1932/ | title=Moments of Horror as Boris Karloff Stars as The Mummy (1932) | date=October 29, 2010 | work=Immortal Ephemera | access-date=January 29, 2023}}</ref>

''The Mummy'' has been criticised by some modern audiences for its "[[othering]]" attitude towards contemporary Egyptian and Eastern culture, with the film portraying it as being more primitive, superstitious and untrustworthy than Western culture - an example given by critic Caroline T. Schroeder being a scene where Helen Grosvenor longs for the "real" ([[Ancient Egypt|Classical]]) Egypt, disparaging contemporary [[Islam in Egypt|Islamic Egypt]].<ref>Schroeder, Carolyn T. ''Ancient Egyptian Religion on the Silver Screen: Modern Anxieties about Race, Ethnicity, and Religion''. University of Nebraska Omaha. Oct 2003. January 20, 2015.</ref>

According to Mark A. Hall of the Perth Museum and Art Gallery, film portrayals of Egypt, especially in Egyptian archaeology, often deal with themes of appropriating and controlling the dangers of non-European cultures, or deal with the past if it relates to legend and superstition. Mummies, Hall says, are a common example of this. While he commended the archaeological wisdom espoused by Sir Joseph Whemple in the film, he writes that "much more is learned from studying bits of broken pottery than from all the sensational finds" and that archaeologists' job is to "increase the sum of human knowledge of the past", and mentions that the archaeological element was only used as a [[Foil (literature)|foil]] for the supernatural elements.<ref name="Hall, Mark A 2004">Hall, Mark A. "Romancing the stones: archaeology in popular cinema". ''European Journal of Archaeology'' 7.2 (2004): p. 161.</ref> As a result, per Hall, what it and similar films offered was a "depiction of archaeology as a colonial imposition by which cultural inheritance is appropriated".<ref name="Hall, Mark A 2004"/>

==Related works==
===Sequels===
[[File:MummysTomb001Crop.png|thumb|286px|[[Lon Chaney Jr.]] appeared as [[Kharis|Kharis the Mummy]] in three follow-ups to the original film.]] 
Unlike other [[Universal Monsters]] films, ''The Mummy'' had no official sequel. The character was re-imagined in ''[[The Mummy's Hand]]'' (1940) and its sequels; ''[[The Mummy's Tomb]]'' (1942), ''[[The Mummy's Ghost]]'' (1944), ''[[The Mummy's Curse]]'' (1944), and the studio's [[comedy horror|comedy–horror]] [[crossover (fiction)|crossover]] film ''[[Abbott and Costello Meet the Mummy]]'' (1955). These films focus on the titular character named [[Kharis]] (Klaris in the Abbott and Costello film). ''The Mummy's Hand'' recycled footage from the original film for use in the telling of Kharis's origins, where Karloff is clearly visible in several of these recycled scenes but was not credited. [[Tom Tyler]] played the Mummy in ''The Mummy's Hand'', and [[Lon Chaney Jr.]] played the Mummy in ''The Mummy's Tomb'', ''The Mummy's Ghost'', and ''The Mummy's Curse''.

===Hammer Film Productions series===
In the late 1950s, British [[Hammer Film Productions]] took up the ''Mummy'' theme, beginning with ''[[The Mummy (1959 film)|The Mummy]]'' (1959), which, rather than being a [[remake]] of the 1932 Karloff film, is based on Universal's ''The Mummy's Hand'' (1940) and ''The Mummy's Tomb'' (1942). Hammer's follow-ups — ''[[The Curse of the Mummy's Tomb]]'' (1964), ''[[The Mummy's Shroud]]'' (1966) and ''[[Blood from the Mummy's Tomb]]'' (1971) — are unrelated to the first film or even to each other, apart from the appearance of ''The Scroll of Life'' in ''Curse of the Mummy's Tomb'' and ''Blood from the Mummy's Tomb''.

===Remake series===
{{Main|The Mummy (franchise)#Stephen Sommers series (1999–2018)}}
The much later Universal film ''[[The Mummy (1999 film)|The Mummy]]'' (1999) also suggests that it is a remake of the 1932 film, but has a different storyline and is more similar to The Mummy's Hand than the original. In common with most postmodern remakes of classic horror and science-fiction films, it may be considered as such in that it is produced-distributed by the same studio, its titular character is again named Imhotep, resurrected by the Book of the Dead, and out to find the present-day embodiment of the soul of his beloved Anck-su-namun, and features an Egyptian [[Medjay]] named Ardeth Bay (in this case, a guard of the city and of Imhotep's tomb). This film spawned two sequels with ''[[The Mummy Returns]]'' (2001) and ''[[The Mummy: Tomb of the Dragon Emperor]]'' (2008). ''The Mummy Returns'' also spawned a prequel spin-off of that sequel, ''[[The Scorpion King]]'' (2002), which in turn spawned a prequel, ''[[The Scorpion King 2: Rise of a Warrior]]'' (2008), and three sequels, ''[[The Scorpion King 3: Battle for Redemption]]'' (2012), ''[[The Scorpion King 4: Quest for Power]]'' (2015) and ''[[Scorpion King: Book of Souls]]'' (2018). Also, a short-lived animated series, simply titled ''[[The Mummy (TV series)|The Mummy]]'', ran from 2001 to 2003.

===Reboot===
In 2012, Universal announced a reboot of the film, more in-line with the horror films, from the [[Universal Monsters]] film franchise.<ref>Kroll, Justin, Snieder, Jeff, [https://variety.com/2012/film/news/u-sets-mummy-reboot-with-spaihts-1118052291/ "U sets 'Mummy' reboot with Spaihts"], Variety.com, Published 2012-04-04, Retrieved May 4, 2012.</ref> The reboot was directed by [[Alex Kurtzman]]. ''[[The Mummy (2017 film)|The Mummy]]'' was planned as the first film in a series of interconnected monster films, as Universal has planned to build a shared universe film series out of its vault of classic monster movies.<ref>{{Cite web|url = https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/heat-vision/new-mummy-universals-monster-universe-831774|title=New Mummy in Universal's Monster Universe Might Be Female |date=October 14, 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250520000545/https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/new-mummy-universals-monster-universe-831774/|archive-date=May 20, 2025|url-status=live|first1=Rebecca|last1=Ford|website=[[The Hollywood Reporter]]}}</ref> [[Tom Cruise]] stars in the film.<ref>{{Cite web|url = https://variety.com/2016/film/news/tom-cruise-mummy-universal-release-date-1201685122/|title = Tom Cruise's 'The Mummy' Gets New Release Date|date = January 21, 2016 |access-date = January 21, 2016 |website = Variety |last = Kroll |first = Justin}}</ref> [[Sofia Boutella]] portrays [[Amunet#Film|Princess Ahmunet / The Mummy]] for the film, while [[Russell Crowe]] appears as [[Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (character)|Dr. Henry Jekyll / Mr. Hyde]], two roles which had previously been portrayed by [[Boris Karloff]] in the studios' previous film franchise. It received negative reviews and is considered a [[box-office bomb|box office bomb]], scrapping the plans for the upcoming films of the Dark Universe.

Another [[Lee Cronin's The Mummy|reimagining of the film]], directed by [[Lee Cronin (director)|Lee Cronin]], was released theatrically in 2026.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Gubecka |first=Zanda |date=2026-01-09 |title='Lee Cronin's The Mummy' Synopsis & Plot Details Revealed (Exclusive) |url=https://featurefirst.net/lee-cronins-the-mummy-synopsis-plot-details-revealed-exclusive/ |access-date=2026-04-01 |website=Feature First |language=en-US}}</ref>
