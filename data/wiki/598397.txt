'''''Shaun of the Dead''''' is a 2004 British<ref name="THR">{{Cite web |last1=Bennett |first1=Ray |date=24 September 2024 |title='Shaun of the Dead': THR's 2004 review |url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/shaun-of-the-dead-review-movie-simon-pegg-nick-frost-1236010657/ |website=[[The Hollywood Reporter]] |access-date=4 December 2025}}</ref> [[zombie comedy]] film directed by [[Edgar Wright]] and written by Wright and [[Simon Pegg]]. Pegg stars as Shaun, a downtrodden [[London]] salesman who is caught alongside his loved ones in a [[zombie apocalypse]]. It also stars [[Nick Frost]] in his film debut, [[Kate Ashfield]], [[Lucy Davis]], [[Dylan Moran]], [[Bill Nighy]], and [[Penelope Wilton]]. It is the first film in Wright and Pegg's ''[[Three Flavours Cornetto]]'' trilogy, followed by ''[[Hot Fuzz]]'' (2007) and ''[[The World's End (film)|The World's End]]'' (2013).

''Shaun of the Dead'' was inspired by ideas Pegg and Wright used for their 1999–2001 television sitcom ''[[Spaced]]'', particularly an episode in which Pegg's character hallucinates a zombie invasion. The film references the [[Night of the Living Dead (film series)|''Night of the Living Dead'' films]] by [[George A. Romero]]. [[Principal photography]] took place across London and at [[Ealing Studios]] for nine weeks between May and June 2003.

''Shaun of the Dead'' premiered in London on 29 March 2004, before it was theatrically released in the United Kingdom on 9 April 2004 and in the United States on 24 September. It was met with critical acclaim and commercial success, grossing $38.7 million worldwide on a budget of $6.1 million and receiving three nominations at the [[British Academy Film Awards]]. It was ranked third on the [[Channel 4]] list of the [[100 Greatest (TV series)|50 Greatest Comedy Films]] and quickly acquired a [[cult following]]. In [[film studies]], it is seen as a product of post-[[September 11 attacks|9/11]] anxiety, and portrait of post-Thatcher Britain as well as a model for transnational comedy, while the zombie outbreak as depicted in the film has been used as a modelling example for [[Preventive healthcare|disease control]].

== Plot ==
<!--Please keep plot summary between 400-700 words per WP:FILMPLOT-->
In the [[Crouch End]] area of London, 29-year-old sales advisor Shaun is disrespected by his colleagues, does not get along with his stepfather Philip, and is dumped by his girlfriend Liz after he promises to improve himself but is unsuccessful in making decent plans for an anniversary date. At his favourite pub, the Winchester, a heartbroken Shaun gets drunk with his [[slacker]] best friend Ed. At home, Shaun and Ed's roommate Pete complains of a bite wound from a mugger and berates Shaun, telling him to stop leaning on Ed and to get his life together.

The following day, a [[zombie apocalypse]] has overwhelmed London. Shaun and Ed do not notice until they encounter two zombies in their garden, whom they beat to death after throwing household implements and Shaun's vinyl collection at them. They devise a plan to rescue Shaun's mother, Barbara, and Liz, and then wait out the crisis at The Winchester. They escape from a now-zombified Pete by stealing his car. They pick up Philip, who has been bitten, and Barbara. Ed deliberately crashes Pete's car so he can drive Philip's [[Jaguar XJ#Series 3 (1979–1992)|Jaguar Series 3]]. They fetch Liz and her flatmates David and Dianne. Philip reconciles with Shaun before becoming a zombie.

Forced to abandon the car and their weapons, they sneak through the neighbourhood and briefly encounter a group led by Shaun's friend Yvonne, with each group member resembling a [[doppelgänger]] of the other group's members. Shaun defends Barbara from a zombie and impales it with a [[Totem tennis|swingball]] pole. After imitating zombies to bypass a horde and reach The Winchester, Shaun and Ed argue when Ed wastes time on his phone, attracting the zombies' attention. David panics and breaks a window of The Winchester, forcing Shaun to lure the horde away using himself as bait before returning.

As the group takes refuge inside The Winchester, Shaun discovers that the zombies followed him, and Ed inadvertently attracts them by playing on the pub's [[slot machine]]. While fighting the pub's zombified owner, Shaun discovers that the [[Winchester rifle]] above the bar is functional, which he had previously debated with Ed. Barbara reveals she was bitten and dies after giving Liz and Shaun's relationship her approval. David attempts to shoot Barbara, but Shaun stops him, accusing David of hating him and being in love with Liz. David denies this, but Dianne admits she knows it is true. Barbara becomes a zombie, forcing a distraught Shaun to shoot her, and further fighting with David ensues.

The zombies break through a window of the pub and pull David out, devouring him alive. In a foolish and senseless attempt to save the dead David, Dianne opens the doors to the pub and rushes into the horde of zombies using David's severed leg as a weapon. With the doors now open, zombies pour into the bar. The zombified Pete reappears in the horde and is shot by Shaun after biting Ed. Shaun, Liz, and Ed take cover behind the bar; Shaun sets the bar on fire with alcohol to serve as a barrier. The three take refuge in the cellar, where they realise they only have two bullets left. Shaun and Liz contemplate suicide while Ed volunteers to sacrifice himself. Upon discovering a small lift for kegs that opens onto the street, Shaun and Liz exit the pub while Ed buys them time by staying behind with the rifle. Yvonne arrives with the [[British Army]], who gun down the zombies before taking Shaun and Liz to safety.

Six months later, normality has returned but the surviving zombies are now used for free unskilled labour and entertainment. Liz has moved in with Shaun, who keeps the now-zombified Ed chained up in his garden shed. Shaun plays video games in the shed with the somewhat-sentient Ed.

''Shaun of the Dead'' was inspired by "[[List of Spaced episodes|Art]]", an episode of [[Simon Pegg]] and [[Edgar Wright]]'s sitcom ''[[Spaced]]''. Written by Pegg and [[Jessica Hynes]] and directed by Wright, the episode involves Pegg's character hallucinating a zombie invasion after playing the video game ''[[Resident Evil 2]]'' while under the influence of [[amphetamine]].<ref name=mentalfloss>{{Cite web|date=2016-01-23|title=12 Killer Facts About Shaun of the Dead|url=https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/74230/12-killer-facts-about-shaun-dead|access-date=2020-06-24|website=Mental Floss|archive-date=27 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200627034740/https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/74230/12-killer-facts-about-shaun-dead|url-status=live}}</ref>

With a mutual appreciation for [[George A. Romero]]'s ''Dead'' trilogy of zombie films, Pegg and Wright decided to write their own zombie film.<ref name="guardian2020" /> In late 1999, they conceived and pitched the film to [[Film4]],<ref>{{cite web |title=Spaced Out - Other Projects |url=http://www.spaced-out.org.uk/projects/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200127185944/http://www.spaced-out.org.uk/projects/ |archive-date=27 January 2020 |access-date=2020-06-25 |website=www.spaced-out.org.uk}}</ref> who took it on until their production budget was cut back. Despite this, Wright was still invested in the production and refused to take other television directing jobs until ''Shaun of the Dead'' was made, which left him in some debt for a while.<ref name="mentalfloss" /> According to Wright, other companies passed on it because "they weren't sure what the tone was and said it wasn't all that scary and not that funny. They didn't get it."<ref name="dread1">{{cite web|date=2004-10-11|title=Pegg, Simon & Wright, Edgar (Shaun of the Dead)|url=https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/3268/pegg-simon-wright-edgar-shaun-of-the-dead/|access-date=2020-06-24|website=Dread Central|archive-date=26 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200626222045/https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/3268/pegg-simon-wright-edgar-shaun-of-the-dead/|url-status=live}}</ref> After eighteen months, [[Working Title Films]] picked the project up, which Wright felt was ironic as the film mocks the classic British [[Romantic comedy|rom-coms]] that Working Title produces.<ref name=":9">{{cite web|date=2004-08-12|title='Shaun of the Dead' Creators Simon Pegg & Edgar Wright Swing Their Cricket Bat at B. Alan Orange [Exclusive]|url=https://movieweb.com/exclusive-shaun-of-the-dead-creators-simon-pegg-edgar-wright-swing-their-cricket-bat-at-b-alan-orange/|access-date=2020-06-25|website=Movieweb|archive-date=28 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200628074741/https://movieweb.com/exclusive-shaun-of-the-dead-creators-simon-pegg-edgar-wright-swing-their-cricket-bat-at-b-alan-orange/|url-status=live}}</ref> The film was first announced at the [[2002 Cannes Film Festival]].<ref>{{cite news|last=Jeffries|first=Stuart|date=2002-05-18|title=Cannes diary|work=The Guardian|url=https://www.theguardian.com/world/2002/may/18/uk.cannes20021|access-date=2020-06-25|issn=0261-3077|archive-date=27 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200627214705/https://www.theguardian.com/world/2002/may/18/uk.cannes20021|url-status=live}}</ref>

Wright has said that he came up with the film while riding in a cab with Pegg after the excitement of filming the zombie episode in ''Spaced'',<ref name="mentalfloss" /> and while watching a [[horror film]] with both Pegg and [[Nick Frost]].<ref name="guardian2020" /> He began developing it in earnest after playing ''[[Resident Evil (1996 video game)|Resident Evil]]'' one late night himself and then going out in the early morning hours, wondering what a British person's reaction to a zombie apocalypse would be. He considered the lack of firearms typical of American zombie movies, and his experience of the dazed early morning walk to the shop turned into a scene in the film where Shaun does the same thing.<ref name="guardian2020" /> Another influence from Wright's life came from how he missed the [[2001 United Kingdom foot-and-mouth outbreak|2001 foot-and-mouth epidemic]] by simply not having paid attention to the news for a fortnight and turning his television on one day to see cattle being burnt, which left him confused. Due to this, Wright said "it's plausible that the world could be ending and these two guys could be the last to know" as they also skip over the news on television in the film.<ref name=":8" /> The idea of Shaun and Ed spending their time at the Winchester was also inspired by their actors' lives, as Pegg and Frost "always used to go to the same bar all the time" according to Wright, who had been trying to convince them not to.<ref name=":8" />

===Casting===
The film's cast features several British comedians, comedy actors and sitcom stars, most prominently from ''Spaced'', ''[[Black Books]]'' and ''[[The Office (British TV series)|The Office]]'', and co-stars other actors from the same shows.<ref name=mentalfloss/> Cast members from them include Pegg, Frost, Stevenson, and [[Peter Serafinowicz]].<ref name="guardian">{{cite web|title=Simon Pegg and Edgar Wright|url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2007/feb/12/guardianinterviewsatbfisouthbank|last=Solomons|first=Jason|date=12 February 2007|work=Guardian|access-date=13 February 2008|archive-date=28 February 2008|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080228044310/http://film.guardian.co.uk/interview/interviewpages/0,,2013834,00.html|url-status=live}}</ref> Frost met Pegg when he was working as a waiter and was brought aboard ''Spaced'' despite a lack of acting experience;<ref name="ugo-frost">{{cite web|title=Nick Frost of Shaun of the Dead (Rogue Pictures) Interview|url=http://www.ugo.com/channels/filmTv/features/shaunofthedead/nickfrost.asp|last=Epstein|first=Daniel Robert|website=Under Ground Online|access-date=13 February 2008 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080214041644/http://www.ugo.com/channels/filmTv/features/shaunofthedead/nickfrost.asp |archive-date=14 February 2008}}</ref> Frost explained that Shaun and Ed have a dynamic similar to that of Pegg and himself in real life, as they had been living together for years.<ref name="echo">{{cite web|title=Interview with film's stars|url=http://www.easternecho.com/cgi-bin/story.cgi?7034|last=Kargol|first=Dave|date=24 September 2004|website=Echo Online|access-date=31 January 2008 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20061023134712/http://www.easternecho.com/cgi-bin/story.cgi?7034|archive-date=23 October 2006}}</ref>

[[Kate Winslet]] was approached to play Shaun's girlfriend, and met with Wright and Pegg to discuss the role, but she turned it down. Wright later confessed that he was relieved, saying "She's brilliant, but it was obvious that her fame would capsize the film in maybe a not useful way."<ref>{{cite book |last1=Collis |first1=Clark |title=You've Got Red On You |date=2021 |isbn=9781948221191 |page=132}}</ref> The filmmakers originally approached [[Helen Mirren]] to play Shaun's mother, Barbara, which she turned down with a note that she would rather play other funnier characters.<ref name=guardian2020/> The role of Barbara ultimately went to [[Penelope Wilton]], who was asked to take on the role because of her work in the 1984-89 sitcom ''[[Ever Decreasing Circles]]''.<ref name="radiofree"/> Shaun's stepfather Philip is played by [[Bill Nighy]], who accepted the role after Wright sent him an early script of the film to read.<ref name="ign">{{cite web|title=IGN Interviews Simon Pegg, Nick Frost & Edgar Wright|url=http://movies.ign.com/articles/550/550143p1.html|last=Head|first=Steve|website=IGN|date=21 September 2004|access-date=13 February 2008|archive-date=9 November 2007|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071109155651/http://movies.ign.com/articles/550/550143p1.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Filming===
[[File:Duke of Albany.jpg|thumb|The Duke of Albany pub, used for exterior shots of the Winchester (image taken in 2008, immediately prior to conversion to flats).]]
The film was shot over nine weeks in London, both [[Location shooting|on location]] and at [[Ealing Studios]], between May and July 2003.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.workingtitlefilms.com/news/view/post/17 |title=SHAUN OF THE DEAD wraps |access-date=26 April 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20111104032934/http://workingtitlefilms.com/news/view/post/17|archive-date=4 November 2011|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.eatmybrains.com/showfeature.php?id=14 |title=EAT MY BRAINS! - Shaun of the Dead on-set photos – Feature Article |access-date=26 April 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130812195449/http://www.eatmybrains.com/showfeature.php?id=14|archive-date=12 August 2013|url-status=live}}</ref> Wright uses in-camera transitions, typical to his style, to enable powerful [[Visual narrative|visual storytelling]].<ref>{{cite web|last=Viewinder|date=2004-04-09|title=Film School: Shaun of the Dead: 'The Plan' Montage|url=https://viewinder.com/shaun-of-the-dead-the-plan-montage/|access-date=2020-06-25|website=viewinder|archive-date=25 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200625092338/https://viewinder.com/shaun-of-the-dead-the-plan-montage/|url-status=live}}</ref> Pegg also commented on the use of a [[magical realism]] style, which he says is part of Wright's direction.<ref name=":9" />

Many exterior shots were filmed in and around the North London areas of [[Crouch End]] (where the film is set), [[Highgate]], [[Finsbury Park (area)|Finsbury Park]] and [[East Finchley]].<ref>{{Cite web|title=Filming Locations for Shaun Of The Dead (2004), around London.|url=http://www.movie-locations.com/movies/s/Shaun-Of-The-Dead.php|access-date=2020-06-25|website=The Worldwide Guide to Movie Locations|archive-date=20 January 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200120133613/http://www.movie-locations.com/movies/s/Shaun-Of-The-Dead.php|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|title=Movie Locations Guide: Maps and Directions to Filming Locations|url=http://www.movielocationsguide.com/shaun-of-the-dead/filming-locations|access-date=2020-06-25|website=www.movielocationsguide.com|archive-date=29 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200629065143/http://www.movielocationsguide.com/shaun-of-the-dead/filming-locations|url-status=live}}</ref> The electrical appliance shop that Shaun works at is a real shop located in [[North Finchley]].<ref>{{cite news |title=The London filming locations you might not have known about |url=https://www.telegraph.co.uk/travel/destinations/europe/united-kingdom/england/london/articles/london-filming-locations/ |access-date=23 August 2019 |work=The Telegraph|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190823122907/https://www.telegraph.co.uk/travel/destinations/europe/united-kingdom/england/london/articles/london-filming-locations/|archive-date=23 August 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> The scenes filmed in and around the Winchester pub were shot at the Duke of Albany pub in [[New Cross]], South London. ===Music===
The [[film score]] by Pete Woodhead and Daniel Mudford is a [[pastiche]] of Italian zombie film soundtracks by [[Goblin (band)|Goblin]] and [[Fabio Frizzi]]. It also uses many musical cues from the original ''Dawn of the Dead'' that were originally taken by George A. Romero from the [[De Wolfe Music|De Wolfe]] [[production music]] library.<ref>Edgar Wright and Simon Pegg commentary, Shaun of the Dead (DVD). Universal Pictures</ref> A friend of the assistant editor on the film had been compiling music library tracks from zombie films, making finding some music for the film much easier.<ref name=":9" /> Before production began on the film, Wright and Pegg had created a mixtape of songs they wanted to use.<ref name=":8" /> The Goblin music, though, was used as a [[temp track]] by Wright in editing; he liked the feel of it so much they decided to get the clearance to use it.<ref name=":9" />

Bobby Olivier of ''[[Billboard (magazine)|Billboard]]'' attributes the initial rebirth of [[Queen (band)|Queen]]'s "[[Don't Stop Me Now]]" to its appearance in the film, which "introduced it to a new generation of listeners", saying: "Perhaps the most famous scene from ''Shaun of the Dead'' features "Don't Stop Me Now" which blares from a pub jukebox while stars Simon Pegg, Nick Frost, and Kate Ashfield bash a zombie with pool cues to the song's hurtling beat".<ref name="Billboard 2019">{{cite news|title=The Evolution of Queen's 'Don't Stop Me Now': How a Minor Hit Became One of The Band's Most Beloved (And Inescapable) Songs|magazine=Billboard|url=https://www.billboard.com/music/rock/queen-dont-stop-me-now-biggest-hits-8501348/|url-status=live|access-date=22 August 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190528073855/https://www.billboard.com/articles/columns/rock/8501348/queen-dont-stop-me-now-biggest-hits|archive-date=28 May 2019}}</ref> The moment had been Wright's idea, as he loves Queen and "had the idea of playing Don't Stop Me Now – one of the most positive, exciting, happy tunes ever – over a scene of extreme violence".<ref name=guardian2020/> Pegg explained that the fight in the pub was choreographed to the song even before it had been cleared to be used in the film, so they wrote to [[Brian May]] and begged to use it.<ref name=guardian2020/>

The other choreographed sequence, near the start of the film, used different music than that which it had been set to. The original was a [[Cornelius (musician)|Cornelius]] song, and had been the track written in for the scene from the screenplay. Wright then heard the song used in the film, "[[Neveroddoreven|The Blue Wrath]]" by [[I Monster]], when editing and felt that it worked better. The [[tempo]] of both songs is the same, so the new song fitted the original choreography.<ref name=":9" />

== Release ==
The film was distributed by Universal Pictures—through [[United International Pictures]] (UIP)— in the United Kingdom (UK) and [[Rogue Pictures]]—through [[Focus Features]]— in the United States and Canada, as well as other international markets.<ref name=":4" /> However, Universal also distributed [[Zack Snyder]]'s [[Dawn of the Dead (2004 film)|remake]] of ''[[Dawn of the Dead (1978 film)|Dawn of the Dead]]'' at that time, and only took on ''Shaun of the Dead'' after setting the condition that they would market and release it two weeks after the release of Snyder's film.<ref name=":4">{{Cite web|last=Elley|first=Derek|date=2004-03-11|title=Shaun of the Dead|url=https://variety.com/2004/film/reviews/shaun-of-the-dead-1200534580/|access-date=2020-06-24|website=Variety|archive-date=2 July 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190702144626/https://variety.com/2004/film/reviews/shaun-of-the-dead-1200534580/|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Marketing ===
UIP created a heavy targeted marketing strategy in the UK, including hiring actors to play zombies and dropping them around London to create disruption shortly before the film's release. ZenithOptimedia ran this part of the campaign; their head said that "It has to be the most powerful way to communicate what ''Shaun of the Dead'' is all about".<ref name=":5">{{Cite web|date=6 May 2004|title=Campaign Casebook: Shaun of the Dead|url=https://www.campaignlive.co.uk/article/campaign-casebook-shaun-dead/210038|url-status=live|access-date=24 June 2020|website=Campaign Live|archive-date=25 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200625074218/https://www.campaignlive.co.uk/article/campaign-casebook-shaun-dead/210038}}</ref>

Beyond traditional print advertising, posters were also placed in the [[London Underground]]. As the poster depicted Shaun crammed tightly against train windows surrounded by zombies, the company chose to buy up poster spaces that "would give the impression to anyone walking through the tunnels between platforms that the zombie carriages were on the tracks"; this is a tactic that UIP had not done before, but the creative angle of the posters' positioning naturally lent itself to such a move.<ref name=":5" /> A similar tactic was used with digital posters for the film at an international football match between [[England national football team|England]] and [[Sweden men's national football team|Sweden]]; less-restrictive advertising laws in Sweden, where the game was held, meant that UIP had "hoardings incorporating flailing zombie-like arms", which would not have been permitted in England.<ref name=":5" />

=== Comic adaptations and other media ===
{{Main|Shaun of the Dead (comics)}}

Pegg and Wright scripted a one-off tie-in comic strip for the British comic magazine ''[[2000 AD (comics)|2000 AD]]'' titled "[[Shaun of the Dead (comics)#2000 AD|There's Something About Mary]]". Set the day before the zombie outbreak, the strip follows and expands on the character of Mary, who appears briefly in the introductory credits and is the first zombie whom Shaun and Ed are aware of; the strip details how she became a zombie. The strip was made available on the DVD release of ''Shaun'', along with two other strips that wrapped up plotholes in the film, like how Dianne escaped and survived the Winchester incident and Ed's fate after taking refuge in the pub's basement.<ref>Shaun of the Dead: "There's Something About Mary" (by Simon Pegg, Edgar Wright, and Frazer Irving, in 2000 AD #1384, 2004</ref> The comics, which feature Pegg and Wright's voices on the DVD and are in [[black and white]], were drawn by Oscar Wright, a graphic artist and Edgar Wright's brother.<ref name=":9" /> In 2005, [[IDW Publishing]] released a four-issue adaptation written by Chris Ryall (with input from Edgar Wright & Simon Pegg) and drawn by Zack Howard. ==Analysis==
Film scholar Kyle Bishop, Literature scholar and leading zombie film researcher [[Peter Dendle]], and Sci-Fi scholar Gerry Canavan all comment on ''Shaun of the Dead'' as part of a large body of zombie narratives produced in the wake of [[September 11 attacks|9/11]].<ref name="Bishop">{{cite journal|last=Bishop|first=Kyle|date=April 2009|title=Dead Man Still Walking|journal=[[Journal of Popular Film & Television]]|volume=37|issue=1|pages=16–25|doi=10.3200/JPFT.37.1.16-25|s2cid=191452605|issn=0195-6051}}</ref><ref name="Dendle">{{cite book|last=Dendle|first=Peter|chapter=The Zombie as Barometer of Cultural Anxiety|title=Monsters and the Monstrous|year=2007|chapter-url=http://www.pilotprojectspilotprojects.com/Dendle_Barometer.pdf|pages=45–557|editor-last=Scott|editor-first=Niall|publisher=Brill|doi=10.1163/9789401204811_005|isbn=978-94-012-0481-1|access-date=2020-06-25|archive-date=6 January 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180106221131/http://pilotprojectspilotprojects.com/Dendle_Barometer.pdf|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="Canavan">{{cite journal|last=Canavan|first=Gerry|date=January 2010|title="We Are the Walking Dead": Race, Time, and Survival in Zombie Narrative|url=https://online.liverpooluniversitypress.co.uk/doi/pdf/10.3828/extr.2010.51.3.7|journal=Extrapolation|volume=51|issue=3|pages=431–453|doi=10.3828/extr.2010.51.3.7|issn=0014-5483|url-access=subscription}}{{Dead link|date=August 2023 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref> Bishop explained that the "renaissance of the subgenre reveals a connection between zombie cinema and post-9/11 cultural consciousness", because "horror films function as barometers of society's anxieties, and zombie movies represent the inescapable realities of unnatural death while presenting a grim view of the modern apocalypse".<ref name="Bishop" /> He finds that the subgenre of zombie films "can shock and terrify a population that has become numb to other horror subgenres",<ref name="Bishop" /> with Dendle similarly assessing that "the possibility of wide-scale destruction and devastation which 9/11 brought once again into the communal consciousness found a ready narrative expression in the zombie apocalypses which over thirty years had honed images of desperation subsistence and amoral survivalism to a fine edge".<ref name="Dendle" />

Dirk Eitzen also examined the film in depth as an example of how comedy is made in film, particularly how interpretive humour and satire are used. For instance, when Shaun slips and falls in the shop in the opening sequence, per Eitzen's explanation, it is funny on several levels. Falling is funny; not noticing the zombie apocalypse is funny; the social satire that contrasts Shaun's mindless behaviour with the mindless zombies is funny; and the self-reference to where Shaun had slipped on a curb earlier in the sequence is funny, too.<ref name="Eitzen">{{cite journal|last=Eitzen|first=Dirk|date=2012-01-01|title=The Nature of Film Comedy, or Why Is Shaun of the Dead Funny?|url=https://www.berghahnjournals.com/view/journals/projections/6/2/proj060202.xml|journal=Projections|volume=6|issue=2|pages=1–17|doi=10.3167/proj.2012.060202|issn=1934-9688|access-date=25 June 2020|archive-date=10 July 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200710065527/https://www.berghahnjournals.com/view/journals/projections/6/2/proj060202.xml|url-status=live|url-access=subscription}}</ref> Media scholar Lindsey Decker wrote on how the film created comedy through transnational generic hybridisation, taking cues from American zombie films as well as "British comedic practices from WWII-era [[Ealing comedies]], television two-man comedy teams and the [[Monty Python]] sketch troupe".<ref name="Decker">{{cite journal|last=Decker|first=Lindsey|date=2016-01-02|title=British cinema is undead: American horror, British comedy and generic hybridity in Shaun of the Dead|url=http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20403526.2015.1078120|journal=Transnational Cinemas|volume=7|issue=1|pages=67–81|doi=10.1080/20403526.2015.1078120|s2cid=192877657|issn=2040-3526|url-access=subscription}}</ref> In this use of British comedy within an American genre, it also serves as commentary on British-American relations in the film industry.<ref name="Decker" />

Multiple chapters of the 2016 book ''The Laughing Dead: The Horror-Comedy Film from Bride of Frankenstein to Zombieland'' are devoted to analysing aspects of ''Shaun of the Dead''.<ref>{{cite book|title=The Laughing Dead: The Horror-Comedy Film from Bride of Frankenstein to Zombieland|year=2016|editor-last1=Miller|editor-first1=Cynthia J.|editor-last2=Van Riper|editor-first2=A. Bowdoin|isbn=978-1-4422-6832-6|location=Lanham|oclc=936205548}}</ref> Steven Webley's chapter looks at the use of the [[Uncanny]] in the film,<ref name="Webley">{{cite book|last=Webley|first=Steven|chapter="The Limeys are Coming, Barbara, and They're Laughing!" The Art of the Romeroesque in Shaun of the Dead and Dead Set|chapter-url=https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/c3f4/c1d66857ac88331d2c9c5e2ff755ad6cd537.pdf|title=The Laughing Dead: The Horror-Comedy Film from Bride of Frankenstein to Zombieland|s2cid=193926637|editor-last1=Miller|editor-first1=Cynthia J.|editor-last2=Van Riper|editor-first2=A. Bowdoin|access-date=25 June 2020|archive-date=28 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200628091241/https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/c3f4/c1d66857ac88331d2c9c5e2ff755ad6cd537.pdf|url-status=dead}}</ref> while Shelley S. Rees' chapter discusses the film's [[Marxism|Marxist]] implications and the transgressive nature of zombies and zombie films in terms of relationships and sexuality.<ref name="Rees">{{cite book|last=Rees|first=Shelley S.|chapter=The Queer and the Dead: Transgressive Sexuality in Shaun of the Dead|chapter-url=https://www.academia.edu/27477341|title=The Laughing Dead: The Horror-Comedy Film from Bride of Frankenstein to Zombieland|editor-last1=Miller|editor-first1=Cynthia J.|editor-last2=Van Riper|editor-first2=A. Bowdoin|access-date=25 June 2020|archive-date=28 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200628121416/https://www.academia.edu/27477341/The_Queer_and_the_Dead_Transgressive_Sexuality_in_Shaun_of_the_Dead|url-status=live}}</ref> Comparatively, Kathryn A. Cady and Thomas Oates write in their article 'Family Splatters: Rescuing Heteronormativity from the Zombie Apocalypse' that the film "imagines a single-generation heteronormative family as the outcome of zombie invasion".<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Cady|first1=Kathryn A.|last2=Oates|first2=Thomas|date=2016-07-02|title=Family Splatters: Rescuing Heteronormativity from the Zombie Apocalypse|journal=[[Women's Studies in Communication]]|volume=39|issue=3|pages=308–325|doi=10.1080/07491409.2016.1194935|s2cid=148168573|issn=0749-1409}}</ref>

Beyond film studies, a [[Bayesian statistics|Bayesian]] mathematical model using [[Markov chain Monte Carlo]] methods was performed on examples of epidemic progression by Caitlyn Witkowski and Brian Blais in 2013. As an example of how such modelling could be applied to infectious disease control, Witkowski and Blais took examples of zombie apocalypses in Romero's original ''Dead'' trilogy and ''Shaun of the Dead'' to demonstrate disease dynamics.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Witkowski|first1=Caitlyn|last2=Blais|first2=Brian|date=2013-11-27|title=Bayesian Analysis of Epidemics - Zombies, Influenza, and other Diseases|arxiv=1311.6376}}</ref>

==Reception==
=== Box office ===
In the UK (all figures in [[United States dollar|US$]]), ''Shaun of the Dead'' grossed $3.0{{nbsp}}million at the box office in its opening weekend, being shown in 367 cinemas and starting a run of five consecutive weekends in the UK top 10.<ref name="BOM">{{Cite Box Office Mojo|access-date=2023-09-08}}</ref> In the United States and Canada, it grossed $3.3{{nbsp}}million in its opening weekend, across 607 theatres, starting a run of three consecutive weekends in the US-Canada Top 10.<ref name="BOM" /> Including brief re-releases in 2020 and 2022, ''Shaun of the Dead'' grossed $13.5{{nbsp}}million in the United States and Canada, and $25.1{{nbsp}}million in other territories, for a worldwide total of $38.7{{nbsp}}million,<ref name="BOM" /> against a budget of $6{{nbsp}}million.<ref>{{cite web |author=Andrew Collins |title=Simon Pegg: The World's End is $4 million shy of double what Hot Fuzz cost |url=http://www.radiotimes.com/news/2013-07-19/simon-pegg-the-worlds-end-is-4-million-shy-of-double-what-hot-fuzz-cost |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170803170906/http://www.radiotimes.com/news/2013-07-19/simon-pegg-the-worlds-end-is-4-million-shy-of-double-what-hot-fuzz-cost |archive-date=3 August 2017 |access-date=22 August 2013 |work=RadioTimes}}</ref>

===Critical response===
[[File:Premios Goya 2018 - Bill Nighy 02.jpg|alt=Bill Nighy on a red carpet|thumb|upright|Bill Nighy was praised for his role as Shaun's stepfather Philip.<ref name="Ebert"/><ref name="Elder"/>]]
''Shaun of the Dead'' received critical acclaim. {{RT prose|{{RT data|score}}|{{RT data|average}}|{{RT data|count}}|''Shaun of the Dead'' cleverly balances scares and witty satire, making for a bloody good zombie movie with loads of wit.|ref=yes|access-date=2023-09-08}}[[Metacritic]], which uses a [[Weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average]], assigned the {{#switch:  | episode = episode | series = series | season = season | film }} a score of 76 out of 100, based on 34 critics, indicating "generally favorable" reviews.<ref name="Metacritic">{{cite web |title=''Shaun of the Dead'' |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/shaun-of-the-dead |website=[[Metacritic]] |publisher=[[Fandom (website)#Fandom, Inc.|Fandom, Inc.]] |access-date=2023-09-08 |archive-url= |archive-date= |url-status=}}</ref>

Nev Pierce, reviewing the film for the [[BBC]], called it a "side-splitting, head-smashing, gloriously gory horror comedy" that will "amuse casual viewers and delight genre fans".<ref name=pierce>{{cite web|title=Shaun of the Dead (2004)|url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/films/2004/03/26/shaun_of_the_dead_2004_review.shtml|author=Nev Pierce|publisher=BBC|date=7 April 2004|access-date=1 November 2009|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090407002708/http://www.bbc.co.uk/films/2004/03/26/shaun_of_the_dead_2004_review.shtml|archive-date=7 April 2009|url-status=live}}</ref>  [[Peter Bradshaw]] gave it four stars out of five, saying it "boasts a script crammed with real gags" and is "pacily directed [and] nicely acted".<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2004/apr/09/simonpegg|title=Shaun of the Dead|author=Peter Bradshaw|work=The Guardian|date=9 April 2004|access-date=1 November 2009|location=London|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131208074253/http://www.theguardian.com/film/2004/apr/09/simonpegg|archive-date=8 December 2013|url-status=live|author-link=Peter Bradshaw}}</ref> Wendy Ide for [[Screen Daily]] wrote that the film "proves that the move from small to big screen comedy does not always end in artistic failure", saying that the film stays true to Pegg and Wright's style but also makes use of comedy more accessible to the British masses than that of ''Spaced''.<ref name="screendaily">{{Cite web|last=Ide|first=Wendy|date=10 March 2004|title=Shaun Of The Dead|url=https://www.screendaily.com/shaun-of-the-dead/4017736.article|access-date=2020-06-24|website=Screen|archive-date=26 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200626065758/https://www.screendaily.com/shaun-of-the-dead/4017736.article|url-status=live}}</ref>

Pierce wrote that the choice of weapons was amusing, and he suggested that the film's real strength was the characterisation of the unhappy leads. He felt that the climax at the pub was lacking in horror and comedy compared to the rest of the film.<ref name="pierce"/> Like Pierce, Ide felt that there is "a convincing emotional depth" despite the comedy; she similarly noted that the second half was slower but chalked this up to being darker in tone at the climax.<ref name="screendaily"/> She praised the special effects make-up and prosthetics created by Stuart Conran.<ref name="screendaily"/> [[Keith Phipps]] of ''[[The A.V. Club]]'' enjoyed the record-throwing scene, citing it as an example of where the film "doesn't mind putting in extra work for its laughs", as it comes off funnier with Shaun and Ed debating which records they sacrifice rather than throwing indiscriminately.<ref name=":3">{{Cite web|title=Shaun Of The Dead|url=https://www.avclub.com/shaun-of-the-dead-1798199976|access-date=2020-06-24|website=Film|date=20 September 2004 |language=en-us|archive-date=25 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200625083020/https://film.avclub.com/shaun-of-the-dead-1798199976|url-status=live}}</ref> He found Wright's technical skills to be impressive, adding that Wright left the spotlight to the performances rather than the camerawork, but found the finale to be disappointingly played straight.<ref name=":3"/>

Both the American critics [[Roger Ebert]] and [[Robert K. Elder]] said that the film brought something more to the zombie genre. Ebert wrote that he was "by now more or less exhausted by the cinematic possibilities of killing [zombies]", and so he was glad for what ''Shaun of the Dead'' brought to the table outside of this, writing that "instead of focusing on the Undead and trying to get the laughs there, it treats the living characters as sitcom regulars whose conflicts and arguments keep getting interrupted by annoying flesh-eaters".<ref name="Ebert">{{cite web|last=Ebert|first=Roger|author-link=Roger Ebert|date=24 September 2004|title=Shaun of the Dead Review|url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/shaun-of-the-dead-2004|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190914192420/https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/shaun-of-the-dead-2004|archive-date=14 September 2019|access-date=12 July 2019}}</ref> Elder agreed that by its release the zombie film had "ambled its course", but thought that "''Shaun of the Dead'' stands on its own, a romantic comedy crossed with a quarter-life crisis drama–just played against a background horde of brain-hungry, decomposing undead".<ref name="Elder">{{Cite web|last=Elder|first=Robert K.|date=24 September 2004|title=As a zombie flick send-up, this 'Shaun' is dead on|url=https://www.chicagotribune.com/news/ct-xpm-2004-09-24-0409240008-story.html|url-status=live|access-date=2020-06-25|website=Chicago Tribune|archive-date=12 October 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181012114257/http://www.chicagotribune.com/news/ct-xpm-2004-09-24-0409240008-story.html}}</ref> B. Alan Orange of [[MovieWeb]] wrote that "The British Zombie experience 'is' different enough to change the outlook of a whole genre".<ref name=":9"/>

Of the cast, Ebert particularly praised Nighy, writing that "there's something endearing about his response ["I ran it under a cold tap"] when he is bitten by a zombie".<ref name="Ebert"/> Elder described Nighy as the film's [[scene stealer]].<ref name="Elder"/>

[[Peter Travers]] also gave the film three out of four stars and praised Pegg: "[he] makes you root for Shaun, even when he’s slacking with Ed [...], neglecting Liz and battling with his mum".<ref>{{Cite magazine|last=Travers|first=Peter|author-link=Peter Travers|date=2004-09-03|title=Shaun of the Dead|url=https://www.rollingstone.com/movies/movie-reviews/shaun-of-the-dead-192159/|access-date=2020-06-24|magazine=Rolling Stone|archive-date=25 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200625090221/https://www.rollingstone.com/movies/movie-reviews/shaun-of-the-dead-192159/|url-status=live}}</ref> Phipps' take on Shaun was that "Pegg gives his hero a defeated look that slowly melts away as the crisis at last gives him a chance to become a man of action".<ref name=":3"/>
