'''''Solaris''''' ({{langx|ru|link=no|Солярис|Solyaris}}) is a 1972 Soviet [[Science fiction film|science fiction]] and [[psychological horror]] film based on [[Stanisław Lem]]'s 1961 [[Solaris (novel)|novel of the same title]]. The film was co-written and directed by [[Andrei Tarkovsky]], and stars [[Donatas Banionis]] and [[Natalya Bondarchuk]]. The [[electronic music]] score was performed by [[Eduard Artemyev]] and the film also features [[Orgelbüchlein#Miscellaneous BWV 639–644|a composition]] by [[J.S. Bach]] as its main theme. The plot centers on a [[space station]] orbiting the fictional planet [[Solaris (planet)|Solaris]], where a scientific mission has stalled because the skeleton crew of three scientists have fallen into emotional crises. [[Psychologist]] Kris Kelvin (Banionis) [[Spaceflight|travels]] to the station to evaluate the situation, only to encounter the same mysterious phenomena as the others.

''Solaris'' won the [[Grand Prix (Cannes Film Festival)|Grand Prix Spécial du Jury]] at the [[1972 Cannes Film Festival]] and was nominated for the [[Palme d'Or]].<ref name="festival-cannes.com">{{cite web |url=https://www.festival-cannes.com/en/f/solaris|title=Festival de Cannes: Solaris |access-date=November 29, 2023|publisher=festival-cannes.com}}</ref> It received critical acclaim, and is often cited as one of the greatest science fiction films in the history of cinema.<ref>{{cite news |url=http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/entertainment/3600802.stm |title=Blade Runner tops scientist poll |publisher=[[BBC News]] |access-date=July 3, 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120522175149/http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/entertainment/3600802.stm |archive-date=May 22, 2012 |url-status=live |date=August 26, 2004}}</ref><ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.theguardian.com/life/news/page/0,12983,1290764,00.html |title=Top 10 sci-fi films |work=[[The Guardian]] |access-date=July 3, 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120620102101/http://www.guardian.co.uk/life/news/page/0,12983,1290764,00.html |archive-date=June 20, 2012 |location=London |url-status=dead }}</ref> The film was Tarkovsky's attempt to bring greater emotional depth to science fiction films; he viewed most Western works in the genre, including the recently released ''[[2001: A Space Odyssey]]'' (1968), as shallow due to their focus on technological innovation.<ref name="Criterion">{{cite web | last = Lopate | first = Phillip | title = Solaris | publisher = [[The Criterion Collection]] | url = https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/239-solaris-inner-space| access-date = November 29, 2023 |date=24 May 2011}}</ref> Some of the ideas Tarkovsky expresses in this film are further developed in his film ''[[Stalker (1979 film)|Stalker]]'' (1979).<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.referatcollection.ru/32632.html |title=Бытие и человек в творчестве Андрея Тарковского |last=Solovyeva |first=O. N. |author2=Oboturov A. B. |year=2002 |publisher=Vologda State Pedagogical University |language=ru |access-date=May 7, 2010|trans-title=Genesis and a human in the work of Andrei Tarkovsky}}</ref>

==Plot==
Psychologist Kris Kelvin is about to be sent on an [[interstellar travel|interstellar]] journey to evaluate whether a decades-old [[space station]], positioned over the oceanic planet Solaris, should continue its research. He spends his last day on Earth with his elderly father and a retired pilot named Burton. Years earlier, Burton had been part of an exploratory team at Solaris but was recalled when he described strange happenings, including seeing a four-meter-tall child on the surface of the water on the planet. A panel of scientists and military personnel dismissed these visions as hallucinations, but now that the remaining crew members are making similarly strange reports, Kelvin's skills are needed. After leaving the house, Burton tells Kelvin that he recognized the child's face as that of one who was orphaned due to the disappearance of one of the Solaris explorers.

Upon his arrival at the Solaris research station, he finds it in disarray. He soon learns that his friend among the scientists, Dr. Gibarian, has killed himself. The two surviving crewmen—Snaut and Sartorius—are erratic. Kelvin also catches fleeting glimpses of others aboard the station who were not part of the original crew. He finds that Gibarian left him a rambling, cryptic farewell video message, warning him about the strange things happening at the station. The video shows two appearances of a little girl who should not be aboard the station, with Gibarian asking Kelvin if he has seen her and insisting he is not insane, and should strange things happen to Kelvin, it will not be Kelvin having gone insane.

After a fitful sleep, Kelvin is shocked to find Hari, his wife who died ten years earlier, sitting in his sleeping quarters. She is unaware of how she got there. Terrified by her presence, Kelvin launches the replica of his wife into outer space. Snaut explains that the "visitors" or "guests" began appearing after the scientists conducted radiation experiments, directing [[X-rays]] at the swirling surface of the planet in a desperate attempt to understand its nature.

That evening, Hari reappears in Kelvin's quarters. This time, he calmly accepts her and they fall asleep together in an embrace. Hari panics when Kelvin briefly leaves her alone in the room, and injures herself attempting to break through the door. But before Kelvin can give first aid, her injuries spontaneously heal before his eyes. Sartorius and Snaut explain to Kelvin that Solaris created Hari from his memories of her. The Hari present among them, though not human, thinks and feels as though she were. Sartorius theorizes that the visitors, also called "guests", are not composed of atoms like regular humans, but are made of [[neutrino]]s, and that it might still be possible to destroy them through use of a device known as "the annihilator". Later, Snaut proposes beaming Kelvin's [[Neural oscillation|brainwave]] patterns at Solaris in hopes that it will understand them and stop the disturbing apparitions.

In time, Hari becomes more human and independent and is able to exist away from Kelvin's presence without panicking. She learns from Sartorius that the original Hari had taken her own life ten years earlier. Sartorius, Snaut, Kelvin and Hari gather together for a birthday party, which evolves into a philosophical argument, during which Sartorius reminds Hari that she is not real. Distressed, Hari kills herself again by drinking [[liquid oxygen]], only to painfully resurrect after a few minutes. On the surface of Solaris, the ocean begins to swirl faster into a funnel.

Kelvin falls ill and goes to sleep. He dreams of his mother as a young woman, washing away dirt or scabs from his arm. When he awakens, Hari is gone; Snaut reads her farewell note, in which she explains how she petitioned the two scientists to destroy her with the "annihilator" device. Snaut then tells Kelvin that since broadcasting Kelvin's brainwaves into Solaris, the visitors have stopped appearing and islands have started forming on the planet's surface. Kelvin debates whether to return to Earth or remain on the station.

Kelvin appears to be at the family home seen at the beginning of the film. He sinks to his knees and embraces his father. The camera slowly cranes away to reveal that they are on a Solaris island.

==Production==

===Writing===
In 1968 the director Andrei Tarkovsky had several motives for cinematically adapting [[Stanisław Lem]]'s science fiction novel ''[[Solaris (novel)|Solaris]]'' (1961). First, he admired Lem's work. Second, he needed work and money, because his previous film, ''[[Andrei Rublev (film)|Andrei Rublev]]'' (1966), had gone unreleased, and his screenplay ''A White, White Day'' had been rejected (in 1975, it was realised as ''[[Mirror (1975 film)|Mirror]]''). A film of a novel by Lem, a popular and critically respected writer in the [[Soviet Union|USSR]], was a logical commercial and artistic choice.<ref name=wpowell>{{cite book | last = Tarkovsky | first = Andrei | translator-last1 = Powell | translator-first1 = William | translator-last2 = Synessios |translator-first2 = Natasha | title = Collected Screenplays | publisher = Faber & Faber | year = 1999 | location = London | isbn = 9780571142668}}</ref> Another inspiration was Tarkovsky's desire to bring emotional depth to the [[science fiction]] genre, which he regarded as shallow due to its focus on technological invention; in a 1970 interview, he singled out [[Stanley Kubrick]]'s 1968 film ''[[2001: A Space Odyssey]]'' as "phoney on many points" and "a lifeless schema with only pretensions to truth".<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |date=2014-12-23 |title=Andrei Tarkovsky, Solaris and Stalker |url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/andrei-tarkovsky-solaris-stalker |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241128195132/https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/andrei-tarkovsky-solaris-stalker |archive-date=28 November 2024 |access-date=2026-03-24 |website=BFI |language=en}}</ref>

Tarkovsky and Lem collaborated and remained in communication about the adaptation. With [[Friedrich Gorenstein]], Tarkovsky co-wrote the first screenplay in the summer of 1969; two-thirds of it occurred on Earth. The [[Mosfilm]] committee disliked it, and Lem became furious over the drastic alteration of his novel. The final screenplay yielded the shooting script, which has less action on Earth and deletes Kelvin's marriage to his second wife, Maria, from the story.<ref name=wpowell/> In the novel Lem describes science's inadequacy in allowing humans to communicate with an alien life form, because certain forms, at least, of sentient [[extra-terrestrial life]] may operate well outside of human experience and understanding. In the movie, Tarkovsky concentrates on Kelvin's feelings for his wife, Hari, and the impact of [[outer space]] exploration on the [[human condition]]. Dr. Gibarian's monologue (from the novel's sixth chapter) is the highlight of the final library scene, wherein Snaut says: "We don't need other worlds. We need mirrors". Unlike the novel, which begins with Kelvin's [[Interstellar travel|spaceflight]] and takes place entirely on Solaris, the film shows Kelvin's visit to his parents' house in the country before leaving Earth. The contrast establishes the worlds in which he lives – a vibrant Earth versus an austere, closed-in space station orbiting Solaris – demonstrating and questioning space exploration's impact on the human psyche.<ref>{{cite book | last = Lem | first = Stanisław | title = Solaris | publisher = Harvest Books | date = November 2002 | isbn = 978-0-15-602760-1 | url-access = registration | url = https://archive.org/details/solaris00lems_0 }}</ref>

[[Image:Pieter Bruegel the Elder - Hunters in the Snow (Winter) - Google Art Project.jpg|thumb|left|A detail of ''The Hunters in the Snow'' (1565) by [[Pieter Bruegel the Elder]], a thematic reference]]

The set design of ''Solaris'' features paintings by the [[Old Masters]]. The interior of the [[space station]] is decorated with full reproductions of the 1565 painting cycle of ''The Months'' (''[[The Hunters in the Snow]]'', ''[[The Gloomy Day]]'', ''[[The Hay Harvest]]'', ''[[The Harvesters (painting)|The Harvesters]]'', and ''[[The Return of the Herd]]''), by [[Pieter Bruegel the Elder]], and details of ''[[Landscape with the Fall of Icarus]]'' and ''The Hunters in the Snow'' (1565). The scene of Kelvin kneeling before his father and the father embracing him alludes to ''[[The Return of the Prodigal Son (Rembrandt)|The Return of the Prodigal Son]]'' (1669) by [[Rembrandt]]. The references and allusions are Tarkovsky's efforts to give the young art of cinema historical perspective, to evoke the viewer's feeling that cinema is a mature art.<ref name=eduard>{{cite video | people = Artemyev, Eduard | title = Eduard Artemyev Interview | medium = DVD | publisher = Criterion Collection}}</ref>

The film references Tarkovsky's 1966 film ''[[Andrei Rublev (film)|Andrei Rublev]]'', as an icon by [[Andrei Rublev]] is present in Kelvin's room.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Jones |first=Jonathan |date=2005-02-12 |title=Out of this world |url=https://www.theguardian.com/books/2005/feb/12/featuresreviews.guardianreview11 |access-date=2026-03-24 |work=The Guardian |language=en-GB |issn=0261-3077}}</ref> It is the second of a series of three films referencing Rublev, the last being Tarkovsky's next film ''Mirror'', which was released in 1975 and references ''Andrei Rublev'' by having a poster of the film hung on a wall.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.electricsheepmagazine.co.uk/reviews/2011/07/16/mirror/ |title=Mirror |last=Cairns |first=David |date=July 16, 2011 |website=Electric Sheep |access-date=August 18, 2014}}</ref>

===Casting===
Tarkovsky initially wanted his ex-wife, [[Irma Raush]], to play Hari, but after meeting actress [[Bibi Andersson]] in June 1970 decided that she was better for the role. Wishing to work with Tarkovsky, Andersson agreed to be paid in [[Soviet rouble|roubles]]. Ultimately, [[Natalya Bondarchuk]], whom Tarkovsky met when they were students at the [[All-Union State Institute of Cinematography|State Institute of Cinematography]], was cast as Hari; Bondarchuk had introduced the novel ''Solaris'' to him. Tarkovsky auditioned Bondarchuk in 1970 but decided she was too young for the part. Tarkovsky instead recommended Bondarchuk to director [[Larisa Shepitko]], who cast her in ''[[You and Me (1971 film)|You and Me]]'' (1971). Half a year later, Tarkovsky screened ''You and Me'' and was so impressed by her performance that he decided to cast Bondarchuk as Hari.<ref>{{cite video | people = Bondarchuk, Natalya | title = Natalya Bondarchuk Interview | medium = DVD | publisher = Criterion Collection}}</ref>

Tarkovsky cast [[Donatas Banionis]] as Kelvin, [[Jüri Järvet]] as Snaut, [[Anatoly Solonitsyn]] as Sartorius, [[Nikolai Grinko]] as Kelvin's father, and [[Olga Barnet]] as Kelvin's mother. Tarkovsky had already worked with Solonitsyn, who played Andrei Rublev, and with Grinko, who appeared in ''Andrei Rublev'' and ''[[Ivan's Childhood]]'' (1962). Tarkovsky thought Solonitsyn and Grinko would need extra directorial assistance.<ref>{{cite book | last = Tarkovsky | first = Andrei | author2 = transl. by Kitty Hunter-Blair | title = Time Within Time: The Diaries 1970–1986 | publisher = [[Seagull Books]] | year = 1991 | location = Calcutta | pages = 5–6 (June 13, June 15 & July 11, 1970) | isbn = 81-7046-083-2}}</ref> After filming was almost completed, Tarkovsky ranked the actors and performances thusly: Bondarchuk, Järvet, Solonitsyn, Banionis, Dvorzhetsky, and Grinko; he also wrote in his diary that "Natalya B. has outshone everybody".<ref>{{cite book | last = Tarkovsky | first = Andrei |author2=transl. by Kitty Hunter-Blair | title = Time Within Time: The Diaries 1970–1986 | publisher = [[Seagull Books]] | year = 1991 | location = Calcutta | pages = 44–45 (December 4, 1970) | isbn = 81-7046-083-2}}</ref>

===Filming===
In the summer of 1970 the [[State Committee for Cinematography]] (Goskino SSSR) authorized the production of ''Solaris'', with a length of {{convert|4000|m|ft|0}}, equivalent to a two-hour-twenty-minute running time. The exteriors were photographed at [[Zvenigorod]], near Moscow; the interiors were photographed at the [[Mosfilm]] studios. The scenes of space pilot Burton driving through a city were photographed in September and October 1971 at [[Akasaka, Tokyo|Akasaka]] and Iikura in Tokyo. The original plan was to film futuristic structures at the World [[Expo '70]], but the trip was delayed. The shooting began in March 1971 with cinematographer [[Vadim Yusov]], who also photographed Tarkovsky's previous films. They quarreled so much on this film that they never worked together again.<ref>{{cite book | last = Tarkovsky | first = Andrei |author2=transl. by Kitty Hunter-Blair | title = Time Within Time: The Diaries 1970–1986 | publisher = [[Seagull Books]] | year = 1991 | location = Calcutta | pages = 38–39 (July 12 & August 10, 1970) | isbn = 81-7046-083-2}}</ref><ref>{{cite web | last = Yuji | first = Kikutake | title = Solaris locations in Akasaka and Iikura, Tokyo | url = http://www.acs.ucalgary.ca/~tstronds/nostalghia.com/ThePhotos/jp_Solaris.html | access-date = January 15, 2008 | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20071210105436/http://www.acs.ucalgary.ca/~tstronds/nostalghia.com/ThePhotos/jp_Solaris.html | archive-date = December 10, 2007 | url-status = dead | df = mdy-all }}</ref> [[Eastman Kodak]] color film was used to shoot the color scenes, though it had to be specially procured for the production as it was not widely available in the Soviet Union.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Misek |first1=Richard |chapter='Last of the Kodak': Andrei Tarkovsky's Struggle With Colour |editor1-last=Everett |editor1-first=Wendy |title=Questions of Colour in Cinema: From Paintbrush to Pixel. New Studies in European Cinema |year=2007 |publisher=Peter Lang |pages=161–168 |isbn=978-3-03911-353-8 }}</ref> The first version of ''Solaris'' was completed in December 1971.

[[File:Akasaka, Tokyo.jpg|thumb|'''Solaris locale:''' [[Akasaka, Tokyo]], the future city that space pilot Henri Berton traverses in his car]]
The Solaris ocean was created with [[acetone]], [[aluminium powder]], and dyes.<ref>{{cite video | people = Yusov, Vadim | title = Vadim Yusov Interview | medium = DVD | publisher = Criterion Collection}}</ref> [[Mikhail Romadin]] designed the space station as lived-in, beat-up and decrepit rather than shiny, neat and futuristic. The designer and director consulted with scientist and [[aerospace]] engineer Lupichev, who lent them a 1960s-era [[mainframe computer]] for set decoration. For some of the sequences, Romadin designed a mirror room that enabled Yusov to hide within a mirrored sphere so as to be invisible in the finished film. [[Akira Kurosawa]], who visited the [[Mosfilm]] studios during filming, expressed admiration for the space station design.<ref>{{cite video | people = [[Mikhail Romadin|Romadin, Mikhail]] | title = Mikhail Romadin Interview | medium = DVD | publisher = Criterion Collection}}</ref>

In January 1972 the State Committee for Cinematography requested editorial changes before releasing ''Solaris''. Requests included a more [[Realism (arts)|realistic]] film with a clearer image of the future and deletion of allusions to God and Christianity. Tarkovsky successfully resisted such major changes, and after a few minor edits ''Solaris'' was approved for release in March 1972.<ref>{{cite book | last = Tarkovsky | first = Andrei |author2=transl. by Kitty Hunter-Blair | title = Time Within Time: The Diaries 1970–1986 | publisher = [[Seagull Books]] | year = 1991 | location = Calcutta | pages = 49–55 (January 12 & March 31, 1972) | isbn = 81-7046-083-2}}</ref>

===Music===
The soundtrack of ''Solaris'' features [[Johann Sebastian Bach]]'s chorale prelude for organ [[Orgelbüchlein#Miscellaneous BWV 639–644|''Ich ruf' zu dir, Herr Jesu Christ'', BWV 639]], played by {{ill|Leonid Roizman|ru|Ройзман, Леонид Исаакович}}, and an electronic score by [[Eduard Artemyev]]. The prelude is the central musical theme. Tarkovsky initially wanted the film to be devoid of music and asked Artemyev to orchestrate ambient sounds as the score; the latter proposed subtly introducing orchestral music. The classical music used for Earth's theme stands in counterpoint to the fluid [[electronic music]] used as the theme for the planet Solaris. The character Hari has her own subtheme, a [[cantus firmus]] based on Bach's music featuring Artemyev's music atop it; it is heard at Hari's death and at the story's end.<ref name=eduard/>

==Reception and influence==
''Solaris'' premiered at the [[1972 Cannes Film Festival]], where it won the [[Grand Prix (Cannes Film Festival)|Grand Prix Spécial du Jury]] and was nominated for the [[Palme d'Or]]. In the USSR, the film premiered in the ''Mir'' film theater in Moscow on February 5, 1973. Tarkovsky did not consider the ''Mir'' cinema the best projection venue.<ref>{{cite book | last=Tarkovsky | first=Andrei |author2=translated by Kitty Hunter-Blair  | title=Time Within Time: The Diaries 1970–1986 | publisher=Seagull Books | year=1991 | location=Calcutta | pages=67–70 (January 29, 1973) | isbn=81-7046-083-2}}</ref> The film sold 10.5 million tickets.<ref>{{cite book|last=Segida|first=Miroslava|author2=Sergei Zemlianukhin |title=Domashniaia sinemateka: Otechestvennoe kino 1918–1996 |publisher=Dubl-D |year=1996 |language=ru}}</ref>

Unlike the vast majority of commercial and ideological films in the 1970s, ''Solaris'' screened in the USSR in a [[limited release|limited run]] for 15 years.<ref name=":0" /> ''Solaris'' premiered later in the [[Eastern Bloc]] and in the West. Upon exhibition in the United States, the film, truncated by 30 minutes, premiered at the [[Ziegfeld Theatre (1969)|Ziegfeld Theatre]] in New York City on October 6, 1976.<ref>{{Cite news | last=Eder | first=Richard | author-link = Richard Eder| title=Movie Review: Solaris (1972) | newspaper=[[The New York Times]] | date=October 7, 1976 |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1976/10/07/archives/film.html|page=61}}</ref>

===Critical response===
Film critic [[Roger Ebert]] reviewed the 1976 release for ''[[The Chicago Sun-Times]]'', giving three out of four stars and writing, "''Solaris'' isn't a fast-moving action picture; it's a thoughtful, deep, sensitive movie that uses the freedom of science fiction to examine human nature. It starts slow, but once you get involved, it grows on you.'<ref name="Chicago">{{Cite web |title=Solaris movie review & film summary (1976) |url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/solaris-1976|date=28 December 1976|website=Chicago Sun-Times|last=Ebert|first=Roger|via=RogerEbert.com}}</ref> Ebert added ''Solaris'' to his ''[[Great Movies]]'' list in 2003, saying he had initially "balked" at its length and pacing but later came to admire Tarkovsky's goals. "No director makes greater demands on our patience. Yet his admirers are passionate and they have reason for their feelings: Tarkovsky consciously tried to create art that was great and deep. He held to a romantic view of the individual able to transform reality through his own spiritual and philosophical strength."<ref>{{Cite web |last=Ebert |first=Roger |date=19 January 2003 |title=Solaris movie review & film summary review |url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/great-movie-solaris-1972 |access-date=2026-03-24 |website=Chicago Sun-Times |language=en-US |via=RogerEbert.com}}</ref> Ebert later compared the 2011 film ''[[Another Earth]]'' to ''Solaris'', writing that ''Another Earth'' "is as thought-provoking, in a less profound way, as Tarkovsky's ''Solaris'', another film about a sort of parallel Earth".<ref name="Ebert">{{cite news |last=Ebert |first=Roger |date=July 27, 2011 |title=Another Earth (PG-13) |url=http://rogerebert.suntimes.com/apps/pbcs.dll/article?AID=/20110727/REVIEWS/110729984 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130310021111/http://rogerebert.suntimes.com/apps/pbcs.dll/article?AID=/20110727/REVIEWS/110729984 |archive-date=March 10, 2013 |access-date=February 11, 2012 |work=[[Chicago Sun-Times]]}}</ref> Ebert and [[Jonathan Rosenbaum]] also noted ''Solaris''{{'}}s influence on the 1997 film ''[[Event Horizon (film)|Event Horizon]].''<ref>[https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/event-horizon-1997 "Event Horizon"], film review by [[Roger Ebert]]</ref><ref>[https://www.chicagoreader.com/chicago/event-horizon/Film?oid=16580962 "Event Horizon"], film review by [[Jonathan Rosenbaum]]</ref>

Since garnering further acclaim, the film has a rating of 92% on [[Rotten Tomatoes]] based on 66 reviews, with an average rating of 8.90/10. The consensus states: "''Solaris'' is a haunting, meditative film that uses sci-fi to raise complex questions about humanity and existence."<ref>{{rotten-tomatoes|solaris_1976|Solaris}}</ref> It has a score of 93 out of 100 on [[Metacritic]], based on eight critics.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Solaris (1972) |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/solaris-1972/ |website=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=11 April 2025}}</ref> In 2018, the film ranked 57th on the [[BBC]]'s list of the 100 greatest foreign-language films, as voted on by 209 film critics from 43 countries.<ref>{{cite web |date=29 October 2018 |title=The 100 Greatest Foreign Language Films |url=https://www.bbc.com/culture/article/20181029-the-100-greatest-foreign-language-films |access-date=10 January 2021 |publisher=[[British Broadcasting Corporation]]}}</ref> A list of "The 100 Best Films of World Cinema" ''[[Empire (magazine)|Empire]]'' magazine compiled in 2010 ranked Tarkovsky's ''Solaris'' at No. 68.<ref>{{cite web | title=The 100 Best Films of World Cinema | url=http://www.empireonline.com/features/100-greatest-world-cinema-films/default.asp?film=68 | work=Empire|date=2010|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20111125102535/http://www.empireonline.com/features/100-greatest-world-cinema-films/default.asp?film=68|archive-date=25 November 2011}}</ref>

===Retrospective response===
[[Salman Rushdie]] has called ''Solaris'' "a sci-fi masterpiece", adding, "This exploration of the unreliability of reality and the power of the human unconscious, this great examination of the limits of rationalism and the perverse power of even the most ill-fated love, needs to be seen as widely as possible before it's transformed by Steven Soderbergh and James Cameron into what they ludicrously threaten will be ''2001'' meets ''[[Last Tango in Paris]]''. What, sex in space with floating butter? Tarkovsky must be turning over in his grave."<ref>Rushdie, Salman. ''Step Across This Line: Collected Nonfiction 1992–2002''. New York: Random House, 2002, p. 335.</ref> In her 1997 article "Identifying Fears", M. Galina called ''Solaris'' "one of the biggest events in Soviet science fiction cinema" and one of few that do not now seem anachronistic.<ref>{{cite web |last=Galina |first=M. S. |year=1997 |title=Strangers among us. Identifying fears |url=http://ecsocman.edu.ru/images/pubs/2004/07/03/0000165154/017Galina1.pdf |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220530013158/http://ecsocman.edu.ru/images/pubs/2004/07/03/0000165154/017Galina1.pdf |archive-date=May 30, 2022 |access-date=May 7, 2010 |work=Social Sciences and Modernity |publisher=ecsocman.edu.ru |pages=160 |language=ru}}</ref> Japanese filmmaker [[Akira Kurosawa]] cited ''Solaris'' as one of his favorite films.<ref>{{cite web |last=Jagernauth |first=Kevin |date=January 9, 2015 |title=Akira Kurosawa's 100 Favorite Movies Include Films By Martin Scorsese, Francis Ford Coppola, Woody Allen, And More |url=https://www.indiewire.com/2015/01/akira-kurosawas-100-favorite-movies-include-films-by-martin-scorsese-francis-ford-coppola-woody-allen-and-more-268564/ |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220407234847/https://www.indiewire.com/2015/01/akira-kurosawas-100-favorite-movies-include-films-by-martin-scorsese-francis-ford-coppola-woody-allen-and-more-268564/ |archive-date=April 7, 2022 |access-date=May 26, 2024 |work=[[IndieWire]]}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=Akira Kurosawa's Top 100 Movies! |url=http://wildgrounds.com/index.php/2009/01/17/akira-kurosawas-top-100-movies/ |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100327124349/http://wildgrounds.com/index.php/2009/01/17/akira-kurosawas-top-100-movies/ |archive-date=March 27, 2010}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |date=2015-01-26 |title=Akira Kurosawa on watching 'Solaris' with Andrei Tarkovsky |url=https://cinephiliabeyond.org/akira-kurosawa-watching-solaris-andrei-tarkovsky/ |access-date=2024-03-01 |language=en-US|website=Cinephilia & Beyond}}</ref> [[Adam Curtis]]' documentary film ''[[Bitter Lake (film)|Bitter Lake]]'' (2015) features scenes from ''Solaris''.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Wollaston |first=Sam |date=2015-01-26 |title=Bitter Lake – review: Adam Curtis’s beautiful, gripping film unravels a story of violence, bloodshed and bitter ironies |url=https://www.theguardian.com/tv-and-radio/2015/jan/26/bitter-lake-review-adam-curtis-afghanistan |access-date=2026-03-24 |work=The Guardian |language=en-GB |issn=0261-3077}}</ref> Its influence on [[Christopher Nolan]]'s ''[[Inception]]'' (2010) was noted.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Bowen |first=Kevin |date=2010-07-16 |title=Inception - THE OTHER VIEW |url=https://screencomment.com/2010/07/the-other-view-bowen-sounds-off-on-inception/ |access-date=2026-03-24 |website=Screen Comment |language=en-US}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book |last=Botz-Bornstein |first=Thorsten |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=q62dW7vEjE0C&pg=PA205 |title=Inception and Philosophy: Ideas to Die for |date=2011 |publisher=Open Court Publishing |isbn=978-0-8126-9733-9 |page=205 |language=en |chapter=The Movie as a Thinking Machine}}</ref>

Although Lem worked with Tarkovsky and [[Friedrich Gorenstein]] to develop the screenplay, he maintained he "never really liked Tarkovsky's version" of his novel.<ref>{{cite web |last=Lem |first=Stanisław |title=Solaris |url=http://english.lem.pl/arround-lem/adaptations/qsolarisq-by-tarkovsky |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181207103021/http://english.lem.pl/arround-lem/adaptations/qsolarisq-by-tarkovsky |archive-date=December 7, 2018 |access-date=January 14, 2008}}</ref> Tarkovsky wanted a film based on the novel but artistically independent of it, while Lem opposed any divergence of the screenplay from the novel. Lem went as far as to say that Tarkovsky made ''[[Crime and Punishment]]'' rather than ''Solaris'', omitting [[Epistemology|epistemological]] and [[Cognition|cognitive]] aspects of his book.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Bereś |first=Stanisław |url= |title=Rozmowy ze Stanisławem Lemem |title-link=Rozmowy ze Stanisławem Lemem |last2=Lem |first2=Stanisław |date=1987 |publisher=Wydawn. Literackie |isbn=978-83-08-01656-5 |location=Krakow, WL |pages=133–135 |language=pl}}</ref> But Lem also said in an interview that he had only seen part of the finale, much later, after Tarkovsky's death.<ref name="ATK">{{Cite book |last=Franz |first=Norbert P. |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=1l6FDQAAQBAJ&pg=PA60 |title=Andrej Tarkovskij: Klassiker – Классик – Classic – Classico: Beiträge zum internationalen Tarkovskij-Symposium an der Universität Potsdam ; Band 1 |date=2016-11-14 |publisher=Universitätsverlag Potsdam |isbn=978-3-86956-351-0 |page=60 |language=de}}</ref> Tarkovsky claimed that Lem did not fully appreciate cinema and expected the film to merely illustrate the novel without creating an original cinematic piece; Tarkovsky's film is about the inner lives of its characters while Lem's novel is about the conflicts of man's condition in nature and the nature of man in the universe. For Tarkovsky, Lem's exposition of that existential conflict was the starting point for depicting the characters' inner lives.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Illg |first=Jerzy |author2=Leonard Neuger |year=1987 |title=Z Andriejem Tarkowskim rozmawiają Jerzy Illg, Leonard Neuger (The Illg_Neuger Tarkovsky Interview (1985)) |url=http://www.acs.ucalgary.ca/~tstronds/nostalghia.com/TheTopics/interview.html#On_Solaris |url-status=dead |journal=[[Res Publica (journal)|Res Publica]] |language=en |location=Warsaw |volume=1 |pages=137–160 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080116155850/http://www.acs.ucalgary.ca/~tstronds/nostalghia.com/TheTopics/interview.html#On_Solaris |archive-date=January 16, 2008 |access-date=January 16, 2008 |df=mdy-all}}</ref> In the autobiographical documentary ''[[Voyage in Time]]'' (1983), Tarkovsky says he viewed ''Solaris'' as an artistic failure because it did not transcend genre as he believed his film ''[[Stalker (1979 film)|Stalker]]'' (1979) did, due to the required technological dialogue and special effects.<ref>{{cite video |title=[[Voyage in Time]] |medium=DVD |publisher=Facets |people=Tarkovsky, Andrei}}</ref> 

In an example of life imitating art, Bondarchuk revealed in a 2010 interview that she fell in love with Tarkovsky while filming ''Solaris'' and, after their relationship ended, became suicidal. She said her decision was partly influenced by her role.<ref name="KP-20100331">{{cite web |last=Pleshakova |first=Anastasia |date=March 31, 2010 |title=Natalya Bondarchuk: "After an affair with Tarkovsky I baptized" |url=http://www.kp.ru/daily/24465/625480/ |access-date=July 25, 2014 |work=[[Komsomolskaya Pravda]]}}</ref>

===Home media===
''Solaris'' was released on [[LaserDisc]] in Japan in 1986.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://dinca.org/andrei-tarkovsky-solaris-1972-poster-gallery/5902.htm|title=14 Posters: Andrei Tarkovsky's Solaris (1972) - Dinca|date=August 4, 2010|access-date=May 3, 2018|archive-date=May 4, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180504155446/https://dinca.org/andrei-tarkovsky-solaris-1972-poster-gallery/5902.htm|url-status=dead}}</ref>

Like many other films by Tarkovsky, it was initially released on DVD by Ruscico in 2000. The film is split onto two discs and contains a newly-mixed 5.1 surround sound track, optional voice-overs in English and French, and subtitles in thirteen different languages. In addition to the release being all-region, the contents of the discs have been licensed to various international distributors, including [[Curzon Film|Artificial Eye]] in the U.K., MK2 in France <ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.dvdbeaver.com/film/dvdcompare/solaris.htm|title="Solaris" - Krupnyi plan (Russia) - Region 0 - Andrei Tarkovsky Solaris DVD Review comparison|website=DVDBeaver}}</ref>, Llamentol in Spain, and Shock Records in Australia. In Italy, a separate DVD was released by 20th Century Fox Home Entertainment in 2001 that featured its own restoration, as well as an Italian dub. <ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.dvdbeaver.com/film/DVDCompare/solaris-it.htm|title=Solaris DVD Comparison - 20th Century Fox PAL vs. Criterion NTSC|website=DVDBeaver}}</ref>

[[The Criterion Collection]] released the film on DVD in North America on 26 November 2002. It is a two-disc set, with the first disc containing the feature alongside an audio essay by Vida Johnson and Graham Petrie (co-authors of ''The Films of Tarkovsky: A Visual Fugue'') On May 24, 2011, they released it on [[Blu-ray Disc]] in North America, alongside a DVD re-release.<ref name="Criterion" /><ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.blu-ray.com/movies/Solaris-Blu-ray/20744/|title=Solaris Blu-ray}}</ref> The most noticeable difference from their previous DVD release<ref>{{cite web|url=http://people.ucalgary.ca/~tstronds/nostalghia.com/TheTopics/newsCriterionOnSolaris.html|title=Solaris DVD – FAQ}}</ref> was that the blue and white tinted monochrome scenes from the film were restored.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://criterioncast.com/2011/02/13/criterion-announces-new-solaris-dvd-blu-ray-for-may-2011-selling-current-stock-at-65-off/|title=Criterion Announces New Solaris DVD & Blu-ray For May 2011, Selling Current Stock At 65% Off|first=Ryan|last=Gallagher|date=February 13, 2011|website=Criterion Cast}}</ref> However, whereas the original DVD is region-free, the second DVD is locked to region 1. Criterion also released it in 2017 in the U.K.

==Legacy==
[[Steven Soderbergh]] directed a [[Solaris (2002 film)|2002 adaptation]] of ''Solaris'' for American audiences, starring [[George Clooney]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.filmthreat.com/interviews/126/|archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20131103110119/http://www.filmthreat.com/interviews/126/|title=Steven Soderbergh Unleashed: Part 2|work=[[Film Threat]]|first=Chris|last=Gore|date=February 18, 2001|accessdate=April 3, 2024|archivedate=November 3, 2013}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=https://variety.com/2002/film/awards/solaris-1117877198/|work=Variety|title=Solaris|last=Koehler|first=Robert|date=December 9, 2002|accessdate=April 3, 2024}}</ref> It received mixed to positive reviews from critics and underperformed at the box office.

The 1972 film was selected for screening as part of the Cannes Classics section at the [[2016 Cannes Film Festival]].<ref name="Classics">{{cite web |date=20 April 2016 |title=Cannes Classics 2016 |url=http://www.festival-cannes.fr/en/article/62136.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170210012746/http://www.festival-cannes.fr/en/article/62136.html |archive-date=February 10, 2017 |access-date=21 April 2016 |work=Cannes Film Festival |df=mdy-all}}</ref>
