'''''Titicut Follies''''' is a 1967 American [[direct cinema]] [[documentary film]] produced, written, and directed by [[Frederick Wiseman]] and filmed by [[John Marshall (filmmaker)|John Marshall]]. It deals with the patient-inmates of [[Bridgewater State Hospital]] for the Criminally Insane, a [[List of Massachusetts state correctional facilities|Massachusetts Correctional Institution]] in [[Bridgewater, Massachusetts]]. The title is taken from that of a [[talent show]] put on by the hospital staff. ''Titicut'' is the [[Massachusett language|Wampanoag]] name for the nearby [[Taunton River]].

The film won accolades in Germany and Italy. Wiseman went on to produce many more such films examining social institutions (e.g. hospitals, police, schools, etc.) in the United States.

In 2022, the film was selected for preservation in the United States [[National Film Registry]] by the [[Library of Congress]] as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".<ref name="Ulaby">{{cite web|url=https://www.npr.org/2022/12/14/1141630795/iron-man-super-fly-and-carrie-are-inducted-into-the-national-film-registry|title='Iron Man,' 'Super Fly' and 'Carrie' are inducted into the National Film Registry|date=December 14, 2022|first=Neda|last=Ulaby|website=[[NPR]]|access-date=December 14, 2022|archive-date=July 9, 2024|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240709111500/https://www.npr.org/2022/12/14/1141630795/iron-man-super-fly-and-carrie-are-inducted-into-the-national-film-registry|url-status=live}}</ref>

== Synopsis ==
''Titicut Follies'' portrays the occupants of [[Bridgewater State Hospital]], who were often kept in barren cells and infrequently bathed. It also depicts inmates/patients forced to strip naked publicly, [[force feeding]], and the indifference and bullying by many of the hospital's staff.

The film employs methods of [[direct cinema]], which emphasizes observation, limited stylization, and non-intervention by filmmakers.<ref name="TITICUT FOLLIES">{{cite web|url=http://userhome.brooklyn.cuny.edu/anthro/jbeatty/COURSES/non_fiction/titicut.html|title=Titicut Follies|website=[[Brooklyn College]]|access-date=April 20, 2023|archive-date=August 18, 2024|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240818000129/http://userhome.brooklyn.cuny.edu/anthro/jbeatty/COURSES/non_fiction/titicut.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

== Production ==
=== Development ===
''Titicut Follies'' was the beginning of the documentary career of [[Frederick Wiseman]], a Boston-born lawyer turned filmmaker. He had taken his law classes from [[Boston University]] to the institution for educational purposes and had "wanted to do a film there". He began calling the facility superintendent, seeking permission to film a year prior to production. Wiseman had previously produced ''[[The Cool World]]'' (1964), based on [[Warren Miller (author)|Warren Miller]]'s [[The Cool World (novel)|novel of the same name]], an experience that informed his desire to direct.

=== Filming ===
Wiseman drafted a proposal that was verbally agreed to by the superintendent, which later came into question when the film began distribution. Following that agreement, filming began, with corrections staff following Wiseman at all times and determining on the spot whether the subjects filmed were mentally competent, adding further confusion to an already fraught process.<ref name=Anderson1981>{{cite journal |last1=Anderson |first1=Carolyn |title=The Conundrum of Competing Rights in TITICUT FOLLIES |journal=Journal of the University Film Association |date=1981 |volume=33 |issue=1 |pages=15–22 |jstor=20687550 }}</ref> While on location, Wiseman recorded the sound and directed the {{nowrap|cameraman{{mdash}}}}established [[ethnography|ethnographic]] filmmaker [[John Marshall (filmmaker)|John Marshall⁠]]{{nowrap|{{mdash}}}}via microphone or by hand.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Anderson |first1=Carolyn |last2=Benson |first2=Thomas W. |title=Documentary Dilemmas: Frederick Wiseman's Titicut Follies |date=1991 |publisher=SIU Press |isbn=978-0-8093-1518-5 }}{{page needed|date=February 2022}}</ref>

=== Post-production ===
Twenty-nine days were spent documenting the conditions at Bridgewater and 80,000 feet of film were shot. Wiseman spent approximately a year editing the footage into the final 84-minute narrative.<ref name=Anderson1981/> Wiseman edited the film in secret, working after hours in the editing rooms at [[WGBH-TV]].<ref>{{Cite web |via=[[YouTube]] |title=WGBH - 125 Western Avenue tour - Part 1 |date=7 July 2011 |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2TzrZlD0Ng |access-date=2023-10-29 |language=en |archive-date=December 27, 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241227132736/https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2TzrZlD0Ng |url-status=live }}</ref>

== Release ==
=== Censorship ===
Just before the film was to be shown at the 1967 [[New York Film Festival]], the Massachusetts government tried to procure an injunction banning its release,<ref name="Vice">{{cite news|first=Jesse|last=Pearson|title=The Follies of Documentary Filmmaking|url=https://www.vice.com/en/article/doc-v14n9/|work=[[Vice (magazine)|Vice]]|access-date=2008-04-21|archive-date=December 27, 2024|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241227120714/https://www.vice.com/en/article/doc-v14n9/|url-status=live}}</ref> claiming that the film violated the patients' privacy and dignity.<ref name="WaPo">{{cite news |first=David |last=Mills |title=Mass. Court Lifts Ban On 24-Year-Old Film; Privacy Right Overruled for Wiseman's 'Titicut' |url=http://www.highbeam.com/doc/1P2-1077949.html |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121019152252/http://www.highbeam.com/doc/1P2-1077949.html |url-status=usurped |archive-date=2012-10-19 |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |date=1991-08-02 |via=[[HighBeam Research]] |url-access=subscription |access-date=2008-04-21 }}</ref> Despite Wiseman having received permission from all the people portrayed or that of the hospital superintendent (the inmates' [[legal guardian]]), Massachusetts claimed that this permission could not take the place of release forms from the inmates.<ref name="MassCourt">{{cite court |litigants=WISEMAN v. MASSACHUSETTS |vol=398 |reporter=U.S. |opinion=960 |pinpoint= |court= |date=1970 |url=http://caselaw.lp.findlaw.com/scripts/getcase.pl?navby=CASE&court=US&vol=398&page=960}}</ref> Wiseman was also accused of breaching an "[[oral contract]]", giving the state government editorial control over the film.<ref name="Vice"/> A New York state court allowed the screening,<ref name="WaPo"/> but in 1968, [[Massachusetts Superior Court]] judge Harry Kalus ordered the film to be recalled from distribution and all copies destroyed, once more citing the state's concerns about violations of the patients' privacy and dignity.<ref name="lettheviewerdecide">{{cite journal |last1=Walker |first1=J |title=Let the Viewer Decide: Documentarian Frederick Wiseman on free speech, complexity, and the trouble with Michael Moore |journal=Reason |date=2007 |volume=39 |issue=7 |pages=50–54 |oclc=182570840 }}</ref>

Wiseman appealed to the [[Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court]], which in 1969 allowed it to be shown only to doctors, lawyers, judges, health-care professionals, social workers, and students in these and related fields.<ref name="Vice"/> Wiseman appealed to the [[United States Supreme Court]], which refused to hear the case.<ref name="MassCourt"/>

Wiseman believes that the government of Massachusetts (concerned that the film portrayed a state institution in a bad light) intervened to protect its reputation. The state intervened after a social worker in Minnesota wrote to Massachusetts governor [[John Volpe]], expressing shock at a scene involving a naked man being taunted by a guard.<ref name="Vice"/>

The dispute was the first known instance of a film being banned from general American distribution for reasons other than obscenity, immorality, or national security.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Lesser |first1=Wendy |title=Unwise Restrictions |journal=The Threepenny Review |date=1992 |issue=48 |pages=25 |jstor=4384055 }}</ref> It was also the first time that Massachusetts recognized a [[right to privacy]] at the state level.<ref name="lettheviewerdecide"/> Wiseman has said, "The obvious point that I was making was that the restriction of the court was a greater infringement of civil liberties than the film was an infringement on the liberties of the inmates."<ref name="lettheviewerdecide"/>

Little changed until 1987, when the families of seven inmates who had died at the hospital sued the hospital and state. Steven Schwartz represented one of the inmates, who was "restrained for 2½ months and given six psychiatric drugs at vastly unsafe levels—choked to death because he could not swallow his food."<ref name="shiningalight">{{cite news |last1=Horn |first1=Miriam |title=Shining A Light On Their Follies |work=U.S. News & World Report |date=12 April 1993 }}</ref> Schwartz has said "There is a direct connection between the decision not to show that film publicly and my client dying 20 years later, and a whole host of other people dying in between,"<ref name="shiningalight"/> "... in the years since Mr. Wiseman made ''Titicut Follies'', most of the nation's big mental institutions have been closed or cut back by court orders"<ref>{{cite news |last1=Goodman |first1=Walter |title=Review/Television; An Unhealthy Hospital Stars in 'Titicut Follies' |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1993/04/06/movies/review-television-an-unhealthy-hospital-stars-in-titicut-follies.html |work=The New York Times |date=6 April 1993 |archive-date=February 28, 2022 |access-date=February 28, 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220228135102/https://www.nytimes.com/1993/04/06/movies/review-television-an-unhealthy-hospital-stars-in-titicut-follies.html |url-status=live }}</ref> and "the film may have also influenced the closing of the institution featured in the film."<ref>{{cite book |last1=Aufderheide |first1=Patricia |title=Documentary Film: A Very Short Introduction |date=2007 |publisher=Oxford University Press |isbn=978-0-19-983998-8 }}{{page needed|date=February 2022}}</ref>

In August 1991, Superior Court judge Andrew Meyer allowed the film's release to the general public, saying that as time had passed, privacy concerns had become less important than [[First Amendment to the United States Constitution|First Amendment]] concerns. He also said that many of the former patients had died, so there was little risk of a violation of their dignity.<ref name="WaPo"/> The state Supreme Court ordered that "A brief explanation shall be included in the film that changes and improvements have taken place at Massachusetts Correctional Institution Bridgewater since 1966."<ref>{{cite video | people =Fredrick Wiseman (director)| title =Titicut Follies| medium =| publisher =Zipporah Films, Inc.| location =| date = 1992}}</ref> 

According to Thomas W. Benson and Carolyn Anderson, "When Wiseman learned of Judge Meyer's decision, he called Alan Friedberg, President of Loews Cineplex Entertainment, which sponsors the Boston Film Festival, and a showing of'' Titicut Follies'' was arranged for the Cheri Theatre on 12 September 1991".<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Benson |first1=Thomas W. |last2=Anderson |first2=Carolyn |title=The Freeing of ''Titicut Follies'' |journal=Free Speech Yearbook |volume=30 |issue=1 |page=51 |year=1992 |doi=10.1080/08997225.1992.10556139}}</ref> 

The film premiered{{NoteTag|According to some sources, the film premiered on September 4th, 1992, but there is no evidence to support this.}} on American television on [[PBS]] in April 1993.<ref>{{cite news |last=Moore |first=Frazier |date=April 5, 1993 |title='Titicut Follies' airs on public TV |work=The Birmingham News |page=12D |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-birmingham-news/203138353/ |url-access=subscription |agency=Associated Press}}</ref> Before it was shown, a narrative warning and an introduction by [[Charlie Rose]] was played. Following the broadcast, a message was shown stating that improvements had been made since the time of production.

The film is now legally available through its distributor, Zipporah Films Inc., for purchase or rental on [[DVD]] and for educational and individual license. Zipporah released the DVD to the home market in December 2007.

In 2020, the film was shown on [[Turner Classic Movies]]. The film was selected for preservation in the United States [[National Film Registry]] by the [[Library of Congress]] in 2022 as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".<ref name="Ulaby"/> The film was released in the UK in 2026 as part of a boxed set Blu-ray collection of several of Frederick Wiseman's films, titled ''Cinema Expanded: The Films of Frederick Wiseman''.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.orbitdvd.com/products/pre-order-cinema-expanded-the-films-of-frederick-wiseman-region-b|title=Cinema Expanded: The Films of Frederick Wiseman|website=Orbitdvd.com|access-date=February 17, 2026}}</ref> The film was scanned and restored in 4K by the [[British Film Institute]]; this marks the first time ''Titicut Follies'' has been released on high-definition media anywhere in the world.

== Reception ==
[[Review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]] reports that 100% of 12 critics gave the film a positive review, with an average rating of 7.9/10.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Titicut Follies |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/titicut_follies |access-date=June 14, 2023 |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |publisher=[[Fandango Media]] |archive-date=June 2, 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230602180150/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/titicut_follies |url-status=live }}</ref>
