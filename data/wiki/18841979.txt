'''''The Blair Witch Project''''' is a 1999 American [[psychological horror film]] written, edited, and directed by [[Daniel Myrick]] and [[Eduardo Sánchez (director)|Eduardo Sánchez]]. The [[Found footage (film technique)|found footage]] work is a [[pseudo-documentary]] in which three young students ([[Heather Donahue]], [[Michael C. Williams (actor)|Michael C. Williams]], and [[Joshua Leonard]]) hike into the [[Appalachian Mountains]] near [[Burkittsville, Maryland]], to shoot a documentary about a local myth known as the Blair Witch.

Myrick and Sánchez conceived of a fictional legend of the Blair Witch in 1993. They developed a 35-page screenplay with the dialogue to be [[improvisation|improvised]]. A [[casting (performing arts)|casting]] call advertisement on ''[[Backstage (publication)|Backstage]]'' magazine was prepared by the directors; Donahue, Williams, and Leonard were cast. The film entered production in October 1997, with the [[principal photography]] lasting eight days. Most of the filming was done on the Greenway Trail along Seneca Creek in [[Montgomery County, Maryland]]. About 20 hours of footage was shot, which was edited down to 82 minutes. Shot on an original budget of $35,000–$60,000, the film had a final cost of $200,000–$750,000 after post-production and marketing.

When ''The Blair Witch Project'' premiered at the [[Sundance Film Festival]] at midnight on January 23, 1999, its promotional marketing campaign listed the actors as either "missing" or "deceased". Due to its successful Sundance run, [[Artisan Entertainment]] bought the film's distribution rights for $1.1 million. The film had a [[limited theatrical release]] on July 14 of the same year, before expanding to a [[wide release|wider release]] starting on July 30. While the film received highly positive reviews from critics, audience reception was polarized.

One of the most successful [[independent film]]s of all time, ''The Blair Witch Project'' was a [[sleeper hit]] that grossed $248.6 million worldwide. The film was also ranked the best found footage movie of all time by editors from both ''[[IndieWire]]''<ref>{{Cite web |last=Chapman |first=Wilson |date=October 7, 2024 |title=The 22 Best Found Footage Movies, from 'Paranormal Activity' to 'One Cut of the Dead' |url=https://www.indiewire.com/gallery/best-found-footage-movies/mcdlani-if014/ |access-date=March 31, 2025 |website=[[IndieWire]] |language=en-US}}</ref> and ''[[Entertainment Weekly]]''.<ref>{{Cite magazine |title=The 25 best found footage movies of all time, ranked |url=https://ew.com/best-found-footage-movies-ranked-8710733 |access-date=March 31, 2025 |magazine=[[Entertainment Weekly]] |language=en |archive-date=March 31, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250331141808/https://ew.com/best-found-footage-movies-ranked-8710733 |url-status=live }}</ref> Despite the success, the three main actors had reportedly lived in poverty. In 2000, they sued Artisan Entertainment claiming unfair compensation, eventually reaching a $300,000 settlement. ''The Blair Witch Project'' launched a [[Blair Witch|media franchise]], which includes two sequels (''[[Book of Shadows: Blair Witch 2|Book of Shadows]]'' and ''[[Blair Witch (film)|Blair Witch]]''), novels, comic books, and video games. It revived the found-footage technique and influenced similarly successful horror films such as ''[[Paranormal Activity]]'' (2007), ''[[Rec (film)|Rec]]'' (2007), and ''[[Cloverfield]]'' (2008).

==Plot==
<!--Please see WP:FILMPLOT before adding any material. The plot summary should be between 400-700 words.-->
In October 1994, film students Heather, Mike, and Josh set out to produce a [[documentary]] about the mythical Blair [[Witchcraft|Witch]]. They travel to [[Burkittsville, Maryland]], and interview residents about the myth. Locals tell them of Rustin Parr, a [[hermit]] who lived deep in the forest and abducted seven children in 1941; he murdered them all in his basement, killing them in pairs while having one stand in a corner, facing the wall. The students explore the forest in north Burkittsville to research the myth. They meet two fishermen, one of whom warns them that the forest is cursed. He tells them of a young child named Robin Weaver, who went missing in 1888; when she returned three days later, she talked about an old woman whose feet never touched the ground.

The students decide to hike to Coffin Rock for their first day in the woods due to its connection with the Robin Weaver story. Despite Robin returning to Burkittsville, one of the search parties that set out into the Black Hills Forest to look for her never came back. Eventually, another search party came across the bodies of the five missing men, their hands and feet tied together as they were ritualistically gutted and displayed upon Coffin Rock. They went back to town to gather help in burying the bodies of the missing search party, but upon returning to Coffin Rock, the bodies had vanished without a trace.

They [[Camping|camp]] for the night and, the next day, find an old graveyard with seven small [[cairn]]s, one of which Josh accidentally knocks over. That night, they hear the sound of sticks snapping. The following day, they try to hike back to the car but cannot find it before dark and make camp. They again hear sticks snapping. In the morning, they find three cairns built beside their tent. Heather learns her map is missing; Mike reveals he kicked it into a creek out of frustration, which provokes a fight between the trio as they realize they are lost. They head south, using Heather's compass, and discover stick figures hanging from trees. They again hear mysterious sounds that night, including children laughing. After an unknown force shakes the tent, they run outside and hide in the forest until dawn.

Upon returning to their tent, they find their possessions have been rifled, and Josh's equipment is covered with slime. They come across the same river they crossed earlier and realize they have been walking in circles. Josh vanishes the next morning, and Heather and Mike fail to find him. That night, they hear Josh's agonized cries but are unable to locate him; they theorize that his yells are a fabrication by the Blair Witch to draw them out of their camp.

Heather discovers a bundle of twigs tied with fabric from Josh's shirt the next day. Upon opening the bundle, she finds a blood-soaked scrap of his shirt containing bloodied teeth and hair. Although distraught, she does not tell Mike. That night, she records herself tearfully apologizing to her, Mike's, and Josh's mothers, taking responsibility for their predicament. She admits that something evil is haunting them and will ultimately take them.

Later, they hear Josh's voice again calling for help and follow it to the abandoned ruins of a house, featuring children's bloody handprints on some of the walls. Trying to find Josh, they head to the attic but do not find him. Hearing the voice everywhere, Mike rushes downstairs, leaving Heather behind. As he enters the basement, an unseen force strikes Mike; he drops his camera and falls silent. A hysterical Heather enters the basement, to see Mike standing in a corner facing the wall, unresponsive to her screaming his name. The unseen force strikes Heather, causing her to drop her camera and fall silent as well; the camera then cuts to black.

==Production==
===Development===
Development of ''The Blair Witch Project'' began in 1993.<ref name="onion"/> While film students at the [[University of Central Florida]], Daniel Myrick and Eduardo Sánchez were inspired to make the film after realizing that they found [[documentaries]] on paranormal phenomena scarier than traditional horror films. The two decided to create a film that combined the styles of both. In order to produce the project, they, along with [[Gregg Hale (producer)|Gregg Hale]], Robin Cowie and Michael Monello, started [[Haxan Films]]. The namesake for the production company is [[Benjamin Christensen]]'s 1922 [[silent film|silent]] documentary horror film ''[[Häxan]]'' (English: ''Witchcraft Through the Ages'').<ref name="villagevoice1">{{cite web |last=Kaufman |first=Anthony |title=Season of the Witch |work=[[The Village Voice]] |date=July 14, 1999 |url=http://www.villagevoice.com/news/9928,kaufman,7024,1.html |access-date=September 26, 2006 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070303170002/http://www.villagevoice.com/news/9928%2Ckaufman%2C7024%2C1.html |archive-date=March 3, 2007}}</ref>

Myrick and Sánchez developed a 35-page screenplay for their fictional film, intending dialogue to be [[improvisation|improvised]]. The directors placed a [[casting (performing arts)|casting]] call advertisement in ''[[Backstage (magazine)|Backstage]]'' in June 1996, asking for actors with strong improvisational abilities.<ref name="real"/><ref name=timeline/> The informal improvisational audition process narrowed the pool of 2,000 actors.<ref name="houseofhorrors"/><ref name="wapo">{{cite news |author=<!--Not stated--> |title=Film Special: With Dan Myrick and Eduardo Sanchez |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/talk/movies/blairwitchdirectors.htm |access-date=March 17, 2017 |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |date=June 30, 1999 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160615193314/http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/talk/movies/blairwitchdirectors.htm |archive-date=June 15, 2016}}</ref>

According to Heather Donahue, auditions for the film were held at Musical Theater Works in [[New York City]]. The advertisement said a "completely improvised feature film" would be shot in a "wooded location". Donahue said that during the audition, Myrick and Sánchez posed her the question: "You've served seven years of a nine-year sentence. Why should we let you out on parole?" to which she had to respond.<ref name="real"/> Joshua Leonard said he was cast due to his knowledge of how to run a camera, as no omniscient camera was used to film the scenes.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.backstage.com/news/spotlight/how-joshua-leonard-fell-love-moviemaking/ |work=[[Backstage (magazine)|Backstage]] |title=How Joshua Leonard Fell In Love With Moviemaking |author=Loewenstein, Melinda |date=March 16, 2013 |access-date=October 31, 2015 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130309205137/http://www.backstage.com/news/spotlight/how-joshua-leonard-fell-love-moviemaking/ |archive-date=March 9, 2013}}</ref>

Pre-production began on October 5, 1997, and Michael Monello became a co-producer.<ref name="makingofpt3">{{cite news |last1=Rock |first1=Ben |author-link=Ben Rock |title=The Making of The Blair Witch Project: Part 3 – Doom Woods Preppers |url=http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/180906/making-blair-witch-project-part-3-doom-woods-preppers/ |access-date=March 27, 2017 |publisher=[[Dread Central]] |date=August 15, 2016 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170328022029/http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/180906/making-blair-witch-project-part-3-doom-woods-preppers/ |archive-date=March 28, 2017}}</ref><ref name=timeline/> In developing the mythology behind the film, the creators used many inspirations. For instance, several character names are near-[[anagram]]s: Elly Kedward (The Blair Witch) is [[Edward Kelley]], a 16th-century mystic, and Rustin Parr, the fictional 1940s child-murderer, began as an anagram for [[Rasputin]].<ref name="makingofpt1">{{cite news |last1=Rock |first1=Ben |author-link=Ben Rock |title=The Making of The Blair Witch Project: Part 1 – Witch Pitch |url=http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/180272/making-blair-witch-project-part-1-witch-pitch/ |access-date=March 19, 2017 |publisher=[[Dread Central]] |date=August 1, 2016 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161230161548/http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/180272/making-blair-witch-project-part-1-witch-pitch/ |archive-date=December 30, 2016}}</ref> The Blair Witch is said to be, according to legend, the ghost of Elly Kedward, a woman banished from the Blair Township (latter-day [[Burkittsville, Maryland|Burkittsville]]) for [[witchcraft]] in 1785.

The directors incorporated that part of the legend, along with allusions to the [[Salem witch trials]] and [[Arthur Miller]]'s 1953 play ''[[The Crucible]]'', to play on the themes of injustice done to those who were classified as witches.<ref name="witch">{{cite web |last=Aloi |first=Peg |title=Blair Witch Project – an Interview with the Directors |publisher=[[The Witches' Voice]] |date=July 11, 1999 |url=http://www.witchvox.com/va/dt_va.html?a=usma&c=media&id=2416 |access-date=July 29, 2006 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20060525160439/http://www.witchvox.com/va/dt_va.html?a=usma&c=media&id=2416 |archive-date=May 25, 2006}}</ref>

The directors also cited influences such as the television series ''[[In Search of... (TV series)|In Search of...]]'', and horror documentary films ''[[Chariots of the Gods (film)|Chariots of the Gods]]'' and ''[[The Legend of Boggy Creek]]''.<ref name="houseofhorrors">{{cite news |title=An Exclusive Interview with Dan Myrick, Director of The Blair Witch Project |url=http://www.houseofhorrors.com/bwinterview.htm |access-date=March 17, 2017 |work=House of Horrors |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160303183648/http://www.houseofhorrors.com/bwinterview.htm |archive-date=March 3, 2016}}</ref><ref name="wapo"/> Other influences included commercially successful horror films such as ''[[The Shining (film)|The Shining]]'', ''[[Alien (film)|Alien]]'', ''[[The Omen]]'', and ''[[Jaws (film)|Jaws]]''—the latter film being their major influence, as the film hides the witch from the viewer for its entirety, increasing the suspense of the unknown.<ref name="onion">{{cite web |last=Klein |first=Joshua |title=Interview – The Blair Witch Project |work=[[The A.V. Club]] |date=July 22, 1999 |url=http://www.avclub.com/article/the-blair-witch-project-13607 |access-date=January 26, 2015 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150121163922/http://www.avclub.com/article/the-blair-witch-project-13607 |archive-date=January 21, 2015}}</ref><ref name="houseofhorrors"/>

In talks with investors, the directors presented an eight-minute documentary, along with newspapers and news footage.<ref name="stone">{{cite magazine |last=Conroy |first=Tom |title=The Do-It-Yourself Witch Hunt |magazine=[[Rolling Stone]] |date=July 14, 1999 |url=https://www.rollingstone.com/news/story/5924486/the_doityourself_witch_hunt |access-date=August 2, 2006 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071001001407/http://www.rollingstone.com/news/story/5924486/the_doityourself_witch_hunt |archive-date=October 1, 2007}}</ref> The documentary was aired on the television series ''[[Split Screen (TV series)|Split Screen]]'' hosted by [[John Pierson (filmmaker)|John Pierson]] on August 6, 1998.<ref name="houseofhorrors"/><ref name=timeline>{{cite news |newspaper=[[Daily Variety]] |date=September 8, 1999 |page=A2 |title='Blair' timeline |last=Lyons |first=Charles}}</ref>

===Filming===
[[Principal photography]] began on October 23, 1997, in [[Maryland]] and lasted eight days, overseen by cinematographer [[Neal Fredericks]], who provided a [[CP-16]] film camera.<ref name="villagevoice1"/><ref name="makingofpt3"/><ref name="makingofpt4">{{cite news |last1=Rock |first1=Ben |author-link=Ben Rock |title=The Making of The Blair Witch Project Part 4: Charge of the Twig Brigade |url=http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/181921/part-4-charge-twig-brigade/ |access-date=March 19, 2017 |publisher=[[Dread Central]] |date=August 22, 2016 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160909111425/http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/181921/part-4-charge-twig-brigade/ |archive-date=September 9, 2016}}</ref> The three actors shot all the footage shown in the film, except for one interview about Rustin Parr's murders.<ref name="dissolve">{{cite web |last1=D'Angelo |first1=Mike |title=15 years beyond the hype and hatred of The Blair Witch Project / The Dissolve |url=https://thedissolve.com/features/movie-of-the-week/800-the-blair-witch-project-15-years-beyond-the-hype-a/ |website=The Dissolve |date=28 October 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141028111820/https://thedissolve.com/features/movie-of-the-week/800-the-blair-witch-project-15-years-beyond-the-hype-a/ |archive-date=October 28, 2014}}</ref> The "[[Found footage (film technique)|found footage]]" was shot with a Hi8 camcorder.<ref name="makingofpt4"/><ref>{{cite book|last=Metz|first=Cade|title=PC Magazine May 23, 2006: Making an indie film|url={{Google Books|7HCzmWfb47sC|page=76|plainurl=yes}}|pages=76–82}}</ref> Most of the film was shot in [[Seneca Creek State Park]] in [[Montgomery County, Maryland]]. A few scenes were filmed in the historic town of Burkittsville. Some of the townspeople interviewed in the film were not actors, and some were planted actors, unknown to the main cast.<ref name="makingofpt4"/> Donahue had never operated a camera before and spent two days in a "crash course". Donahue said she modeled her character after a director she had once worked with, noting her character's "self-assuredness" when everything went as planned, and confusion during crisis.<ref>{{cite web |last=Lim |first=Dennis |title=Heather Donahue Casts A Spell |work=[[The Village Voice]] |date=July 14, 1999 |url=http://www.villagevoice.com/news/9928,lim,7025,1.html |access-date=September 26, 2007 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071204214220/http://www.villagevoice.com/news/9928%2Clim%2C7025%2C1.html |archive-date=December 4, 2007}}</ref>

The actors were given clues as to their next location through messages hidden inside 35&nbsp;mm [[film can]]s left in milk crates they found with [[satellite navigation]] units. They were given individual instructions to use to help improvise the action of the day.<ref name="real">{{cite web |author=<!--Not stated--> |title=Heather Donohue – Blair Witch Project |work=KAOS 2000 Magazine |date=August 14, 1999 |url=http://www.kaos2000.net/interviews/heatherdonohue/heatherdonohue.html |access-date=July 30, 2006 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100330204910/http://www.kaos2000.net/interviews/heatherdonohue/heatherdonohue.html |archive-date=March 30, 2010}}</ref><ref name="makingofpt4"/><ref name="Q&A"/> Teeth were obtained from a Maryland dentist for use as human remains in the film.<ref name="real"/> Influenced by producer Gregg Hale's memories of his military training, in which "enemy soldiers" would hunt a trainee through wild terrain for three days, the directors moved the characters a long way during the day, harassing them by night, and depriving them of food.<ref name="stone"/>

Instead of using fictional names, all three actors used their real names in the film, something Donahue has regretted doing. She revealed in 2014 that she had trouble finding new roles because of it.<ref>{{cite AV media |people=Wallace, James |title=TFW 2014: 15 Years of The Blair Witch Project |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J-aA5g37sbk |archive-url=https://ghostarchive.org/varchive/youtube/20211221/J-aA5g37sbk |time=28:58 |url-status=live |website=YouTube |archive-date=2021-12-21 |access-date=19 October 2022}}{{cbignore}}</ref>

===Post-production===
After filming, the 20 hours of raw footage was cut down to 81 minutes; the editing process took more than eight months. The directors screened the first cut in small [[film festival]]s in order to get feedback and make changes that would ensure that it appealed to as large an audience as possible.<ref name="onion"/> Originally, it was hoped that the film would make it on to cable television, and the directors did not anticipate a [[wide release]].<ref name="onion"/> The final version was submitted to [[Sundance Film Festival]].<ref name="makingofpt6">{{cite news |last1=Rock |first1=Ben |author-link=Ben Rock |title=The Making of The Blair Witch Project: Part 6 – Guerrilla Tactics |url=http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/183493/the-making-of-the-blair-witch-project-part-6-guerrilla-tactics/ |access-date=March 19, 2017 |publisher=[[Dread Central]] |date=September 5, 2016 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160909120653/http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/183493/the-making-of-the-blair-witch-project-part-6-guerrilla-tactics/ |archive-date=September 9, 2016}}</ref>

After becoming a surprise hit at Sundance, during its midnight premiere on January 25, 1999, [[Artisan Entertainment]] bought the distribution rights for $1.1 million.<ref name="onion"/> Prior to that, Artisan had wanted to change the film's original ending, as the test audience, although scared, were confused by it.<ref name="ewending">{{cite magazine |last1=Kinane |first1=Ruth |title=''The Blair Witch Project'' almost ended with a different terrifying fate |url=http://ew.com/movies/2017/04/05/blair-witch-project-alternate-endings/ |access-date=June 17, 2017 |magazine=[[Entertainment Weekly]] |date=April 5, 2017 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170603135931/http://ew.com/movies/2017/04/05/blair-witch-project-alternate-endings/ |archive-date=June 3, 2017}}</ref> Although the ending was not changed, an additional interview was added to the first section of the film to contextualize the ending.<ref name="wtop">{{cite web |title=Q&A: 'Blair Witch Project' celebrates 20th anniversary in Frederick |url=https://wtop.com/entertainment/2019/10/qa-blair-witch-director-ed-sanchez-hits-library-of-congress-20-years-later/ |website=WTOP News |language=en |date=16 October 2019}}</ref><ref name="dissolve"/> The directors and Williams traveled back to Maryland and shot four alternate endings,<ref name="makingofpt7">{{cite news |last1=Rock |first1=Ben |author-link=Ben Rock |title=The Making of The Blair Witch Project: Part 7 – The Embiggening |url=http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/184950/the-making-of-the-blair-witch-project-part-7-the-embiggening/ |access-date=March 19, 2017 |publisher=[[Dread Central]] |date=September 12, 2016 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160919170355/http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/184950/the-making-of-the-blair-witch-project-part-7-the-embiggening/ |archive-date=September 19, 2016}}</ref> one of which employed bloody elements. They also shot additional interviews, at least one of which (the Parr backstory) made it into the wide-release cut.<ref name="wtop"/> This footage would be the only segment of film not shot by the main actors.<ref name="dissolve"/> Ultimately, the directors and Williams decided to keep the original ending. Myrick said: "What makes us fearful is something that's out of the ordinary, unexplained. The first ending kept the audience off balance; it challenged our real world conventions and that's what really made it scary".<ref name="ewending"/>

Post-production fees increased the cost of the film to several hundred thousand dollars before its Sundance debut and, after marketing costs, the total cost of the film has been estimated as ranging between $500,000 and $750,000.<ref name="Q&A"/><ref name=season>{{cite news |newspaper=[[Daily Variety]] |date=September 8, 1999 |page=A2 |title=Season of the 'Witch' |last=Lyons |first=Charles}}</ref>

===Compensation controversy===
In 2024, Donahue, Leonard and Williams detailed to ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' how, according to their claims, they had never been adequately compensated by Artisan Entertainment/Lionsgate for their work in the original movie. They recall living in poverty after the success, being barred from saying so publicly, and having few options since they had no "proper union or legal representation when the film was made." In the summer of 1999, they received a "performance bump" in the low five figures. In October 2000, with the release of ''[[Book of Shadows: Blair Witch 2]]'' impending, Donahue convinced Williams and Leonard to sue Artisan. They reached a $300,000 [[Settlement (litigation)|settlement]] for each of them. The three actors claim that they had no clear knowledge that the entire movie would be composed of their found footage, and that they had not given much thought to the clause that allowed production to use their real identities. This led to subsequent discussions with Lionsgate regarding the usage of their likenesses.
Despite the conflicts, Donahue, Leonard and Williams have stated that they are proud of their work in the movie.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Vary |first=Adam B. |date=2024-06-12 |title='The Blair Witch Project' Actors Call Out 'Reprehensible Behavior' After Missing Out on Profits for Decades: 'Don't Do What We Did' (EXCLUSIVE) |url=https://variety.com/2024/film/news/blair-witch-project-cast-robbed-financial-success-1236033647/ |access-date=2024-06-12 |website=Variety |language=en-US |archive-date=June 12, 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240612234301/https://variety.com/2024/film/news/blair-witch-project-cast-robbed-financial-success-1236033647/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Fictional legend===
The backstory for the film is a legend fabricated by Sánchez and Myrick which is detailed in the ''Curse of the Blair Witch'', a [[mockumentary]] broadcast on the [[Sci-Fi Channel]] on July 12, 1999.<ref name=curseoftheblairwitch>{{cite magazine |title=Curse of the Blair Witch |url=http://www.ew.com/article/1999/10/29/curse-blair-witch |magazine=[[Entertainment Weekly]] |date=October 29, 1999 |access-date=August 4, 2016 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160903185750/http://www.ew.com/article/1999/10/29/curse-blair-witch |archive-date=September 3, 2016}}</ref><ref name=timeline/> Sánchez and Myrick also maintained a website which added further details to the legend.<ref name="Curse of the Blair Witch">{{cite web |url=http://www.blairwitch.com/mythology.html |title=The Blair Witch |website=blairwitch.com |access-date=August 4, 2016 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070707140445/http://www.blairwitch.com/mythology.html |archive-date=July 7, 2007}}</ref>

The legend describes the killings and disappearances of some of the residents of Blair, Maryland (a fictitious town on the site of [[Burkittsville, Maryland]]) from the 18th to 20th centuries. Residents blamed these occurrences on the ghost of Elly Kedward, a Blair resident accused of practicing witchcraft in 1785 and sentenced to death by [[hypothermia|exposure]]. ''Curse of the Blair Witch'' presents the legend as real, complete with manufactured newspaper articles, [[newsreels]], television news reports, and staged interviews.<ref name=curseoftheblairwitch />

==Release==
''The Blair Witch Project'' premiered as a Midnight Screening on Saturday, January 23, 1999, at the [[Sundance Film Festival]], and opened Wednesday, July 14, at the [[Angelika Film Center]] in [[New York City]] before expanding to 25 cities at the weekend. It expanded nationwide on July 30.<ref name=timeline/>

====Books====
In September 1999, D.{{nbsp}}A. Stern compiled ''The Blair Witch Project: A Dossier''. Building on the film's "true story" angle, the dossier consisted of fictional police reports, pictures, interviews, and newspaper articles presenting the film's premise as fact, as well as further elaboration on the Elly Kedward and Rustin Parr legends. Another "dossier" was created for ''[[Book of Shadows: Blair Witch 2|Blair Witch 2]]''. Stern wrote the 2000 novel ''Blair Witch: The Secret Confessions of Rustin Parr''. He revisited the franchise with the novel ''Blair Witch: Graveyard Shift'', which features original characters and plot.<ref name="ComingSoon">{{cite news |last1=Lesnick |first1=Silas |title=The Complete Blair Witch Legend |url=http://www.comingsoon.net/horror/features/763177-complete-blair-witch-legend#/slide/1 |access-date=January 9, 2017 |publisher=[[CraveOnline]] |date=September 16, 2016 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170110015921/http://www.comingsoon.net/horror/features/763177-complete-blair-witch-legend |archive-date=January 10, 2017}}</ref>

A series of eight [[young adult fiction|young adult]] books, titled ''[[The Blair Witch Files]]'', were released by [[Random House|Random]] subsidiary [[Bantam Books|Bantam]] from 2000 to 2001. The books center on Cade Merill, a fictional cousin of Heather Donahue, who investigates phenomena related to the Blair Witch. She tries to learn what really happened to Heather, Mike, and Josh.<ref name="Files">{{cite web |last=Merill |first=Cade |title=Cade Merill's The Blair Witch Files |publisher=[[Random House]] |year=2000 |url=http://www.randomhouse.com/features/blairwitch/home.html |access-date=September 8, 2009 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20081207020654/http://www.randomhouse.com/features/blairwitch/home.html |archive-date=December 7, 2008}}</ref>

====Comic books====
In July 1999, [[Oni Press]] released a [[One-shot (comics)|one-shot comic]] promoting the film, titled ''The Blair Witch Project'' #1. Written and illustrated by Cece Malvey, the comic was released in conjunction of the film.<ref name="screenrant2">{{cite news |last1=Kleinhenz |first1=Marc N. |title=A Complete Guide to the Blair Witch Mythology |url=http://screenrant.com/blair-witch-project-mythology/ |access-date=March 17, 2017 |work=Screen Rant |date=September 20, 2016 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170226013433/http://screenrant.com/blair-witch-project-mythology/ |archive-date=February 26, 2017}}</ref> In October 2000, coinciding with the release of ''Book of Shadows: Blair Witch 2'', [[Image Comics]] released a one-shot called ''Blair Witch: Dark Testaments'', drawn by [[Charlie Adlard]].<ref name="ComingSoon"/>

====Video games====
In 2000, [[Gathering of Developers]] released a [[Blair Witch (video games)|trilogy of computer games]] based on the film, which greatly expanded on the myths first suggested in the film. The graphics engine and characters were all derived from the producer's earlier game ''[[Nocturne (Game)|Nocturne]]''.<ref>{{cite web |last1=Smith |first1=Jeff |title=Blair Witch Project Interview |url=https://ign.com/articles/2000/04/15/blair-witch-project-interview |access-date=January 10, 2017 |website=[[IGN]] |date=April 14, 2000 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160305152032/https://ign.com/articles/2000/04/15/blair-witch-project-interview |archive-date=March 5, 2016}}</ref>

The first volume, ''[[Blair Witch Volume I: Rustin Parr|Rustin Parr]]'', received the most praise, ranging from moderate to positive, with critics commending its storyline, graphics and atmosphere; some reviewers claimed that the game was scarier than the film.<ref>{{cite web |title=Blair Witch Volume I: Rustin Parr |url=https://metacritic.com/game/pc/blair-witch-volume-i-rustin-parr |website=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=January 10, 2017 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160814163455/https://metacritic.com/game/pc/blair-witch-volume-i-rustin-parr |archive-date=August 14, 2016}}</ref> The following volumes, ''[[Blair Witch Volume II: The Legend of Coffin Rock|The Legend of Coffin Rock]]'' and ''[[Blair Witch Volume III: The Elly Kedward Tale|The Elly Kedward Tale]]'', were less well received, with ''[[PC Gamer]]'' saying that Volume 2's "only saving grace was its cheap price",<ref>{{cite journal |title=Blair Witch 2: The Legend of Coffin Rock |journal=PC Gamer |date=February 2001 |page=88}}</ref> and calling Volume 3 "amazingly mediocre".<ref>{{cite journal |url=http://www.pcgamer.com/reviews/archives/review_2001-08-07b.html |title=Blair Witch Volume 3: The Elly Kedward Tale |last=Williamson |first=Colin |journal=[[PC Gamer]] |date=April 2001 |page=90 |access-date=March 10, 2014 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20050311162242/http://www.pcgamer.com/reviews/archives/review_2001-08-07b.html |archive-date=March 11, 2005}}</ref>

[[Bloober Team]] developed ''[[Blair Witch (video game)|Blair Witch]]'', a first-person survival horror game based on the ''Blair Witch'' franchise.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.blooberteam.com/blair-witch-game |title=Bloober Team - Blair Witch |access-date=September 2, 2019}}</ref> The game was released on August 30, 2019.

====Documentary====
''The Woods Movie'' (2015) is a feature-length [[documentary]] exploring the production of ''The Blair Witch Project''.<ref>{{cite web |title=Blair Witch Documentary Goes into The Woods Movie |publisher=[[Dread Central]] |date=January 1, 2006 |url=http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/89704/blair-witch-documentary-goes-woods-movie/ |access-date=July 28, 2006 |archive-date=February 14, 2015 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150214005552/http://www.dreadcentral.com/news/89704/blair-witch-documentary-goes-woods-movie/}}</ref> For this documentary, director Russ Gomm interviewed the original film's producer, Gregg Hale, and directors Eduardo Sánchez and Daniel Myrick.<ref>{{cite web |last=Miska |first=Brad |author-link=Brad Miska |title=Finally a Doc On 'The Blair Witch Project' – Trailer For 'The Woods Movie{{'-}} |work=[[Bloody Disgusting]] |date=January 1, 2006 |url=https://bloody-disgusting.com/videos/3332333/finally-doc-blair-witch-project-trailer-woods-movie/ |access-date=2025-02-06 |archive-date=February 13, 2015 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150213183545/https://bloody-disgusting.com/videos/3332333/finally-doc-blair-witch-project-trailer-woods-movie/}}</ref>

===Parodies===
''The Blair Witch Project'' inspired a number of [[parody film]]s, including ''[[Scary Movie]]'', ''[[Da Hip Hop Witch]]'', ''[[The Bogus Witch Project]]'', The Dairy Witch, ''[[Cultural depictions of Tony Blair|The Tony Blair Witch Project]]'' (all in 2000), and ''[[The Blair Thumb]]'' (2001),<ref name="Greene 177">{{cite book |last=Greene |first=Heather |date=2018 |title=Bell, Book and Camera: A Critical History of Witches in American Film and Television |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=9pxWDwAAQBAJ&pg=PA185 |publisher=[[McFarland & Company]] |page=177 |isbn=978-1476662527}}</ref> as well as the [[pornographic film]]s ''The Erotic Witch Project''<ref name="Greene 177" /> and ''The Bare Wench Project''.<ref name="Heller-Nicholas 113">{{cite book |last=Heller-Nicholas |first=Alexandra |date=2014 |title=Found Footage Horror Films: Fear and the Appearance of Reality |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=pkNXAwAAQBAJ&pg=PA113 |publisher=[[McFarland & Company]] |page=113 |isbn=978-0786470778}}</ref> The film also inspired the [[List of Halloween television specials|Halloween television special]] ''[[The Scooby-Doo Project]]'', which aired during a ''[[Scooby-Doo, Where Are You!]]'' marathon on [[Cartoon Network]] on October 31, 1999. 2013's ''[[6-5=2]]'' was also inspired by this film.<ref name="Heller-Nicholas 113" /><ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.pastemagazine.com/tv/how-the-1999-scooby-doo-project-parody-inspired-th/ |title=How the 1999 Scooby Doo Project Parody Inspired Adult Swim's Absurdist, Stoner Comedy |last=Morgan |first=Chris |date=June 2, 2016 |website=[[Paste (magazine)|Paste]] |access-date=September 4, 2020}}</ref>

==Television==
In October 2017, co-director Eduardo Sánchez revealed that he and the rest of the film's creative team were developing a ''Blair Witch'' [[television series]], though he clarified that any decisions would ultimately be up to Lionsgate now which owns the rights to it.<ref>{{cite news |title=Eduardo Sanchez Hints at Blair Witch TV Series |url=https://www.starburstmagazine.com/tv-news19545-blair-witch-tv-series-news |access-date=October 30, 2017 |work=[[Starburst (magazine)|Starburst]] |date=October 30, 2017 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180126030127/https://www.starburstmagazine.com/tv-news19545-blair-witch-tv-series-news |archive-date=January 26, 2018}}</ref><ref>{{cite news |title=74 - Podcast of Horror II: The Blair Witch Project |url=https://www.dimreturns.com/episodes/2017/10/27/74-podcast-of-horror-ii-the-blair-witch-project |access-date=October 30, 2017 |work=Diminishing Returns |date=October 30, 2017 |archive-date=October 31, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171031025433/https://www.dimreturns.com/episodes/2017/10/27/74-podcast-of-horror-ii-the-blair-witch-project |url-status=live }}</ref> The series was later announced to be released on the studio's new subsidiary, Studio L, which specializes in digital releases.<ref>{{cite news |last1=McNary |first1=Dave |title=Lionsgate Unveils 'Studio L' Digital Slate With 'Honor List', 'Most Likely to Murder' |url=https://variety.com/2018/digital/news/lionsgate-unveils-studio-l-digital-slate-1202707449/ |access-date=February 23, 2018 |work=Variety |date=February 22, 2018 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180222180359/https://variety.com/2018/digital/news/lionsgate-unveils-studio-l-digital-slate-1202707449/ |archive-date=February 22, 2018}}</ref>
