'''Female body shape''' or '''female figure''' is the cumulative product of a [[woman]]'s bone structure along with the distribution of muscle and fat on the body.

[[Female]] figures are typically narrower at the [[waist]] than at the [[breast|bust]] and [[hip]]s. The bust, waist, and hips are called [[inflection point]]s, and the ratios of their [[circumference]]s are used to define basic body shapes.

Reflecting the wide range of individual beliefs on what is best for physical [[health]] and what is preferred [[aesthetics|aesthetically]], there is no universally acknowledged [[Ideal (ethics)|ideal]] female body shape. Ideals may also vary across different cultures, and they may exert influence on how a woman perceives her own body image.<ref name="Cultural Diversity and Ethnic Minority Psychology 16(2), 135-143">{{cite web |last1=Gordon |first1=K.H. |last2=Castro |first2=Y. |last3=Sitnikov |first3=L. |last4=Holm-Denoma |first4=J.M. |title=Cultural body shape ideals and eating disorder symptoms among White, Latina, and Black college women |url=http://psycnet.apa.org/record/2010-07475-005 |website=PsycNET / |access-date=6 September 2018}}</ref>

==Physiology==
===Impact of estrogens===
[[Estrogens]], which are primary female sex hormones, have a significant impact on a female's body shape. They are produced in both [[man|men]] and [[woman|women]], but their levels are significantly higher in women, especially in those of reproductive age. Besides other functions, estrogens promote the development of female [[secondary sexual characteristic]]s, such as [[breasts]] and [[hip]]s.<ref name="pmid9393999">{{cite journal | title = A role for estrogens in the male reproductive system | journal=Nature | volume = 390 | issue = 6659 | pages = 447–48 | year = 1997 | pmid = 9393999 | pmc=5719867 | doi = 10.1038/37352 | last1=Hess | first1=R. A. | last2=Bunick | first2=D | last3=Lee | first3=K. H. | last4=Bahr | first4=J | last5=Taylor | first5=J. A. | last6=Korach | first6=K. S. | last7=Lubahn | first7=D. B. | bibcode=1997Natur.390..509H }}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.sciencenews.org/pages/sn_arc97/12_6_97/fob1.htm |title=Science News Online (12/6/97): Estrogen's Emerging Manly Alter Ego |access-date=4 March 2008 |last=Raloff, J. |date=6 December 1997 |publisher=Science News |archive-date=1 August 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130801125427/http://www.sciencenews.org/pages/sn_arc97/12_6_97/fob1.htm }}</ref><ref name="titleScience Blog &ndash; Estrogen Linked To Sperm Count, Male Fertility">{{cite web | url = http://www.scienceblog.com/community/older/1997/B/199701564.html | title = Science Blog – Estrogen Linked To Sperm Count, Male Fertility | access-date = 4 March 2008 | publisher = Science Blog | archive-date = 7 May 2007 | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20070507120938/http://www.scienceblog.com/community/older/1997/B/199701564.html }}</ref> As a result of estrogens, during [[puberty]], girls develop breasts and their hips widen. Working against estrogen, the presence of [[testosterone]] in a pubescent female inhibits breast development and promotes muscle and facial hair development.<ref name="WebMD">{{cite web | url=http://www.webmd.com/women/guide/normal-testosterone-and-estrogen-levels-in-women?page=2 | title=Normal Testosterone and Estrogen Levels in Women | publisher=WebMD | work=Website | access-date=28 October 2015}}</ref>

Estrogen levels also rise significantly during [[pregnancy]]. A number of [[Maternal physiological changes in pregnancy|other changes]] typically occur during pregnancy, including enlargement and increased firmness of the breasts, mainly due to [[Organ hypertrophy|hypertrophy]] of the mammary gland in response to the [[hormone]] [[prolactin]]. The size of the nipples may increase noticeably. These changes may continue during [[breastfeeding]]. Breasts generally revert to approximately their previous size after pregnancy, although there may be some increased sagging.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Physiologic changes of pregnancy: A review of the literature |pmc=5715231 |url=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5715231/ |vauthors=Motosko CC,  Kalowitz Bieber A,  Keltz Pomeranz M, Stein JA,  Martires KJ}}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |title=Anatomy and Physiology of the Breast during Pregnancy and Lactation |pmid=32816256 | doi=10.1007/978-3-030-41596-9_1 | volume=1252 |year=2020 |journal=Adv Exp Med Biol |pages=3–7 | last1 = Alex | first1 = A | last2 = Bhandary | first2 = E | last3 = McGuire | first3 = KP}}</ref>

Breasts can decrease in size at [[menopause]] if estrogen levels decline.<ref>{{cite web |title=How Menopause Affects Your Breasts |url=https://www.webmd.com/menopause/breasts-menopause |website=WebMD |access-date=29 September 2024 |language=en |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161112145310/https://www.webmd.com/menopause/breasts-menopause |archive-date=12 November 2016}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Kim |first1=Eun Young |last2=Chang |first2=Yoosoo |last3=Ahn |first3=Jiin |last4=Yun |first4=Ji-Sup |last5=Park |first5=Yong Lai |last6=Park |first6=Chan Heun |last7=Shin |first7=Hocheol |last8=Ryu |first8=Seungho |title=Menopausal Transition, Body Mass Index, and Prevalence of Mammographic Dense Breasts in Middle-Aged Women |journal=Journal of Clinical Medicine |date=30 July 2020 |volume=9 |issue=8 |page=2434 |doi=10.3390/jcm9082434 |doi-access=free |pmid=32751482 |pmc=7465213 }}</ref>

When women reach [[menopause]] and the estrogen produced by ovaries declines, fat migrates from their buttocks, hips and thighs to their waists;<ref name=fat>{{cite web|quote=Researchers think that the lack of estrogen at menopause plays a role in driving our fat northward |url=http://health.yahoo.com/topic/nutrition/overview/article/womens-health/26287 |title=A Matter of Fat |last=Andrews |first=Michelle |publisher=Yahoo |date=12 January 2006 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071024193506/http://health.yahoo.com/topic/nutrition/overview/article/womens-health/26287 |archive-date=24 October 2007 }}</ref> later fat is stored at the [[abdomen]].<ref>{{Cite web |last=Solan |first=Matthew |date=1 October 2023 |title=An inside look at body fat |url=https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/an-inside-look-at-body-fat |access-date=7 February 2026 |website=Harvard Health |language=en}}</ref>

[[Body fat percentage]] recommendations are higher for females, as this fat may serve as an energy reserve for pregnancy. Males have less [[subcutaneous fat]] in their faces due to the effects of testosterone;<ref>"Subcutaneous fat in face decreases" – [[Testosterone#Advanced postnatal effects|Advanced postnatal effects]]</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Admin |date=30 July 2019 |title=Jawlines: Understanding Gender Differences, Facial Ageing and Defining |url=https://www.ifaas.co/single-post/jawlines-understanding-gender-differences-facial-ageing-and-defining-using-super-embossed-threads-an |access-date=24 November 2024 |website=ifaas |language=en}}</ref> testosterone also reduces fat by aiding fast [[metabolism]]. The lack of estrogen in males generally results in more fat being deposited around the waist and abdomen (producing an "apple shape").<ref>{{Cite web |date=2 November 2013 |title=What It Means to Have an Apple-Shaped Body |url=https://ninacheriefranklin.com/healthy-living/means-apple-shaped-body/ |access-date=25 November 2024 |website=Nina Cherie Franklin |language=en-US}}</ref>

===Muscles===
[[Testosterone]] is a [[steroid hormone]] which helps build and maintain [[muscle]]s for physical activity, such as exercise. The amount of testosterone produced varies from one individual to another, but, on average, an adult female produces around one-eighth of the testosterone of an adult male,<ref name="pmid14981046">{{cite journal | vauthors = Torjesen PA, Sandnes L | title = Serum testosterone in women as measured by an automated immunoassay and a RIA | journal = Clinical Chemistry | volume = 50 | issue = 3 | pages = 678; author reply 678–9 | date = Mar 2004 | pmid = 14981046 | doi = 10.1373/clinchem.2003.027565 | doi-access = free }}</ref> but females are more sensitive to the hormone.<ref name="isbn0-07-135739-4">{{cite book |vauthors=Dabbs M, Dabbs JM | title = Heroes, rogues, and lovers: testosterone and behavior|url=https://archive.org/details/heroesrogueslove00jame |url-access=registration | publisher=McGraw-Hill | location = New York | year = 2000| isbn = 978-0-07-135739-5 }}</ref>

===Changes to body shape===
The [[aging process]] has an inevitable impact on a person's body shape. A woman's [[sex hormone]] levels will affect the fat distribution on her body. According to Dr. Devendra Singh, "Body shape is determined by the nature of body fat distribution that, in turn, is significantly correlated with women's sex hormone profile, risk for disease, and reproductive capability."<ref name="Singh">{{cite journal |author1=Singh, Devendra |date=Spring 2006 |title=An Evolutionary Theory of Female Physical Attractiveness |journal=[[Eye on Psi Chi]] |location=Chattanooga, TN |publisher=[[Psi Chi]] |volume=10 |issue=3 |pages=18–19, 28–31 |doi=10.24839/1092-0803.Eye10.3.18 |s2cid=31804468}}</ref> Concentrations of [[estrogen]] will influence where [[body fat]] is stored.<ref name=Collins/>

Before [[puberty]] both males and females have a similar [[waist–hip ratio]].<ref name="Singh"/> At puberty, a girl's sex hormones, mainly [[estrogen]], will promote breast development and a wider pelvis that is tilted forward, and until menopause a woman's estrogen levels will cause her body to store excess fat in the [[buttocks]], [[hips]] and [[thighs]],<ref name=Collins>{{cite web |last=Collins, Anne |url=http://www.annecollins.com/obesity/reduce-abdominal-fat.htm |title=Reduce Abdominal Fat |access-date=15 November 2008 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110928032733/http://www.annecollins.com/obesity/reduce-abdominal-fat.htm |archive-date=28 September 2011 }}</ref><ref name="Peeke2008" /> but generally not around her waist, which will remain about the same size as it was before puberty. These factors result in women's waist–hip ratio (WHR) being lower than for males, although males tend to have a greater upper-body to waist–hip ratio (WHR) giving them a V-shape look because of their greater muscle mass (e.g., they generally have much larger, more muscular and broader shoulders, [[pectoral muscles]], [[teres major muscles]] and [[latissimus dorsi muscles]]).

During and after pregnancy, a woman experiences body shape changes. After [[menopause]], with the reduced production of estrogen by the [[ovaries]], there is a tendency for fat to redistribute from a female's buttocks, hips and thighs to her waist or [[abdomen]].<ref name=fat/>

The breasts of girls and women in early stages of development commonly are "high" and rounded, dome- or cone-shaped, and protrude almost horizontally from a female's chest wall. Over time, the [[ptosis (breasts)|sag]] on breasts tends to increase due to their natural weight, the relaxation of support structures, and aging.

==Categorisation in fashion industry <span class="anchor" id="Female shapes"></span>==
Body shapes are often categorised in the fashion industry into one of four elementary geometric shapes,<ref name=shape_study>{{cite web |last=McCormack, Helen |url=https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/this-britain/the-shape-of-things-to-wear-scientists-identify-how-womens-figures-have-changed-in-50-years-516259.html |title=The shape of things to wear: scientists identify how women's figures have changed in 50 years |work=The Independent |location=UK |date=21 November 2005}} <br/>How female body shapes have changed over time.</ref> though there are very wide ranges of actual sizes within each shape:

'''Rectangular'''
: The waist is less than {{cvt|9|in|cm}} smaller than the hips and bust.<ref name=shape_study/> Body fat is distributed predominantly in the abdomen, buttocks, chest, and face. This overall fat distribution creates the typical ruler (straight) shape.
'''Inverted triangle'''
: The shoulders are broader than the hips.<ref name=shape_study/> The legs and thighs tend to be slim, while the chest looks larger compared with the rest of the body. Fat is mainly distributed in the chest and face.
'''Spoon'''
: The hips are wider than the bust.<ref name=shape_study/> The distribution of fat varies, with fat tending to deposit first in the buttocks, hips, and thighs. As body fat percentage increases, an increasing proportion of body fat is distributed around the waist and upper abdomen. The women of this body type tend to have a relatively larger rear, thicker thighs, and a small(er) bosom. Also known as a "pear" shape.
'''Hourglass'''
:{{main|Hourglass figure}}
:The hips and bust are almost of equal size, and the waist is narrower than both.<ref name=shape_study/> Body fat distribution tends to be around both the upper body and lower body.

A study of the shapes of over 6,000 women, carried out by researchers at the [[North Carolina State University]] circa 2005,<ref>{{cite web|last1=Istook |first1=C |last2=Simmons |first2=K |last3=Devarajan |first3=P |title=Female Figure Identification Technique (FFIT) for Apparel |url=http://www.iffti.com/downloads/past_conferences/HKPU,%202002/A/147-173.pdf |publisher=International Foundation of Fashion Technology institutes |access-date=11 November 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141112020558/http://www.iffti.com/downloads/past_conferences/HKPU%2C%202002/A/147-173.pdf |archive-date=12 November 2014}}</ref> for apparel, found that 46% were rectangular, just over 20% spoon, just under 14% inverted triangle, and 8% hourglass.<ref name=shape_study/> Another study has found "that the average woman's waistline had expanded by six inches since the 1950s" and that women in 2004 were taller and had bigger busts and hips than those of the 1950s.<ref name=shape_study/> Note, however, that a 2021 study found that slight changes in measurement placement definition can recategorise up to 40% of women into different body shapes, meaning cross-research comparisons may be flawed unless the exact measurement definitions are used.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Parker|first1=Christopher J.|last2=Hayes|first2=Steven George|last3=Brownbridge|first3=Kathryn|last4=Gill|first4=Simeon|date=12 April 2021|title=Assessing the female figure identification technique's reliability as a body shape classification system|url=https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00140139.2021.1902572|journal=Ergonomics|volume=64|issue=8|language=en|pages=1035–1051|doi=10.1080/00140139.2021.1902572|pmid=33719914|s2cid=232231822|issn=0014-0139|url-access=subscription}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|title=Why you might not be the body shape you think|url=https://medicalxpress.com/news/2021-05-body.html|access-date=18 May 2021|website=medicalxpress.com|language=en}}</ref>

=== FFIT for Apparel measurements ===
The "Female Figure Identification Technique for Apparel" uses the following formula to identify an individual's body type:<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Yim Lee |first1=Jeong |last2=Istook |first2=Cynthia L. |last3=Ja Nam |first3=Yun |last4=Mi Park |first4=Sun |title=Comparison of body shape between USA and Korean women |journal=International Journal of Clothing Science and Technology |date=1 January 2007 |volume=19 |issue=5 |pages=374–391 |doi=10.1108/09556220710819555 |url=https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/09556220710819555/full/html |issn=0955-6222|url-access=subscription }}</ref>
; Hourglass: If (bust − hips) ≤ {{cvt|1|in}} AND (hips − bust) < {{cvt|3.6|in}} AND ((bust − waist) ≥ {{cvt|9|in}} OR (hips − waist) ≥ {{cvt|10|in}})
; Bottom hourglass: If (hips − bust) ≥ {{cvt|3.6|in}} AND (hips − bust) < {{cvt|10|in}} AND (hips − waist) ≥ {{cvt|9|in}} AND (high hip/waist) < 1.193
; Top hourglass: If (bust − hips) > {{cvt|1|in}} AND (bust − hips) < {{cvt|10|in}} AND (bust − waist) ≥ {{cvt|9|in}}
; Spoon: If (hips − bust) > {{cvt|2|in}} AND (hips − waist) ≥ {{cvt|7|in}} AND (high hip/waist) ≥ 1.193
; Triangle: If (hips − bust) ≥ {{cvt|3.6|in}} AND (hips − waist) < {{cvt|9|in}}
; Inverted triangle: If (bust − hips) ≥ {{cvt|3.6|in}} AND (bust − waist) < {{cvt|9|in}}
; Rectangle: If (hips − bust) < {{cvt|3.6|in}} AND (bust − hips) < {{cvt|3.6|in}} AND (bust − waist) < {{cvt|9|in}} AND (hips − waist) < {{cvt|10|in}}

The [[circumference]]s of bust, waist, and hips ([[Bust/waist/hip measurements|BWH]]) and the ratios between them are a widespread method of identifying different female body shapes. As noted above, descriptive terms used include "rectangle", "spoon", "inverted triangle", and "hourglass".<ref name=shape_study/>

The waist is typically smaller than the bust and hips, unless there is a high proportion of [[body fat]] distributed around it. How much the bust or hips inflect inward, towards the waist, determines a woman's structural shape. The hourglass shape is present in only about 8% of women.<ref name="shape_study"/>

A woman's dimensions are often expressed by the [[circumference]] around the three inflection points. For example, "36–29–38" in [[US customary units]] would mean a {{cvt|36|in|cm}} bust, {{cvt|29|in|cm}} waist and {{cvt|38|in|cm}} hips.

Height will also affect the appearance of the figure. A woman who is 36–24–36 (91–61–91&nbsp;cm) at {{cvt|5|ft|2|in|m|2}} height will look different from a woman who is 36–24–36 at {{cvt|5|ft|8|in|m|2}} height. If both are the same weight, the taller woman has a much lower [[body mass index]]; if they have the same BMI, the weight is distributed around a greater volume.

A woman's bust measure is a combination of her rib cage and breast size. For convenience, a woman's [[Brassiere measurements|bra measurements]] are often used as a proxy. Conventionally, measurement for the band of a bra is taken around the torso immediately below the breasts, with the tape measure parallel to the floor.<ref name="barenecessitiessizing">{{cite web|title=Find Your Bra Size|url=http://www.barenecessities.com/fit_sizing.aspx|work=Bare Necessities|access-date=12 March 2011}}</ref><ref name="womenshealthsizing">{{cite web|title=How to Measure Bra Size |url=http://www.womenshealthmag.com/beauty-and-style/bra-size |work=Women's Health Magazine (online) |access-date=12 March 2011 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110225011832/http://www.womenshealthmag.com/beauty-and-style/bra-size |archive-date=25 February 2011}}</ref> Bra [[cup size]] is determined by measuring across the crest of the breasts and calculating the difference between that measurement and the band measurement.<ref name="barenecessitiessizing"/><ref name=ytherroom>{{cite AV media |title=How to Measure for Bra |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5_h5-Ov5fm8 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130224173429/http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5_h5-Ov5fm8 |archive-date=24 February 2013 |publisher=HerRoom.com |last=Edmark, Tomima |access-date=23 January 2011}}</ref> The waist is measured at the midpoint between the bottom of the [[rib cage]] and the top of the [[Iliac crest|'front' hip bones]]. The hips are measured at the largest circumference of the hips and buttocks.<ref name=whoreport>{{cite web |url=http://whqlibdoc.who.int/publications/2011/9789241501491_eng.pdf |title=Waist Circumference and Waist–Hip Ratio, Report of a WHO Expert Consultation |date=8–11 December 2008 | publisher=World Health Organization |access-date=21 March 2012}}</ref>

According to [[Camille Paglia]], the ideal body type as envisioned by members of society has changed throughout history. She states that Stone Age [[Venus figurines]] show the earliest body type preference, dramatic [[steatopygia]]; and that the emphasis on protruding belly, breasts, and buttocks is likely a result of both the aesthetic of being well fed and aesthetic of being fertile, traits that were more difficult to achieve at the time. In sculptures from [[Classical Greece]] and [[Ancient Rome]] the female bodies are more tubular and regularly proportioned.<ref name=cam>{{cite journal|last=Paglia|first=Camille|title=The Cruel Mirror|journal=Art Documentation|volume=23|pages=4–7}}</ref>{{rp|5}} There is essentially no emphasis given to any particular body part, not the breasts, buttocks, or belly.

Moving forward there is more evidence that fashion somewhat dictated what people believed were the proper female [[body proportions]]. This is the case because the body is primarily seen through clothing, which always changes the way the underlying structures are conceived.<ref name=anne/>{{rp|xii-xiii}} The first representations of truly fashionable women appear in the 14th century.<ref name=anne/>{{rp|90}} Between the 14th and 16th centuries in northern Europe, bulging bellies were again desirable; however, the stature of the rest of the figure was generally thin. This is most easily visible in paintings of nudes from the time. When looking at clothed images, the belly is often visible through a mass of otherwise concealing, billowing, loose robes. Since the stomach was the only visible anatomical feature, it became exaggerated in nude depictions while the rest of the body remained minimal.<ref name=anne>{{cite book|last=Hollander|first=Anne|title=Seeing Through Clothes|date=1993|publisher=University of California Press|location=California|pages=83–156}}</ref>{{rp|96–100, 106}} In southern Europe, around the time of the [[renaissance]], this was also true. Though the classical aesthetic was being revived and very closely studied, the art produced in the time period was influenced by both factors. This resulted in a beauty standard that reconciled the two aesthetics by using classically proportioned figures who had non-classical amounts of flesh and soft, padded skin.<ref name=anne/>{{rp|96–98,104}}

In the nude paintings of the 17th century, such as those by [[Rubens]], the naked women appear quite plump. Upon closer inspection, however, most of the women have fairly normal statures, Rubens has simply painted their flesh with rolls and ripples that otherwise would not be there. This may be a reflection of the [[1600–50 in Western European fashion#Women's fashions|female style of the day]]: a long, cylindrical, gown with rippling satin accents, tailored over a figure in [[History of corsets|stays]]. Thus Rubens' women have a tubular body with rippling embellishments.<ref name=anne/>{{rp|106, 316}} While stays continued to be fashionable into the [[1750–75 in Western fashion#Women's fashion|18th century]], they were shortened, became more conical, and consequently began to emphasize the waist. It also lifted and separated the breasts as opposed to the 17th century corsets which compressed and minimized the breasts. Consequently, depictions of nude women in the 18th century tend to have a very narrow waist and high, distinct breasts, almost as if they were wearing an invisible corset.<ref name=anne/>{{rp|91, 112–116}} [[La maja desnuda]] is a clear example of this aesthetic. The [[Victorian fashion#Ladies' fashions|19th century]] maintained the general figure of the 18th century. Examples can be seen in the works of many contemporary artists, both academic artists, such as [[Cabanel]], [[Ingres]], and [[Bouguereau]], and [[Impressionists]], such as [[Degas]], [[Renoir]], and [[Toulouse-Lautrec]]. As the 20th century began, the rise of athletics resulted in a drastic slimming of the female figure. This culminated in the 1920s [[flapper]] look, which has informed modern fashion ever since.<ref name=cam/>{{rp|4}}<ref name=anne/>{{rp|152}}

The last 100 years envelop the time period in which that overall body type has been seen as attractive, though there have been small changes within the period as well. The [[1920s in Western fashion#Women's wear|1920s]] was the time in which the overall silhouette of the ideal body slimmed down. There was dramatic flattening of the entire body resulting in a more youthful aesthetic.<ref name=anne/>{{rp|150–153}} As the century progressed, the ideal size of both the breasts and buttocks increased. From the [[1950s in Western fashion|1950s to 1960]] that trend continued with the interesting twist of cone shaped breasts as a result of the popularity of the [[bullet bra]]. In the [[1960s in fashion|1960s]], the invention of the [[miniskirt]] as well as the increased acceptability of pants for women, prompted the idealization of the long leg that has lasted to this day.<ref name=anne/>{{rp|93–95}} Following the invention of the [[push-up bra]] in the [[1970s in fashion|1970s]] the ideal breast has been a rounded, fuller, and larger breast. In the past 20 years the average American bra size has increased from 34B to 34DD,<ref>{{cite news|last=Dicker|first=Ron|title=American Bra Size Average Increases From 34B to 34DD in Just 20 Years, Survey Says|url=http://www.huffingtonpost.com/2013/07/24/bra-size-survey_n_3645267.html|access-date=14 April 2014|newspaper=HuffPost|date=24 July 2013}}</ref> although this may be due to the increase in obesity within the United States in recent years. Additionally, the ideal figure has favored an ever-lower [[waist–hip ratio]], especially with the advent and progression of digital editing software such as [[Adobe Photoshop]].<ref name=cam/>{{rp|4, 6–7}}

==Social and health issues==

Each society develops a general perception of what an ideal female body shape would be like. These ideals are generally reflected in the art and literature produced by or for a society, as well as in [[Media depictions of body shape|popular media]] such as films and magazines. The ideal or preferred female body size and shape has varied over time and continues to vary among cultures;<ref>{{cite web |publisher=physorg.com |url=http://www.physorg.com/news112367616.html |title=Ideal weight varies across cultures, but body image dissatisfaction pervades |date=23 October 2007}}</ref><ref>Rob Kemp. ''Vulture''.</ref> but a preference for a small waist has remained fairly constant throughout history.<ref name=Singhstudy>{{cite web| url=http://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/health/6247625.stm |title=Slim waist holds sway in history |work=BBC News |date=10 January 2007}}</ref> A low waist–hip ratio has often been seen as a sign of good health and reproductive potential.<ref>{{cite web |last=Khamsi |first=Roxanne |title=The hourglass figure is truly timeless |publisher=NewScientist.com news service |date=10 January 2007 |url=https://www.newscientist.com/article/dn10927}}</ref>

A low waist–hip ratio has also often been regarded as an indicator of attractiveness of a woman, but recent research suggests that attractiveness is more correlated to body mass index than waist–hip ratio, contrary to previous belief.<ref>{{cite journal|author1=Tovee M.J. |author2=Maisey D.S. |author3=Emery J.L. |author4= Cornelissen P.L. |title=Visual cues to female physical attractiveness |journal=Proc Biol Sci |date=22 January 1999 |volume=266 |pages=211–18 |pmid=10097394|issue=1415|doi=10.1098/rspb.1999.0624|pmc=1689653}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal|title=The relative contributions of waist-to-hip ratio and body mass index to judgments of attractiveness – Sexualities, Evolution & Gender |journal=Sexualities, Evolution & Gender |volume=7|date=3 December 2005 |pages=245–67| doi= 10.1080/14616660500238769 |issue=3|last1=Wilson |first1=Jan M. He notes that "The finding that the writers describe a small waist as beautiful suggests instead that this body part—a known marker of health and fertility—is a core feature of feminine beauty that transcends ethnic differences and cultures."<ref name=Singhstudy/>

===Waist-to-height ratio===
{{main|Waist-to-height ratio}}

A person's "waist-to-height ratio" (WHtR), is defined as their waist circumference divided by their height, both measured in the same units. It is used as a predictor of obesity-related cardiovascular disease. The WHtR is a measure of the distribution of [[body fat]]. Higher values of WHtR indicate higher risk of obesity-related cardiovascular diseases; it is correlated with [[abdominal obesity]].<ref>{{cite journal | vauthors = Lee CM, Huxley RR, Wildman RP, Woodward M | title = Indices of abdominal obesity are better discriminators of cardiovascular risk factors than BMI: a meta-analysis | journal = Journal of Clinical Epidemiology | volume = 61 | issue = 7 | pages = 646–653 | date = July 2008 | pmid = 18359190 | doi = 10.1016/j.jclinepi.2007.08.012 | doi-access = free }}</ref> In September 2022, the UK's [[National Institute for Health and Care Excellence]] (a government body) announced new guidelines which suggested that all adults "ensure their waist size is less than half their height in order to help stave off serious health problems".<ref>{{cite news |title=Ensure waist size is less than half your height, health watchdog says |work=[[The Guardian]] | url=https://www.theguardian.com/society/2022/apr/08/ensure-waist-size-is-less-than-half-your-height-health-watchdog-says | date=8 April 2022 | access-date=8 April 2022 | last = Gregory |first=Andrew }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title= Obesity: identification, assessment and management {{!}} Clinical guideline [CG189] | date=8 September 2022 |publisher=National Institute for Health and Care Excellence | url=https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg189 }} Recommendations 1.2.11 and 1.2.12</ref> This guideline is independent of gender.

==Bodies as identity==
Over the past several hundred years, there has been a shift towards viewing the body as part of one's identity – not in a purely physical way, but as a means of deeper self-expression. David Gauntlett, in his 2008 book, recognizes the importance of malleability in physical identity, stating, "the body is the outer expression of our self, to be improved and worked upon".<ref>[[#Gauntlett|Gauntlett]], p. 113</ref> One of the more key factors in creating the desire for a particular body shape – most notably for women – is the media, which has promoted a number of so-called "ideal" body shapes.<ref>{{cite book|last=Holland|first=Samantha|title=Alternative femininities: body, age and identity|url=https://archive.org/details/alternativefemin0000holl|url-access=registration|year=2004|publisher=Berg Publishers, Ltd.|location=New York City}}</ref> Fashionable figures are often unrealistic and unattainable for much of the population, and their popularity tends to be short-lived due to their arbitrary nature.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Ngo |first=Nealie Tan |date=1 October 2019 |title=What Historical Ideals of Women's Shapes Teach Us About Women's Self-Perception and Body Decisions Today |url=https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/what-historical-ideals-womens-shapes-teach-us-about-womens-self-perception-and-body-decisions-today/2019-10 |journal=AMA Journal of Ethics |language=en |volume=21 |issue=10 |pages=879–901 |doi=10.1001/amajethics.2019.879 |issn=2376-6980|doi-access=free |pmid=31651388 }}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |author-link=Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health |date=7 October 2022 |title=Unrealistic beauty standards cost U.S. economy billions each year |url=https://www.hsph.harvard.edu/news/hsph-in-the-news/unrealistic-beauty-standards-cost-u-s-economy-billions-each-year/ |access-date=10 March 2024 |website=News |language=en-us}}</ref>
[[File:Marilyn Monroe postcard.JPG|thumb|Postcard photo of the curvaceous Marilyn Monroe, taken before she became famous]]
During the 1950s, the fashion model and celebrity were two separate entities, allowing the body image of the time to be shaped more by television and film rather than high fashion advertisements. While the fashion model of the 1950s, such as [[Jean Patchett]] and [[Dovima]], were very thin, the ideal image of beauty was still a larger one. As the fashion houses in the early 1950s still catered to a specific, elite clientele, the image of the fashion model at that time was not as sought after or looked up to as was the image of the celebrity. While the models that graced the covers of [[Vogue Magazine]] and [[Harper's Bazaar]] in the 1950s were in line with the thin ideal of the day, the most prominent female icon was [[Marilyn Monroe]]. Monroe, who was more curvaceous, fell on the opposite end of the feminine ideal spectrum in comparison to high fashion models. Regardless of their sizes, however, both fashion of the time and depictions of Monroe emphasize a smaller waist and fuller bottom half. The late 1950s, however, brought about the rise of ready-to-wear fashion, which implemented a standardized sizing system for all mass-produced clothing. While fashion houses, such as [[Dior]] and [[Chanel]], remained true to their couture, tailor-made garments, the rise of these rapidly-produced, standardized garments led to a shift in location from Europe to America as the epicenter of fashion. Along with that shift came the standardization of sizes, in which garments were not made to fit the body anymore, but instead the body must be altered to fit the garment.<ref>{{Cite journal | doi = 10.1002/eat.20039| pmid = 15478132| title = No longer just a pretty face: Fashion magazines' depictions of ideal female beauty from 1959 to 1999| journal = International Journal of Eating Disorders| volume = 36| issue = 3| pages = 342–7| year = 2004| last1 = Sypeck | first1 = M. F. | last2 = Gray | first2 = J. J. | last3 = Ahrens | first3 = A. H. }}</ref>

During the 1960s, the popularity of the model [[Twiggy]] meant that women favoured a thinner body, with long, slender limbs.<ref>[[#MacDonald|MacDonald]]</ref> This was a drastic change from the former decade's ideal, which saw curvier icons, such as Marilyn Monroe, to be considered the epitome of beautiful. These shifts in what was seen to be the "fashionable body" at the time followed no logical pattern, and the changes occurred so quickly that one shape was never in vogue for more than a decade. As is the case with fashion itself in the post-modern world, the premise of the ever-evolving "ideal" shape relies on the fact that it will soon become obsolete, and thus must continue changing to prevent itself from becoming uninteresting.<ref>{{cite book|last=Miles|first=Steven|title=Consumerism as a Way of Life|year=1998|publisher=SAGE Publications Ltd.|location=London}}</ref>

An early example of the body used as an identity marker occurred in the [[Victorian era]], when women wore [[corset]]s to help themselves attain the body they wished to possess.<ref name="Bovey1994">{{cite book|last=Bovey|first=Shelley|title=The Forbidden Body|url=https://archive.org/details/forbiddenbodywhy0000bove|url-access=registration|year=1994|publisher=Pandora Press|location=Glasgow|isbn=978-0-04-440871-0}}</ref> Having a tiny waist was a sign of social status, as the wealthier women could afford to dress more extravagantly and sport items such as corsets to increase their [[physical attractiveness]].<ref>{{cite news|last=Barford|first=Vanessa|title=The re-re-re-rise of the corset|url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/magazine-18409752|publisher=BBC|access-date=3 November 2012|work=BBC News|date=19 June 2012}}</ref> By the 1920s, the cultural ideal had changed significantly as a result of the [[suffrage movement]], and "the fashion was for cropped hair, [[Breast binding|flat (bound) breasts]] and a slim, androgynous shape".<ref name="Bovey1994" />{{rp|176}}

More recently, magazines and other popular media have been criticized for promoting an unrealistic trend of thinness. David Gauntlett states that the media's "repetitive celebration of a beauty 'ideal' which most women will not be able to match … will eat up readers' time and money—and perhaps good health—if they try".<ref>[[#Gauntlett|Gauntlett]], p. 200</ref> Additionally, the impact that this has on women and their self-esteem is often a very negative one,<ref>[[#Gauntlett|Gauntlett]], p. 201</ref> and resulted in the diet industry taking off in the 1960s – something that would not have occurred "had bodily appearance not been so closely associated with identity for women".<ref>[[#MacDonald|MacDonald]], p. 201</ref> Melissa Oldman states, "Nowhere is the thin female ideal more evident than in popular media."<ref>{{cite web |last= Oldham |first= Melissa |title= How the media shapes our cultural ideals of body shape|year=2017 |publisher= Culture Matters |url= http://culturematters.org.uk/index.php/culture/tv/item/2524-how-the-media-shapes-our-cultural-ideals-of-body-shape}}</ref>

The importance of "the body as a work zone", as Myra MacDonald asserts, further perpetuates the link between fashion and identity, with the body being used as a means of creating a visible and unavoidable image for oneself.<ref>[[#MacDonald|MacDonald]], p. 202</ref> The tools with which to create the final copy of such a project range from the extreme—plastic surgery—to the more tame, such as diet and exercise.<ref>[[#Gauntlett|Gauntlett]]</ref>

==Alteration of body shape==
{{See also|Body image}}
A study at [[Brigham Young University]] using MRI technology suggested that women experience more anxiety about weight gain than do men,<ref>{{cite web |url=http://news.byu.edu/archive10-apr-bodyimage.aspx |title=Fear of getting fat seen in healthy women's brain scans |date=13 April 2010 |access-date=17 May 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130513215424/http://news.byu.edu/archive10-apr-bodyimage.aspx |archive-date=13 May 2013 }}</ref> while aggregated research has been used to claim that images of thin women in popular media may induce psychological stress.<ref>{{cite news|title=Skinny celebs a health hazard|url=http://www.smh.com.au/lifestyle/wellbeing/skinny-celebs-a-health-hazard-20101020-16t6h.html |work=The Sydney Morning Herald|date=20 October 2010 |access-date=20 October 2010}}</ref> A study of 52 older adults found that females may think more about their body shape and endorse thinner figures than men even into old age.<ref name="Ferraro FR et al.">{{cite journal |date=October 2008|title=Aging, Body Image, and Body Shape |journal=Journal of General Psychology|volume= 135|issue= 4|pages=379–392, 14p |pmid=18959228|doi=10.3200/GENP.135.4.379-392|last1=Ferraro |first1=F. Richard |last2=Muehlenkamp |first2=Jennifer J |last3=Paintner |first3=Ashley |last4=Wasson |first4=Kayla |last5=Hager |first5=Tracy |last6=Hoverson |first6=Fallon|s2cid=37023505 }}</ref>

Various strategies, including exercise, are sometimes employed in an attempt to temporarily or permanently alter the shape of a body. [[Dieting]] is also sometimes used, but is generally not effective in the long term.<ref name="Mann2007">{{cite journal | vauthors = Mann T, Tomiyama AJ, Westling E, Lew AM, Samuels B, Chatman J | title = Medicare's search for effective obesity treatments: diets are not the answer | journal = The American Psychologist | volume = 62 | issue = 3 | pages = 220–233 | date = April 2007 | pmid = 17469900 | doi = 10.1037/0003-066x.62.3.220 | quote = In sum, there is little support for the notion that diets ["severely restricting one's calorie intake"] lead to lasting weight loss or health benefits. | citeseerx = 10.1.1.666.7484 }}</ref>

At times artificial devices are used or surgery is employed. In 2019, 92% of all cosmetic procedures in the US were undertaken by women, with the most popular being a breast augmentation.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Plastic Surgery Statistics|url=https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/plastic-surgery-statistics|access-date=14 April 2021|website=American Society of Plastic Surgeons|language=en}}</ref> Breast size can be artificially increased or decreased. [[Falsies]], [[breast prostheses]] or padded [[brassiere|bras]] may be used to increase the apparent size of a woman's [[breast]]s, while minimiser bras may be used to reduce the apparent size. Breasts can be surgically enlarged using [[breast implant]]s or reduced by the [[breast reduction plasty|systematic removal of parts of the breasts]]. Hormonal breast enhancement may be another option.<ref name="GöretzlehnerLauritzen2012">{{cite book|author1=Gunther Göretzlehner|author2=Christian Lauritzen|author3=Thomas Römer|author4=Winfried Rossmanith|title=Praktische Hormontherapie in der Gynäkologie|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=TIs2WhfYzZ4C&pg=PA385|year=2012|publisher=Walter de Gruyter|isbn=978-3-11-024568-4|pages=385–}}</ref><ref name="ManselFodstad2007">{{cite book|author1=R.E. Mansel|author2=Oystein Fodstad|author3=Wen G. Jiang|title=Metastasis of Breast Cancer|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=14pb5b6gT-oC&pg=PA217|year=2007|publisher=Springer Science & Business Media|isbn=978-1-4020-5866-0|pages=217–}}</ref><ref name="pmid9610425">{{cite journal | vauthors = Hartmann BW, Laml T, Kirchengast S, Albrecht AE, Huber JC | title = Hormonal breast augmentation: prognostic relevance of insulin-like growth factor-I | journal = Gynecol. Endocrinol. | volume = 12 | issue = 2 | pages = 123–27 | year = 1998 | pmid = 9610425 | doi = 10.3109/09513599809024960}}</ref>

Historically, boned [[corset]]s have been used to reduce waist sizes. The corset reached its climax during the [[Victorian era]]. In twentieth century these corsets were mostly replaced with more flexible/comfortable [[foundation garment]]s. Where corsets are used for waist reduction, they may cause temporary reduction through occasional use or permanent reduction through constant and continuous use. Those who use corsets for permanent reduction are often referred to as [[Tightlacing|tightlacers]]. [[Liposuction]] and [[liposculpture]] are common surgical methods for reducing the waist line.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Liposuction NYC {{!}} Mount Sinai - New York |url=https://www.mountsinai.org/care/surgery/services/plastic-surgery/aesthetic/body-contouring/liposuction |access-date=10 March 2024 |website=Mount Sinai Health System |language=en-US}}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Zhang |first1=Yi Xin |last2=Lazzeri |first2=Davide |last3=Grassetti |first3=Luca |last4=Silvestri |first4=Alessandro |last5=Trisliana Perdanasari |first5=Aurelia |last6=Han |first6=Sheng |last7=Torresetti |first7=Matteo |last8=di Benedetto |first8=Giovanni |last9=Castello |first9=Manuel Francisco |date=6 February 2015 |title=Three-dimensional Superficial Liposculpture of the Hips, Flank, and Thighs |journal=Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery Global Open |volume=3 |issue=1 |pages=e291 |doi=10.1097/GOX.0000000000000241 |issn=2169-7574 |pmc=4323395 |pmid=25674372}}</ref>

Padded control briefs or [[hip and buttock padding]] may be used to increase the apparent size of hips and buttocks. [[Buttock augmentation]] surgery may be used to increase the size of hips and buttocks to make them look more rounded.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Buttock Enhancement Procedures NYC {{!}} Mount Sinai - New York |url=https://www.mountsinai.org/care/surgery/services/plastic-surgery/aesthetic/buttock-enhancement |access-date=10 March 2024 |website=Mount Sinai Health System |language=en-US}}</ref>

==Social perceptions of the ideal woman's body==
In a 2012 experiment, researchers Crossley, Cornelissen, and Tovée asked men and women to depict an attractive female body, and the majority of them chose the same ideal.<ref name=Crossley>{{cite journal | journal= PLOS ONE |volume=7 |number=11 |date=29 November 2012 |doi=10.1371/journal.pone.0050601 |title=What Is an Attractive Body? Using an Interactive 3D Program to Create the Ideal Body for You and Your Partner |first1=Kara L. |last1=Crossley |first2=Piers L. |last2=Cornelissen |first3=Martin J. |last3=Tovée|article-number=e50601 |pmid=23209791 |pmc=3510069 |bibcode=2012PLoSO...750601C |doi-access=free }}</ref> The women who participated in this experiment drew their ideal bodies with enlarged busts and narrowed the rest of their bodies. The male participants also depicted their ideal partner with the same image. The researchers state, "For both sexes, the primary predictor of female beauty is a relatively low BMI combined with a relatively curvaceous body".<ref name=Crossley/> However, the generality of their conclusions was limited given their small sample size and single ethnicity of participants.
