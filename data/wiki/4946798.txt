'''''Alligator''''' is a 1980 American [[horror film|horror]] [[thriller film]] directed by [[Lewis Teague]] from a screenplay by [[John Sayles]], and starring [[Robert Forster]], [[Robin Riker]], [[Michael V. Gazzo]], and [[Dean Jagger]]. It also features appearances by [[Jack Carter (comedian)|Jack Carter]], [[Sydney Lassick]], [[Perry Lang]], [[Angel Tompkins]], [[Henry Silva]] and [[Sue Lyon]] in her last screen role. The film follows a police officer and a reptile expert who track an enormous, ravenous [[Man-eating animal#Alligators|man-eating]] [[alligator]] flushed down the toilet years earlier, that is attacking residents after escaping from the city's [[Sewerage|sewers]].

The film was produced [[Independent film|independently]] and released by Group 1 Films on November 14, 1980. While a box office disappointment in North America, ''Alligator'' proved more successful in other territories. It received positive reviews from critics, and has developed a [[Cult film|cult following]]. A [[direct-to-video]] sequel, ''[[Alligator II: The Mutation]],'' was released in 1991.

==Plot==
In 1968, a teenage girl purchases a baby [[American alligator]] while on vacation with her family at a [[tourist trap]] in [[Florida]] and gives it the name Ramon. When the family returns home to [[Missouri]],<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |last=Lê |first=Paul |date=2021-11-29 |title=It came from the sewers: Lewis Teague’s ‘Alligator’ turns 40 |url=https://talesfromthepaulside.com/2021/11/29/alligator-1980-lewis-teague-retrospective/ |access-date=2026-06-30 |website=Tales from the Paulside |language=en |quote="...all signs (literal ones at that) suggest the story takes place in St. Louis, Missouri."}}</ref><ref name=":1">{{Cite web |title=Alligator - Now Playing In Theater at Metrograph |url=https://metrograph.com/film/?vista_film_id=9999003995 |access-date=2026-06-30 |website=Metrograph |language=en-US |quote="...follows a Missouri family’s decision, fresh from a Florida vacation, to dispose of their new baby pet alligator by way of the toilet."}}</ref><!-- The film's locale is not named on-screen, but various Missouri license plates and state flags appear throughout the film. --> the girl's surly, animal-phobic father promptly flushes it down the toilet and into the city's [[Sanitary sewer|sewers]].

The baby alligator survives by feeding on the discarded carcasses of pet animals (mostly dogs) used in illegal experimentation and dumped into the sewers. These animals had been used as test subjects for an experimental growth formula intended to increase agricultural livestock meat production. However, the project was abandoned because while the formula had the desired effect of making the animals larger than normal, it had the unwanted side effect of massively increasing the animals' [[metabolism]], causing them to develop an insatiable appetite. During the 12 years spanning 1968 and 1980, the baby alligator [[bioaccumulation|bioaccumulates]] concentrated amounts of this formula from feeding on these carcasses, causing it to mutate and grow into a perpetually ravenous {{convert|36|foot|m}} monster with an almost-impenetrable hide.

The alligator begins ambushing and devouring sewer workers. The resulting discovery of body parts exiting the sewers draws in world-weary police [[detective]] David Madison who, after a horribly botched case in [[St. Louis]], has gained a reputation for being lethally unlucky for his assigned partners. As David works on the case, his boss Chief Clark introduces him to [[herpetology|herpetologist]] Marisa Kendall. Kendall was in fact the teenager who bought the baby alligator in 1968. The two of them edge into a prickly romantic relationship, and during a visit to Kendall’s house, Madison bonds with her motor-mouthed mother, Madeline.

Madison's reputation as a partner-killer is confirmed when the alligator devours a young officer named Kelly who accompanies Madison into the sewer searching for clues. No one believes Madison’s story due to a lack of a body, and partly because of Slade, the influential local [[Business magnate|tycoon]] who sponsored the illegal experiments and therefore wants the truth concealed. This changes when obnoxious [[Tabloid (newspaper format)|tabloid]] reporter Thomas Kemp, one of the banes of Madison’s existence, goes snooping in the sewers and procures graphic and indisputable photographic evidence of the beast at the involuntary cost of his life. The story quickly garners public attention, and a citywide hunt for the monster is called for.

An attempt by the police to flush out the alligator proves unsuccessful, forcing the alligator to escape from the sewers and come to the surface, where it first kills a police officer and later a young boy who, during a party, is tossed into a swimming pool in which the alligator sought rest. When Madison makes the connection between the alligator and the illegal growth formula experiments, Slade uses his influence with the [[mayor]] to get Madison suspended from the force.

The ensuing hunt continues, including the hiring of pompous [[Big-game hunting|big-game hunter]] Colonel Brock to track the animal. Once again, the effort fails: Brock is devoured, and the police trip over each other confusedly. The alligator goes on a rampage through a high-society [[wedding]] hosted at Slade's mansion, eating alive a female [[Waiting staff|waiter]] serving guests at the [[Wedding reception|reception]] and killing Slade, the mayor, and Slade's chief scientist for the hormone experiments, who was also his intended son-in-law. Madison and Kendall finally lure the reptile back into the sewers before setting off explosives, killing it. As they walk away, a drainpipe into the sewer spits out another baby alligator, thus potentially repeating the cycle all over again.

==Background==
''Alligator'', along with films such as ''[[Grizzly (film)|Grizzly]]'' (1976), ''[[Orca (1977 film)|Orca]]'' (1977) and ''[[Piranha (1978 film)|Piranha]]'' (1978), is sometimes seen as made to capitalize on the success of the film ''[[Jaws (film)|Jaws]]'' (1975), whose main antagonist is a man-eating [[great white shark]]. John Sayles, who wrote the script for ''Alligator'', also created the screenplay for ''Piranha'' two years earlier.<ref name="geek" />

==Production==
===Development===
The film was first announced by producer Brandon Chase in 1976.<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=West Bank Guide|date=28 July 1976|page=39|title=The quote 'em pole}}</ref> Chase was inspired by the urban legend of [[Sewer alligator|alligators living in sewers]], in particular an account of alligators being found in New York City. In 1977 he announced the film would be set in Wisconsin and made for $1.8 million in Georgia, of which $100,000 was allocated for the alligator. Chase had enjoyed success with ''[[The Giant Spider Invasion]]''. "Audiences today are looking for something different," he said. "Huge things coming from outer space or popping up from sewers are different."<ref name="los">{{cite news|newspaper=The Los Angeles Times|date=10 January 1977|page=57|title=More terror coming up}}</ref>

Chase signed [[Lewis Teague]] to direct. Teague later recalled:
<blockquote>We built a thirty-six-foot alligator out of rubber with an aluminum frame that was articulated so the legs could move, and two very strong men with powerful legs who had to carry that weight got inside. One man had his legs in the front, the other in the rear, and they would walk. This wasn’t my idea; I had been very skeptical about it because I had heard about all the problems [[Steven Spielberg]] had with his giant rubber shark when he had made ''[[Jaws (film)|Jaws]]''. I knew the history of that film, and they only used the rubber shark in a few shots. For the most part, he had to create scary scenes from a certain point of view. So I had my doubts about this rubber alligator, but the producer had already started spending money to build it before I got hired to direct the film.<ref name="talk">{{cite web|url=https://filmtalk.org/2020/01/22/lewis-teague-the-job-of-a-director-on-the-set-to-a-large-degree-is-to-keep-everybodys-morale-up/|website=Film Talk|date=22 January 2020|title=Lewis Teague: “The job of a director on the set to a large degree is to keep everybody’s morale up” }}</ref></blockquote>
Teague had just made ''[[The Lady in Red (1979 film)|Lady in Red]]'' from a script by John Sayles. Teague disliked the script for ''Alligator'' and persuaded Chase to hire Sayles to rewrite it.<ref name="AligatorProd">{{cite magazine |last=Eisenberg|first=Adam|date=1980 |title= Alligator|url=https://archive.org/details/cinefantastique_1970-2002/Cinefantastique%20Vol%2010%20No%203%20%281980%29/page/n15/mode/1up?view=theater|magazine=[[Cinefantastique]]|location= |publisher= Fourth Castle Micromedia|access-date=August 10, 2023}}</ref> Sayles said "it wasn't a good script... The alligator lived in a sewer for the whole movie. It never got above ground."<ref>{{cite book|page=10| title=John Sayles : interviews|last1=Sayles|first1= John|year= 1999|last2=Carson|first2= Diane}}</ref>

Sayles said the original draft had the alligator grow from drinking from a brewery. "It never made sense why it was a giant alligator," he said. "They killed this alligator at an old abandoned sawmill. Someone had left the power on at the old abandoned sawmill. And someone had left a chainsaw lying around the old abandoned sawmill. They plugged the chainsaw in and threw it into the alligator's mouth. All the alligator's thrashing around didn't even pull the plug out, even as the chainsaw cut him to bits."<ref>Sayles & Carson p 61</ref> Sayles rewrote the whole script on a cross country flight from New York to Los Angeles.<ref>Sayles & Carson p 61</ref>

Sayles said, "My original idea was that the alligator eats its way through the whole socioeconomic system. It comes out of the sewer in the ghetto, then goes to a middle-class neighborhood, then out to the suburbs, and then to a real kind of high-rent area. That's still sort of there on screen, but they couldn't shoot so many scenes — which makes the alligator move so fast, that you don't get the progression."”<ref>Sayles & Carson p 10</ref>

Sayles said Robert Forster had input to his character. "He was having a hair problem and Lewis said, “I don’t know, I don’t think it looks that bad,” and Forster said, “What if I start working it in?” And I gave him a few lines about it. It gives the actors something to do apart from “Look!” “Watch out!” and “Duck!”"<ref>{{cite book|page=39|first=John|last=Sayles|title=Sayles on Sayles|year=1998}}</ref>

Teague later said he wanted to make the film "scary, but I sort of changed course right before we started shooting." This happened because when he tested the alligator "everybody burst into laughter. I thought, ‘This is a comedy; this isn’t going to be a scary film.’ So we’d better tell a funny story. John Sayles wrote a very witty script with a lot of funny lines. That was effective. I did try to put in a few very scary scenes, but they turned out to be not that very scary."<ref name="talk"/>

===Filming===
Though set in [[Missouri]],<ref name=":0" /><ref name=":1" /> location shooting took place in and around [[Los Angeles]], including in various service tunnels beneath the city streets.<ref name= "AligatorProd"/> In order to create the illusion of the giant alligator, a full scale model was created by Ben Stansbury along with more elaborate head and tail sections for specific action sequences.<ref name= "AligatorProd"/> The internal mechanics used to bring the Alligator to life were created by Richard Helmer who had previous worked on the shark in ''[[Jaws (film)|Jaws]]''.<ref name= "AligatorProd"/> Real alligators were also photographed on miniature sets or integrated with optical enlargement or mattes.<ref name= "AligatorProd"/>

Sayles said "there was a lot of good stuff I wrote that never got shot, whole subplots, because this alligator couldn't cut it. This alligator couldn't do the things they said it could. It couldn't go in the water, for instance."<ref>Sayles & Carson p 61</ref>

While commentary on the [[Lions Gate Entertainment]] DVD gives the location as Chicago, the police vehicles in the film appear to have [[Missouri]] license plates, and Missouri state flags also appear. When the young Marisa returns home with her family from their vacation in Florida, they pass a sign that reads "Welcome to Missouri". Later, the voice of a newscaster identifies Marisa as "a native of our city", implying the location is a city in Missouri other than St. Louis.<ref>{{cite web |first=Kevin |last=Mcgue |url=http://www.alifeatthemovies.com/alligator/ |title=Alligator Film Review |date=April 23, 2010 |work=A Life at the Movies |access-date=February 2, 2016 |archive-date=August 7, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210807144031/http://www.alifeatthemovies.com/alligator/ |url-status=dead }}</ref> Also the movie states multiple times it takes place in a city in Missouri other than St. Louis.

[[Bryan Cranston]] worked as a special-effects assistant on this film, in charge of making and rigging "the alligator guts" for the film's finale.<ref name="Cranston2016">{{cite book|last=Cranston|first=Bryan|title=A Life in Parts|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=_KMxDQAAQBAJ&pg=PA99|date=2016-10-11|publisher=Simon and Schuster|isbn=978-1-4767-9385-6|page=100}}</ref>. Cranston wrote in his memoir that he had favorable interactions with Forster, leading to Forster's role in season 5 of [[Breaking Bad]] 30 years later. 

Sayles said the film "isn't particularly scary... That alligator wasn't a very mobile creature. It was kind of like being afraid of a Sherman tank. Some of the stuff in the sewer is pretty well done and suspenseful, but only when the alligator eats the cop at the beginning is ''Alligator'' scary. That's because of the dangling feet."<ref>Sayles & Carson p 62</ref>

== Release ==
The film was premiered in Los Angeles on November 14, 1980.<ref name="afi" />

=== Home media ===
{{Unreferenced section|date=June 2016}}
On September 18, 2007, [[Lionsgate Studios|Lions Gate Entertainment]] released the film on DVD for the first time in the United States. The disc features a new 16x9 anamorphic widescreen transfer in the original 1.78:1 ratio and a new Dolby Digital 5.1-channel sound mix in addition to the original mono mix. The included extras are a commentary track with director Lewis Teague and star Robert Forster, a featurette titled ''Alligator Author'' in which screenwriter John Sayles discusses the differences between his original story and the final screenplay, and the original theatrical trailer.

The film had previously been available on DVD in other territories, including a version released in the United Kingdom in February 2003 by [[Anchor Bay Entertainment]]. This release features an optional DTS sound mix, includes the sequel ''Alligator II: The Mutation'' (1991) on a second disc, and includes the same Teague-Forster commentary found on the recent Lions Gate U.S. release. The [[Shout! Factory]] released a collectors edition of ''Alligator'' in the two-disc 4K Ultra HD and Blu-Ray set on February 22, 2022 under their Scream! Factory label.<ref>{{Cite AV media |url=https://www.blu-ray.com/movies/Alligator-4K-Blu-ray/306403/ |title=Alligator 4K Blu-ray (4K Ultra HD + Blu-ray) |language=en |access-date=2026-06-30 |via=www.blu-ray.com}}</ref>

==Reception==
===Box office===
The film did not perform particularly strongly at the box office in the USA and Canada but was a large hit in other territories. ABC paid $3 million for the film rights.<ref name="chase" /> Per ''Variety'', the film peaked at No. 11 for a week.

===Critical response===
On the [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], it holds an 87% approval rating based on 30 reviews, with an average of 6.8/10.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/alligator/|title=Alligator|website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]|publisher=[[Fandango Media]]|access-date=November 10, 2024}}</ref> On [[Metacritic]], the film has a [[weighted average]] score of 62 out of 100 based on 10 reviews, indicating "generally favorable reviews".<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/alligator|title=Alligator Reviews|website=[[Metacritic]]|publisher=[[Red Ventures]]|access-date=March 29, 2022}}</ref>

[[Vincent Canby]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' gave the film a mostly positive review, writing that its "suspense is frequently as genuine as its wit and its fond awareness of the clichés [it uses]".<ref>{{cite news|last1=Canby|first1=Vincent|title=The Screen: 'Alligator': Long in the Tooth|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1981/06/05/movies/alligator.html|date=June 5, 1981|work=[[The New York Times]]|issue=C12, Section 3, Page 12, Column 3|access-date=May 2, 2016}}</ref> The staff of ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' concluded: "Dumb as it is, director Lewis Teague brings some plusses to the pic. Robert Forster, as a detective, and Riker are amiable leads, never taking the film too seriously. Tech credits are cheap but serviceable."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://variety.com/1979/film/reviews/alligator-1117789184/|title=Alligator|date=December 31, 1979|website=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|access-date=March 29, 2022}}</ref> [[Roger Ebert]], writing for the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'', gave the film one out of four stars, suggesting that it would be best to "flush this movie down the toilet to see if it also grows into something big and fearsome."<ref>{{cite web|url=http://rogerebert.suntimes.com/apps/pbcs.dll/article?AID=/19801126/REVIEWS/11260301/1023|title=''Alligator''|last=Ebert|first=Roger|author-link=Roger Ebert|date=November 26, 1980|website=[[RogerEbert.com]]|access-date=April 7, 2011|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100330052900/http://rogerebert.suntimes.com/apps/pbcs.dll/article?AID=%2F19801126%2FREVIEWS%2F11260301%2F1023|archive-date=March 30, 2010|url-status=dead}}</ref>

In 2000, [[Kim Newman]] of ''[[Empire (film magazine)|Empire]]'' praised the perceived wit of the film's script, as well as the "solid performances, effective effects", and "spirited B-level direction".<ref>{{cite web|url=https://empireonline.com/movies/alligator/review/|title=Alligator Review|date=January 1, 2000|last=Newman|first=Kim|author-link=Kim Newman|website=[[Empire (magazine)|Empire]]|access-date=June 24, 2016}}</ref> In 2007, ''[[Entertainment Weekly]]''{{'}}s Chris Nashawaty called the film "Clever, funny, and wonderfully bloody," and wrote that "[this] B movie deserves an... 'A-{{' "}}.<ref>{{cite magazine|url=https://ew.com/article/2007/10/01/alligator-3/|title=Alligator|last=Nashawaty|first=Chris|date=October 1, 2007|magazine=[[Entertainment Weekly]]|access-date=March 29, 2022}}</ref> In 2013, Jim Knipfel of ''[[Den of Geek]]'' awarded the film four-and-a-half out of five stars, calling it "intelligent and stylish".<ref name=geek>{{cite web|url=https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/alligator-1980-lookback-and-review/|title=Alligator (1980): Lookback and Review|last=Knipfel|first=Jim|date=June 21, 2013|website=[[Den of Geek]]|access-date=June 24, 2016}}</ref> Film historian [[Leonard Maltin]] gave the film a score of three out of four stars, and wrote: "If you've got to make a film about a giant alligator that's terrorizing Chicago, this is the way to do it—with a sense of fun to balance the expected violence and genuine scares."<ref>{{cite book|last=Maltin|first=Leonard|author-link=Leonard Maltin|date=2017|title=Leonard Maltin's Movie Guide: The Modern Era, Previously Published as Leonard Maltin's 2015 Movie Guide|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=EC08DwAAQBAJ&pg=PA26|publisher=[[Plume (publisher)|Plume]]|page=26|isbn=978-0525536192}}</ref>

== Legacy ==
In an interview, filmmaker [[Quentin Tarantino]] said that Robert Forster's character, David Madison, inspired the character Max Cherry (also played by Forster) of his 1997 film ''[[Jackie Brown]]''.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Theater |first=New Beverly |title=Robert Forster: From Alligator to Jackie Brown |url=https://thenewbev.com/blog/2019/10/robert-forster-from-alligator-hunter-to-bail-bondsman/ |access-date=2025-01-18 |website=New Beverly Cinema - The premier revival theater in Los Angeles |language=en}}</ref>
