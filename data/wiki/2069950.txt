'''''The Celluloid Closet''''' is a 1996 American [[documentary film]] directed and co-written by [[Rob Epstein]] and [[Jeffrey Friedman (filmmaker)|Jeffrey Friedman]], and executive produced by [[Howard Rosenman]]. The film is based on [[Vito Russo]]'s 1981 book ''[[The Celluloid Closet: Homosexuality in the Movies]]'',<ref>{{cite news|last=Walters|first=Berry|date=March 10, 1996|title=Documenting Hollywood's homophobia|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-san-francisco-examiner/126301157/|newspaper=[[The San Francisco Examiner]]|page=B-9|access-date=June 12, 2023|via=[[Newspapers.com]]}}</ref><ref>{{cite news|last=Britton|first=Bonnie|date=May 24, 1996|title=Hollywood's gays step out of 'The Celluloid Closet'|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-indianapolis-news/126301278/|newspaper=[[The Indianapolis News]]|location=Indianapolis, Indiana|page=D-3|access-date=June 12, 2023|via=[[Newspapers.com]]}}</ref> and on lecture and film clip presentations he gave from 1972 to 1982. Russo had researched the history of how motion pictures, especially [[Hollywood (film industry)|Hollywood]] films, had portrayed [[gay]], [[lesbian]], [[bisexuality|bisexual]], and [[transgender]] characters.

''The Celluloid Closet'' was given a limited release in select theatres, including the [[Castro Theatre]] in San Francisco in April 1996, and then shown on cable channel [[Home Box Office|HBO]] as part of its series ''[[America Undercover]]''.

==Overview==
The documentary interviews various men and women connected to the Hollywood industry to comment on various film clips and their own personal experiences with the treatment of LGBT characters in film. From the [[sissy]] characters to the censorship of the Hollywood [[Production Code]], the coded gay characters and cruel stereotypes to the changes made in the early 1990s.

Vito Russo wanted his book to be transformed into a documentary film and helped out on the project until he died in 1990. Some critics of the documentary noted that it was less political than the book and ended on a more positive note. However, Russo had wanted the documentary to be entertaining and to reflect the positive changes that had occurred up to 1990.

==Production==
Russo approached Epstein about making a film version of ''The Celluloid Closet'' and wrote a proposal for the film version in 1986.<ref name="Telling">{{cite web |url=http://www.tellingpictures.com/films/cc/cc_hist.html |title=Telling Pictures: The Celluloid Closet |work=tellingpictures.com |year=2004 |access-date=19 August 2013 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080613201053/http://www.tellingpictures.com/films/cc/cc_hist.html |archive-date=13 June 2008 }}</ref> However, it was not until Russo died in 1990 that Epstein and Friedman gained any traction on the project. After his death, [[Channel 4]] in England approached the filmmakers about the film, and offered development funding in order to write a treatment "and most importantly to determine if it would even be possible to obtain the film clips from studios."<ref name="Telling"/>

After developing the project for years, fundraising remained the biggest obstacle. [[Lily Tomlin]], the actress and comedian who narrated the film, launched a direct mail fundraising campaign in Vito Russo's honor.<ref name="Telling"/> She also headlined a benefit at the Castro Theatre, which featured [[Robin Williams]], [[Harvey Fierstein]], and drag star [[John Epperson|Lypsinka]]. [[Steve Tisch]], [[James Hormel]], and [[Hugh Hefner]] offered "significant support", and the filmmakers began to receive foundation funding from the [[Paul Robeson|Paul Robeson Fund]], the California Council for the Humanities, and the Chicago Resource Center.<ref name="Telling"/>

European television again played an important role in funding the project, when ZDF/arte signed on, but it was not until the filmmakers contacted HBO that they were able to begin production. In May 1994, "Lily Tomlin contacted [[Michael J. Fuchs|Michael Fuchs]], chairman of HBO, on behalf of the project. Epstein, Friedman, Tomlin, and Rosenman flew to New York for a meeting with Fuchs and HBO Vice President [[Sheila Nevins]]. At that meeting, HBO committed to supply the remainder of the budget."<ref name="Telling"/>

== Reception and legacy ==
''The Celluloid Closet'' had precursors in [[Parker Tyler]]'s 1972 book ''Screening the Sexes'' and [[Richard Dyer]]'s 1977 ''Gays and Film''.<ref>{{Citation |last=Rosenbaum |first=Jonathan |author-link=Jonathan Rosenbaum |title=The Celluloid Closet: Homosexuality in the Movies |newspaper=Soho News |date=1981-08-04 |url=http://www.jonathanrosenbaum.net/?p=18511 |access-date=2010-02-25 }}</ref>

The film was released at a dramatic time in gay history, as Bill Clinton was elected president as the first major party presidential candidate to court and to promise openly to gay voters. However, the movement faced a huge public setback when "[[Don't ask, don't tell|Don't Ask, Don't Tell]]" was passed.<ref>{{cite encyclopedia |url= http://www.glbtq.com/social-sciences/gay_rights_movement,3.html |title=The Gay Rights Movement, U. S. |encyclopedia=The Gay, Lesbian, Bisexual, Transgender, and Queer Encyclopedia |year=2013 |access-date=20 August 2013}}</ref> In response to these obstacles, the LGBT-rights movement became increasingly media focused, realizing that the images projected into the world negatively affected perceptions of homosexuality. In 1994, the Gay and Lesbian Alliance Against Defamation was formed as a national organization. ''The Celluloid Closet'' came out in 1996, as marches and protests against homosexual representation in film and television grew.

''[[Time (magazine)|Time]]'' magazine noted that "protests [were] aimed specifically at some of Hollywood's biggest and most prestigious films, including ''[[The Silence of the Lambs (film)|The Silence of the Lambs]]'', which features a crazed transvestite who kills and flays women, and ''[[JFK (film)|JFK]]'', which has a scene in which gays alleged to be conspirators in the Kennedy assassination cavort in sadomasochistic fun and games."<ref name="Simpson">{{cite magazine |url= http://www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,975226,00.html |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20090802072720/http://www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,975226,00.html |url-status= dead |archive-date= August 2, 2009 |title=Out of the Celluloid Closet: Gay Activists are on a Rampage Against Negative Stereotyping and Other Acts of Homophobia in Hollywood |first1=Janice C. |last1=Simpson |first2=Patrick E. |last2=Cole |magazine=Time |volume=139 |issue=6 |page=65 |date=April 6, 1992 |access-date=20 August 2013}}</ref> The article quoted above features an interview with Kate Sorensen, a member of [[Queer Nation]], an organization that helped to organize the protests: "Every lesbian and bisexual character in these films is accused of being a psychotic killer ... And the girl never gets the girl. I'm tired of that." Gay activists across the country attacked films like these, where the homosexual character is portrayed as a disgustingly erotic killer.

It was believed that these portrayals reflected "a perverse fear of AIDS or the rising intolerance that [had] caused an increase in hate crimes of all kinds. Still, Hollywood's treatment of gays [hadn't] helped. With few exceptions, the homosexual characters in films are creepy misfits or campy caricatures".<ref name="Simpson"/> The release of ''The Celluloid Closet'' further emphasized the twisted way homosexuals have been depicted throughout history. Addressing specific issues that were pertinent at the time, Russo exposes the existence of Hollywood homosexuals as well as the uncontrolled homophobia that keeps homosexuality in the closet on and off the screen.<ref>{{cite book |first=Anneke |last=Smelik |chapter=Gay and Lesbian Criticism |editor1-first=John |editor1-last=Hill |editor2-first=Pamela |editor2-last=Church Gibson |title=The Oxford Guide to Film Studies |location=Oxford |publisher=Oxford University Press |year=1998 |page=135 |url=http://www.annekesmelik.nl/Criticism0001.pdf }}</ref>

According to writer Richard Barrios, "Russo essentially did for film what ACT UP did for AIDS awareness ... he opened up a world and a culture that had almost never been discussed before under any circumstances, exposing prejudices and hurts."<ref>{{cite book |title=Screened Out: Playing Gay in Hollywood from Edison to Stonewall |first=Richard |last=Barrios |publisher=Routledge |location=London |year=2005 |isbn=978-0415923293 |page=[https://archive.org/details/screenedoutplayi0000barr/page/5 5] |url=https://archive.org/details/screenedoutplayi0000barr/page/5 }}</ref> The film continued to motivate the need for positive representation of homosexuals. The Gay and Lesbian Alliance Against Defamation held seminars for staff at Columbia Pictures and Carolco. In addition, Hollywood Supports, a service organization with the mission to combat AIDS phobia and homophobia in the entertainment industry, was founded.<ref name="Simpson"/>

== Accolades ==
[[Gay and Lesbian Alliance Against Defamation]] (GLAAD) gives an award called the [[GLAAD Media Awards|Vito Russo Award]] to openly gay or lesbian people within the Hollywood film industry who advance the cause of fighting [[homophobia]]. The award was created after Russo's death. The film received the GLAAD Vito Russo Award in 1996.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0112651/awards/?ref_=tt_awd | title=The Celluloid Closet (1995) - Awards - IMDb | website=[[IMDb]] }}</ref>

In addition, the film was honored with four [[Emmy Award]] nominations in 1996. It was nominated for Outstanding Individual Achievement in Informational Programming for editing, sound recording and director of photography. It was also nominated for Outstanding Informational special. Additionally, the film received both a [[Peabody Award]]<ref>{{cite web |title=The Celluloid Closet |url=http://www.peabodyawards.com/award-profile/the-celluloid-closet |publisher=peabodyawards.com |access-date=30 September 2018}}</ref> and recognition at the 1996 [[Sundance Film Festival]] by winning the [[List of Sundance Film Festival award winners#1996|Freedom of Expression]] award.<ref>{{cite news |last1=Milvy |first1=Erika |title=Out and About |url=http://www.metroactive.com/papers/cruz/04.11.96/celluloid2-9615.html |access-date=30 September 2018 |work=www.metroactive.com}}</ref>

It was also nominated for the Truer Than Fiction Award at the [[12th Independent Spirit Awards]].<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EwC2g6a2lKk|title=12th annual Spirit Awards ceremony - FULL SHOW &#124; 1997 &#124; Film Independent|via=www.youtube.com}}</ref>
