'''''Salò, or the 120 Days of Sodom''''' ({{langx|it|Salò o le 120 giornate di Sodoma|link=no}}), billed on-screen as '''''Pasolini's 120 Days of Sodom''''' on English-language prints<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.dvdbeaver.com/film/DVDCompare/salo.htm |title=Salò, or the 120 Days of Sodom Blu-ray |website=DVDBeaver |access-date=22 May 2016 |archive-date=14 April 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160414072911/http://www.dvdbeaver.com/film/dvdcompare/salo.htm |url-status=live }}</ref> and commonly referred to as simply '''''Salò''''' ({{IPA|it|saˈlɔ|lang}}), is a 1975 Italian-French [[political drama film|political]] [[war film|war]] [[art horror]] film directed and co-written by [[Pier Paolo Pasolini]]. The film is a loose [[Film adaptation|adaptation]] of the 1785 novel (first published in 1904) ''[[The 120 Days of Sodom]]'' by the [[Marquis de Sade]], updating the story's setting to [[Italy]] during [[World War II]]. It was Pasolini's final film, released three weeks after [[Pier Paolo Pasolini#Murder|his murder]].

The film focuses on four wealthy, corrupt Italian [[libertine]]s in the fascist [[Italian Social Republic]] (1943–1945). The libertines kidnap 18 teenagers and subject them to four months of extreme violence, [[Sadomasochism|sadism]], genital torture and [[Psychological torture|psychological]] [[torture]]. The film explores themes of [[political corruption]], [[consumerism]], [[authoritarianism]], [[nihilism]], [[morality]], [[capitalism]], [[totalitarianism]], [[Sadomasochism|sadism]], [[sexuality]], and [[Italian fascism]]. The story is in four segments, inspired by [[Dante]]'s ''[[Divine Comedy]]'': the Anteinferno, the Circle of Manias, the Circle of Shit, and the Circle of Blood. The film also contains frequent references to and several discussions of [[Friedrich Nietzsche]]'s 1887 book ''[[On the Genealogy of Morality]]'', [[Ezra Pound]]'s poem ''[[The Cantos]]'', and [[Marcel Proust]]'s [[novel sequence]] ''[[In Search of Lost Time]]''.

Premiering at the Paris Film Festival on 23 November 1975, the film had a brief theatrical run in Italy before being [[Film censorship|banned]] in January 1976, and was released in the United States the following year on 3 October 1977. Because it depicts youths subjected to graphic violence, torture, sexual abuse, and murder, the film was controversial upon its release and has remained banned in many countries.

The confluence of thematic content in the film—ranging from the political and socio-historical, to psychological and sexual—has led to much critical discussion. It has been both praised and decried by various film historians and critics and was named the 65th-scariest film ever made by the [[Chicago Film Critics Association]] in 2006.<ref name="Chicago Film Critics Association">{{cite web|author=[[Chicago Film Critics Association]]|url=http://www.filmspotting.net/top100.htm|url-status=dead|title=Top 100 Scariest Movies|date=October 2006|via=[[Filmspotting]]|access-date=21 February 2009|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080117102530/http://www.filmspotting.net/top100.htm |archive-date=17 January 2008}}</ref>

===Ante Inferno/Antinferno===

In 1944, in the [[Italian Social Republic]] (RSI), the [[Italian fascism|fascist]] and German-controlled portion of Italy, four wealthy men of power—the Duke, the Bishop, the Magistrate and the President—agree to marry each other's daughters as the first step in a debauched ritual. They rule that once at the mansion the daughters will act as servants, and must remain completely naked at all times. They then recruit four teenage boys—Bruno, Fabrizio, Claudio and Ezio—as [[Collaboration with Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy|collaborators]] (guards) in the ''[[Decima Flottiglia MAS]]'' and four young men ([[Blackshirt]] soldiers), called "studs", who are chosen because of their large penises and good looks. The teenage guards and fascist secret police ([[OVRA]]) are sent out to capture young victims, of whom nine teenage boys and nine teenage girls are picked and brought to a villa near [[Marzabotto]]. One of the boys tries to escape but is shot dead.

===Circle of Manias/{{lang|it|Girone delle Manie}}===

Accompanying the libertines at the villa are four middle-aged [[Prostitution|prostitutes]], also collaborators, whose job it is to orchestrate debauched interludes for the men. During the many days at the villa, the four men subject their victims to increasingly abhorrent tortures and humiliations for their own pleasure. During breakfast, the daughters enter the dining hall naked to serve food. One of the studs, Efisio, trips and rapes Liana, the Duke's elder daughter, in front of the fascists and collaborators, who laugh at her cries of pain, aside from Ezio, who is secretly distressed by the ordeal. The President prompts Efisio to [[anal sex|penetrate him anally]] while the Duke sings "{{lang|it|italic=no|Sul Ponte di perati}}".

After breakfast, one of the victims who had previously tried to escape is shown dead to the crowd. Later, two victims named Sergio and Renata are forced to get married after one of the studs, Guido, and Signora Vaccari forcefully masturbate them. The President and the Duke then respectively rape Sergio and Renata to stop them from having sex with each other, during which the Magistrate additionally engages with the Duke in three-way intercourse.

On another day, the naked victims are forced to [[Animal roleplay|act like dogs]]. When one of the victims, Lamberto, refuses, the Magistrate [[Flagellation|whips]] him and feeds Susanna, the President's daughter, a slice of [[polenta]] filled with nails.

===Circle of Shit/{{lang|it|Girone della Merda}}===

Signora Maggi relates her troubled childhood and her [[coprophilia]]. As she explains how she [[matricide|killed her mother]] over a dispute about her prostitution, Renata cries, remembering the murder of her own mother. The Duke, aroused by the sound of her cries, orders Efisio and the guards to undress her. Renata begs [[God]] for death and the Duke forces her to eat his feces with a spoon. Later, at a mock wedding reception for the Magistrate and Sergio, the victims are presented with a meal of human feces, which the studs, prostitutes and masters eat without question. During a search for the victim with the most beautiful buttocks, for which the reward is instant death, Franco is picked. However, the Bishop reveals he was never going to fulfill the initial 'reward', instead promising a far more painful death in the near future.

===Circle of Blood/{{lang|it|Girone del Sangue}}===

A [[Black Mass]]-like wedding ensues between the studs and the libertines while the latter are dressed in drag. Afterwards, the Bishop examines the captives in their rooms, where they start betraying each other: Claudio reveals that Graziella is hiding a photograph, and Graziella reveals that Eva and Antiniska are having a secret affair. Victim Umberto is appointed to replace Ezio, who is betrayed by Antiniska for having sex with a black maid and, after revealing himself to be a communist, is shot dead by the libertines.

The remaining victims are summoned to determine which of them will be punished, and which ones are eligible to come with the libertines "to Salò". Graziella is spared due to her betrayal of Eva and Antiniska, and Rino is spared due to his submissive relationship with the Duke. Those who are called are given a blue ribbon and sentenced to a painful death, while those who have not been called, provided they keep collaborating with the libertines, can hope to return home. The victims are taken outside and raped, tortured and murdered through methods such as [[Human branding|branding]], [[hanging]], [[scalping]], burning and having their tongues and eyes cut out, as each libertine watches from the villa through binoculars. Horrified by the atrocities being committed, the pianist jumps out of a window and falls to her death.

As the torture continues, Bruno and Claudio dance a [[waltz]] together; Claudio asks the name of Bruno's girlfriend back at home, to which he replies "Margherita".

===Conception===
{{quote box|quote="I also realized that Sade, writing, was certainly thinking of Dante. So I began to restructure the film into three Dantesque ditches."|source=—Pier Paolo Pasolini about the film<ref name=":24">{{cite book|access-date=7 February 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160213053759/https://books.google.it/books?id=lwiPAwAAQBAJ&lpg=PP1&hl=it&pg=PP1#v=onepage&q&f=false |archive-date=13 February 2016 |author=Maurizio Massa |language=it |publisher=Lulu Press |title=Saggio sul cinema italiano del dopoguerra | date=2012 | isbn=978-1-4710-6686-3 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=lwiPAwAAQBAJ&pg=PP1 |url-status=live}}<!-- auto-translated from Italian by Module:CS1 translator --></ref>|align=right|width=25em

Pasolini's writing collaborator [[Sergio Citti]] had originally been attached to direct the intended adaptation of the [[Marquis de Sade]]'s ''[[The 120 Days of Sodom]]''.{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(05:25)}} During the creation of the first drafts of the script, Pasolini called upon several of his usual collaborators, among them Citti, Claudio Masenza, Antonio Troisi and specially [[Pupi Avati]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.lastampa.it/2015/11/02/spettacoli/pupi-avati-io-che-scrissi-sal-non-lho-mai-visto-fino-in-fondo-xS8d30OxVX7btaZQrJ4JKN/pagina.html|work=La Stampa Spettacoli|language=it|title=Pupi Avati: io che scrissi 'Salò', non-l'ho mai visto fino in condo|last=Giubilei|first=Franco|date=11 February 2015|access-date=4 October 2017|archive-date=3 November 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151103162341/http://www.lastampa.it/2015/11/02/spettacoli/pupi-avati-io-che-scrissi-sal-non-lho-mai-visto-fino-in-fondo-xS8d30OxVX7btaZQrJ4JKN/pagina.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

While collaborating with Citti on the script, Pasolini was compelled to transpose the setting of ''Salò'' from 18th-century France (as depicted in de Sade's original book) to the last days of [[Benito Mussolini]]'s Republic of Salò in the spring of 1944.{{sfn|Wells|2008|p=127}} Salò is a toponymical metonymy for the [[Italian Social Republic]] (because Mussolini ruled from this northern town rather than from Rome), which was a [[puppet state]] of [[Nazi Germany]].{{sfn|Annovi|2017|p=41}} While writing the script, it was decided between Citti and Pasolini that the latter would direct the project, as Citti had planned to write a separate project after completing ''Salò''.{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(05:22)}} Pasolini noted his main contribution to Citti's original screenplay as being its "[[Dante]]-esque structure",{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(05:29)}} which Pasolini felt had been de Sade's original intention with the source material.{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(05:38)}}

In the film, almost no background is given on the tortured subjects and, for the most part, they almost never speak.{{sfn|Kerner|2011|page=61}} Pasolini's depiction of the victims in such a manner was intended to demonstrate the physical body "as a commodity... the annulment of the personality of [[Other (philosophy)|the Other]]."{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(07:38)}} Specifically, Pasolini intended to depict what he described as an "anarchy of power",{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(07:47)}} in which sexual acts and physical abuse functioned as metaphor for the relationship between power and its subjects.{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(07:08)}} Aside from this theme, Pasolini also described the film as being about the "nonexistence of history" as it is seen from Western culture and [[Marxism]].{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(08:12)}}

Interior design was chosen to feature paintings and art by artists such as [[Gino Severini|Severini]], [[Marcel Duchamp|Duchamp]], and [[Lyonel Feininger|Feininger]]. Pasolini also stated that the four fascist libertines were written as highly educated characters and were to refer to themes brought up by, among others, [[Maurice Blanchot|Blanchot]], Nietzsche, and [[Pierre Klossowski|Klossowski]].<ref name=Bachmann>{{cite journal | url=https://online.ucpress.edu/fq/article-abstract/29/2/39/39023/Pasolini-on-de-Sade-An-Interview-during-the?redirectedFrom=fulltext | doi=10.2307/1211747 | jstor=1211747 | title=Pasolini on de Sade: An Interview during the Filming of the 120 Days of Sodom | journal=[[Film Quarterly]]| date=December 1975 | volume=29 | issue=2 | pages=39–45 | last1=Bachmann | first1=Gideon | last2=Pasolini | first2=Pasolini | url-access=subscription | archive-date=4 November 2023 | access-date=4 November 2023 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231104143729/https://online.ucpress.edu/fq/article-abstract/29/2/39/39023/Pasolini-on-de-Sade-An-Interview-during-the?redirectedFrom=fulltext | url-status=live }}</ref>

====Trilogy of death====
In contrast to his "Trilogy of Life" (''[[Il Decameron]]'', ''[[I racconti di Canterbury]]'' and ''[[Il fiore delle Mille e una notte]]''), Pasolini initially planned ''The 120 days of Sodom'' and ''Salò'' as separate stories, but noting similarity between both concepts – and based on their experiences in the Republic of Salò – conceived the idea of ''Salò or the 120 Days of Sodom''. Pasolini established that the violent scenes in ''Salò'' were symbolic and reduced the romanticism of his previous films, although knowing that once the film was premiered would be considered as damned.{{citation needed|date=October 2017}} As a continuation, Pasolini planned to make a biographical film about the life of child murderer [[Gilles de Rais]], but was murdered himself before ''Salò''{{'}}s release.{{Citation needed|date=December 2025}}

===Casting===
Initially, [[Ninetto Davoli]] was chosen to play Claudio, a young collaborationist, but due to legal problems he had to decline and was replaced by Claudio Troccoli, a young man who had a similarity to Davoli in his first films. Pupi Avati, being the writer, is not officially accredited also due to legal problems. Most of the actors of the cast, although they were natural performers, were non-professionals with minimal or no prior on-camera acting experience. Many of them were models, cast for their willingness to appear naked on-screen.

[[Franco Merli]] was considered to be a prototype of the Pasolinian boy. Ezio Manni remembers during filming: "The same with Franco Merli, the guy chosen for having the most beautiful butt. When they reward him by holding the gun to his head, he suddenly protested, he couldn't handle that scene. And the assistant director had to go and give him a hug."<ref name="auto">{{Cite news |last=Sciarra |first=Sergio |date=28 February 2007 |title=Pasolini e gli altri, dall'anti-inferno privato ai gironi di Salò |url=http://rassegnastampa.unipi.it/rassegna/archivio/2007/02/28SI43030.PDF |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161024033657/http://rassegnastampa.unipi.it/rassegna/archivio/2007/02/28SI43030.PDF |archive-date=2016-10-24 |journal=[[il Riformista]]}}</ref>

Pasolini regular [[Franco Citti]] was to play one of the soldiers' studs, but he did not appear. [[Laura Betti]] was also going to play Signora Vaccari, but also because of legal problems and prior commitments to ''[[1900 (film)|Novecento]]'' declined the role, though she doubled the voice of Hélène Surgère in post-production.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/person/15580%7C95869/Laura-Betti | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131120061157/http://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/person/15580%7C95869/Laura-Betti/ | url-status=dead | archive-date=20 November 2013 | title=Laura Betti}}</ref>

Uberto Paolo Quintavalle (the Magistrate) was a writer; he knew Pasolini from working on the newspaper ''[[Corriere della Sera]]''. He was chosen for the role because he had all "the characteristics of a decadent intellectual".{{sfn|Hart|2009|page=246}}

[[Aldo Valletti]] (the President) was a friend of Pasolini from the time of ''[[Accattone]]''. Giorgio Cataldi (the Bishop), another friend of Pasolini, was a clothes seller in Rome.<ref name="auto"/>

===Filming===
Several outdoor scenes were filmed in [[Villa Aldini]],{{sfn|Guidi|Sassetti|2009|page=54}} a neoclassical building on the hills of [[Bologna]]. The interiors were shot in {{ill|Villa Sorra|it}} near [[Castelfranco Emilia]].<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.davinotti.com/index.php?forum=50001337|title=Location verificate: Salò o le 120 giornate di Sodoma (1975)|work=Davinotti|date=4 October 2008|language=it|access-date=6 October 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171005201851/http://www.davinotti.com/index.php?forum=50001337|archive-date=5 October 2017|url-status=dead}}</ref> The noble hall of the building and the courtyard were filmed in the [[Cinecittà]] studios. The town on the [[Reno (river)|Reno]] replaces the fictional location in [[Marzabotto]]. Other scenes were filmed in Grand Hotel a Villa Feltrinelli in [[Gargnano]].
[[File:Villa_Sorra_03_Interno.jpg|thumb|Villa Sorra]]
The shooting, carried out mainly in the 16th-century Villa Gonzaga-Zani in [[Villimpenta]] in the spring of 1975, was difficult and involved scenes of [[homophilia]], [[coprophagia]] and [[sadomasochism]]. The acts of torture in the courtyard caused some of the actors to suffer abrasions and burns.{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(01:49)}} Actress Hélène Surgère described the film shoot as "unusual", with nearly 40 actors being on set at any given time, and Pasolini shooting "enormous" amounts of footage.{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(07:14)}} She also noted the mood on the set as "paradoxically jovial and immature" in spite of the content.{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(18:14)}} In-between working, the cast shared large meals of [[risotto]] and also had football games played against the crew of [[Bernardo Bertolucci]]'s ''[[Novecento (film)|Novecento]]'', which was being filmed nearby.{{sfn|Gundle|Rinaldi|2007|p=161}} It also marked the reconciliation between the then 34-year-old Bertolucci and his old mentor after several disagreements following Pasolini's criticism of ''[[Last Tango in Paris]]'' (1972) and his failure to defend it from drastic [[censorship]] measures.{{sfn|Barber|2010|p=99}} During the soccer match against the crew of ''Novecento'' both directors supported their teams, except that while Bertolucci did it from the bench, Pasolini played directly on the field. The crew of ''Novecento'' wore a purple shirt with phosphorescent bands to distract the opponents and had lined up, passing them off as engineers, two players from the youth team of Calcio Parma. The ''Salò'' crew lost 5 to 2 and Pasolini, furious, left the field before the end shouting: "He doesn't read anything anymore, that guy!" to Bertolucci.<ref>Tommaso Guaita, Lorenzo Di Giovanni, ''Vite Segrete dei Grandi Scrittori Italiani''{{full citation needed|date=July 2026}}</ref>

During production, some reels were stolen and the thieves demanded a ransom for their return. Using doubles, the same scenes were reshot but from a different angle. At the trial for Pasolini's murder, it was hypothesized that Pasolini was told the film reels were discovered in [[Ostia (Rome)|Ostia Lido]]. He was led there by Pelosi, the accused, and fell victim to an ambush, where he was murdered. However, there is no concrete evidence for this theory.<ref>{{Cite news|last1=Borgna |first1=Gianni|last2=Veltroni |first2=Walter|url=http://espresso.repubblica.it/dettaglio/chi-ha-ucciso-pasolini/2144956|title=Chi ha ucciso Pasolini|journal=[[L'Espresso]]|date=18 February 2011|access-date=24 May 2014|author2-link=Walter Veltroni|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110506033722/http://espresso.repubblica.it/dettaglio/chi-ha-ucciso-pasolini/2144956|archive-date=6 May 2011|url-status=dead}}</ref>

===Post-production===
====Musical score====
The original music corresponds to [[Ennio Morricone]] interpreted at the piano by {{ill|Arnaldo Graziosi|it}}. Other non-original music was {{lang|la|Veris leta facies|italic=no}} from [[Carl Orff]]'s cantata ''[[Carmina Burana (Orff)|Carmina Burana]]'' at nearly the end of the film during ''Circle of Blood''. Other music were several [[Frédéric Chopin]]'s pieces: [[Preludes (Chopin)#24 Preludes, Op. 28|Preludes Op. 28, no. 17]] and [[Prelude, Op. 28, No. 4 (Chopin)|no. 4]], and [[Waltzes, Op. 34 (Chopin)#No. 2 in A minor|Valse Op. 34, no. 2, in A minor]].<ref>{{cite book|last=Raykoff|first=Ivan|title=Dreams of Love – Playing the Romantic Pianist|publisher=Oxford University Press|year=2014|page=173|isbn=978-0-19-989267-9}}</ref>

==== Dubbing ====
Like most Italian films of the time, ''Salò'' was shot [[MOS (filmmaking)|MOS]] (without direct sound), with all dialogue and [[Foley (filmmaking)|foley effects]] [[Dubbing|dubbed]] in post-production. The controversy surrounding the production dissuaded the actors playing the Masters to return to loop their lines, so they were all re-dubbed by other (uncredited) actors. French actress Hélène Surgère (Vaccari) had her dialogue dubbed by [[Laura Betti]].

====Alternative endings====
Pasolini was undecided on what type of conclusion the film should have, to the point of having conceived and shot four different endings: the first was a shot of a red flag in the wind with the words "Love You", but it was abandoned by the director because he thought it "too pompous" and "prone to the ethics of psychedelic youth", which he detested.<ref name="AFDB">''Pier Paolo Pasolini'', [[Serafino Murri]] (ed.), {{ill|Editrice Il Castoro|it}} (2008)</ref> The second showed all the actors, other than the four gentlemen, the director and his troupe perform a wild dance in a room of the villa furnished with red flags and the scene was filmed with the purpose of using it as a background scene during the credits, but was discarded because it appeared, in the eyes of Pasolini, chaotic and unsatisfactory.<ref name="AFDB"/> Another final scene, discovered years later and which was only in the initial draft of the script, showed, after the torture's end, the four gentlemen walk out of the house and drawing conclusions about the morality of the whole affair.<ref name="fine">Mario Sesti, ''La fine di Salò'', extra del DVD ''La voce di Pasolini'', di Mario Sesti e Matteo Cerami.</ref> Finally, keeping the idea of dance as the summation of carnage, Pasolini chose to mount the so-called final "Margherita", with the two young blackshirts dancing.<ref name="AFDB"/>

==Release==
''Salò'' premiered at the Paris Film Festival on 23 November 1975, three weeks after Pasolini's death.{{sfn|Moliterno|2009|p=242}} In Italy, the film was initially rejected for screening by the Italian censorship, but received approval on 23 December 1975.{{sfn|Barber|2010|p=100}} The approval, however, was withdrawn three weeks after the film's Italian release in January 1976 and it was formally banned.{{sfn|Barber|2010|p=100}}{{sfn|Zampini|2016|page=11}} Worldwide distribution for the film was supplied by [[United Artists]].<ref>{{cite news|title=3 U.S. Films Make N.Y. Festival List|page=28|date=23 August 1977|url=https://www.newspapers.com/newspage/156027406/|work=[[The News Journal]]|location=Wilmington, Delaware|via=Newspapers.com|archive-date=5 October 2017|access-date=5 October 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171005152237/https://www.newspapers.com/newspage/156027406/|url-status=live}}</ref> In the United States, however, the film was given a [[limited release]] via Zebra Releasing Corporation on 3 October 1977.<ref name=canby/>

===Censorship===
{{quote box|width=23%|style=min-width: 15em|align=right|bgcolor=cornsilk|quote="[[Sadomasochism]] is an eternal characteristic of man. It existed during de Sade's time, and it exists now. But that's not what matters most...The real meaning of the sex in my film is as a metaphor for the relationship between power and its subjects."|source=Pasolini on the film's depiction of sex, 1975.{{sfn|Pasolini|Biett|Davoli|2002|loc=(07:08)}}}}

''Salò'' has been banned in several countries, because of its graphic portrayals of rape, torture and murder—mainly of people thought to be younger than eighteen years of age. The film remains banned in several countries and sparked numerous debates among critics and censors about whether or not it constituted pornography due to its nudity and graphic depiction of sexual acts.{{sfn|Cettl|2013|page=146}} In Italy, the film's producer, [[Alberto Grimaldi]], faced obscenity charges in court in January 1976, accused of "offending Italians' "sense of modesty" by producing a work "consisting totally of images and language representing sexual deviations and perversions.""<ref>{{cite news|date=22 January 1976|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-blade/200680193/|work=[[The Blade (Toledo, Ohio)|The Toledo Blade]]|page=13|title=People: Zsa Zsa Seeking Divorce From Barbie Doll Inventor|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref><ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-montreal-star/200680273/|page=C-6|title=Pasolini's producer charged with obscenity|date=23 January 1976|work=[[The Montreal Star]]|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref>

The film was rejected by the [[British Board of Film Classification|British Board of Film Censors]] (BBFC) in January 1976. It was first screened at the Old Compton Street Cinema Club in [[Soho, London]] in 1977, in an uncut form and without certification from BBFC secretary [[James Ferman]]; the premises were raided by the [[Metropolitan Police]] after a few days. A cut version prepared under Ferman's supervision, again without formal certification, was subsequently screened under cinema club conditions for some years. In 2000, in an uncut form, the film was finally passed for theatrical and video distribution in the United Kingdom.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Case Study: Salo/120 Days of Sodom |url=http://www.sbbfc.co.uk/CaseStudies/Salo120_Days_of_Sodom |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121115101138/http://www.sbbfc.co.uk/CaseStudies/Salo120_Days_of_Sodom |archive-date=15 November 2012 |access-date=2025-04-12 |website=www.sbbfc.co.uk |url-status=live }}</ref>

The film was not banned in the United States and received a [[limited release]] in October 1977; it was, however, banned in [[Ontario]], Canada.{{sfn|Lerner|1997|page=802}} In 1994, an undercover policeman in [[Cincinnati]], Ohio, rented the film from a local gay bookstore and then arrested the owners for "[[Procuring (prostitution)|pandering]]". A large group of artists, including [[Martin Scorsese]] and [[Alec Baldwin]], and scholars signed a legal brief arguing the film's artistic merit; the Ohio state court dismissed the case because the police violated the owners' [[Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution|Fourth Amendment]] rights, without reaching the question of whether the film was obscene.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.theroc.org/roc-mag/textarch/roc-19/roc19-05.htm |title=ACLU Arts Censorship Project Newsletter |publisher=Theroc.org |access-date=24 February 2009 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090209092920/http://www.theroc.org/roc-mag/textarch/roc-19/roc19-05.htm |archive-date=9 February 2009 |url-status=dead}}</ref>

It was banned in Australia in 1976 for reasons of indecency.<ref>{{cite journal|journal=Media Information Australia|title=Salò|pages=126–7|year=1993|publisher=Australian Film and Television School|volume=67–70}}{{full citation needed|date=October 2019}}</ref> After a 17-year-long ban, the [[Australian Classification Board]] passed the film with an R-18+ (for 18 and older only) uncut for theatrical release in July 1993. However, the [[Australian Classification Review Board]] overturned this decision in February 1998 and banned the film outright, for "offensive cruelty with high impact, sexual violence and depictions of offensive and revolting fetishes". The film was then pulled from all Australian cinemas. ''Salò'' was resubmitted for classification in Australia in 2008, only to be rejected once again.<ref>{{cite news|first=Rachel|last=Browne|url=http://www.brisbanetimes.com.au/news/national/sadistic-sex-movie-ban-attacks-art-expression/2008/07/19/1216163226456.html|title=Sadistic sex movie ban 'attacks art expression'|newspaper=[[Brisbane Times]]|date=20 July 2008|access-date=21 February 2009}}</ref> The [[DVD-Video|DVD]] print was apparently a modified version, causing outrage in the media over censorship and freedom of speech. In 2010, the film was submitted again and passed with an R18+ rating. According to the Australian Classification Board media release, the DVD was passed due to "the inclusion of 176 minutes of additional material which provided a context to the feature film." The media release also stated that "The Classification Board wishes to emphasise that this film is classified R18+ based on the fact that it contains additional material. Screening this film in a cinema without the additional material would constitute a breach of classification laws."<ref>{{cite web|title=Film Censorship: Salò, or the 120 Days of Sodom In New Zealand, the film was originally banned in 1976. The ban was upheld in 1993. In 1997, special permission was granted for the film to be screened uncut at a film festival. ==Reception==
===Box office===
''Salò, or the 120 Days of Sodom'' grossed $1,810,733 internationally, with $1,786,578 of its box office revenue being generated in Sweden alone.<ref name=BOM>{{cite web|work=[[Box Office Mojo]]|title=Salò, or the 120 Days of Sodom|url-status=live|url=https://www.boxofficemojo.com/title/tt0073650/|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260226084753/https://www.boxofficemojo.com/title/tt0073650/|archive-date=26 February 2026}}</ref>

===Critical response===
The [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]] reports a 70% approval rating based on 40 reviews, with an average rating of 6.70/10. The site's consensus reads, "''Salò, or the 120 Days of Sodom'' will strike some viewers as irredeemably depraved, but its unflinching view of human cruelty makes it impossible to ignore."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/salo_o_le_120_giornate_di_sodoma_1979|title=Salo, or the 120 Days of Sodom|work=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]|publisher=[[Fandango Media]]|access-date=17 May 2023|archive-date=9 June 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230609072347/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/salo_o_le_120_giornate_di_sodoma_1979|url-status=live}}</ref>

Director [[Michael Haneke]] named the film his fourth-favorite film when he voted for the 2002 ''[[Sight and Sound]]'' poll. Director [[Catherine Breillat]] and critic [[Joel David]] also voted for the film.<ref>{{cite web|title=Who voted for which film|url=http://old.bfi.org.uk/sightandsound/polls/topten/poll/voted.php?film=Sal%C3%B2%20(Pasolini)|work=Sight & Sound|publisher=[[British Film Institute]]|access-date=22 November 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140808045541/http://old.bfi.org.uk/sightandsound/polls/topten/poll/voted.php?film=Sal%C3%B2%20(Pasolini)|archive-date=8 August 2014|url-status=dead}}</ref> [[David Cross]], [[Brady Corbet]], [[Gaspar Noé]], and [[Korn]] frontman [[Jonathan Davis]] have named it one of their favorite films.<ref>{{cite web|last=Goodsell|first=Luke|title=Five Favorite Films with David Cross|url=http://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/its_a_disaster/news/1925386/five_favorite_films_with_david_cross/|work=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]|publisher=Flixster, Inc.|access-date=22 November 2013|date=18 June 2012|archive-date=4 November 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131104034809/http://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/its_a_disaster/news/1925386/five_favorite_films_with_david_cross/|url-status=dead}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|last1=Lordygan|first1=Kerr|title=Gaspar Noe's Five Favorite Films|url=https://editorial.rottentomatoes.com/article/gaspar-noes-five-favorite-films/|website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]|publisher=[[Flixster]]|access-date=26 December 2016|date=5 November 2015|archive-date=26 December 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161226150203/https://editorial.rottentomatoes.com/article/gaspar-noes-five-favorite-films/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G6j1UdwajxQ | title=Jonathan Davis (KORN) – What's in My Bag? [[Jonathan Rosenbaum]] of the ''[[Chicago Reader]]'' wrote of the film: "[[Roland Barthes]] noted that in spite of all its objectionable elements (he pointed out that any film that renders Sade real and fascism unreal is doubly wrong), this film should be defended because it 'refuses to allow us to redeem ourselves.' It's certainly the film in which Pasolini's protest against the modern world finds its most extreme and anguished expression. Very hard to take, but in its own way an essential work."<ref name=rosenbaum>{{cite web|work=[[Chicago Reader]]|title=Salo, or the 120 Days of Sodom |date=2013|archive-date=24 September 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160924040145/https://www.chicagoreader.com/chicago/salo-or-the-120-days-of-sodom/Film?oid=1068097|url=https://www.chicagoreader.com/chicago/salo-or-the-120-days-of-sodom/Film?oid=1068097|access-date=4 October 2017}}</ref>

The film's reputation for pushing boundaries has led some critics to criticize or avoid it; the ''[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out]]'' film guide, for example, deemed the film a "thoroughly objectionable piece of work", adding that it "offers no insights whatsoever into power, politics, history or sexuality."<ref>{{cite web|work=[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out]]|title=Salò, o le Centoventi Giornate di Sodoma | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121130223702/http://www.timeout.com/film/reviews/73944/sal_o_le_centoventi_giornate_di_sodoma.html|archive-date=30 November 2012|url-status=dead|access-date=8 October 2010|url=http://www.timeout.com/film/reviews/73944/sal_o_le_centoventi_giornate_di_sodoma.html}}</ref> ''[[TV Guide]]'' gave the film a mixed review awarding it a score of 2.5/4, stating, "despite moments of undeniably brilliant insight, is nearly unwatchable, extremely disturbing, and often literally nauseous".<ref>{{cite web|title=Salo, Or The 120 Days of Sodom Review|url=http://movies.tvguide.com/salo-or-the-120-days-of-sodom/review/129758|website=[[TV Guide]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141029194951/http://movies.tvguide.com/salo-or-the-120-days-of-sodom/review/129758|archive-date=29 October 2014|url-status=dead}}</ref>

Upon the film's United States release, [[Vincent Canby]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' wrote, "''Salo'' is, I think, a perfect example of the kind of material that, theoretically, anyway, can be acceptable on paper but becomes so repugnant when visualized on the screen that it further dehumanizes the human spirit, which is supposed to be the artist's concern."<ref name=canby>{{cite news|url= https://www.nytimes.com/1977/10/01/archives/film-festival-salo-is-disturbing.html |title=Film Festival: 'Salo' Is Disturbing...|last=Canby|first=Vincent|author-link=Vincent Canby|work=[[The New York Times]]|date=1 October 1977|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200929211905/https://www.nytimes.com/1977/10/01/archives/film-festival-salo-is-disturbing.html|archive-date=29 September 2020}}</ref> In 2011 [[Roger Ebert]] wrote that he owned the film since its release on [[LaserDisc]] but had not watched it, citing the film's transgressive reputation.{{sfn|Ebert|2011|page=733}} In 2011, David Haglund of ''[[Slate (magazine)|Slate]]'' surveyed five film critics and three of them said that it was required viewing for any serious critic or [[Cinephilia|cinephile]]. Haglund concluded that he still would not watch the film.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.slate.com/blogs/browbeat/2011/10/04/salo_five_film_critics_on_why_you_have_to_see_it.html|title=Must Film Buffs Watch the Revolting Salò?|last=Haglund|first=David|work=[[Slate (magazine)|Slate]]|date=4 October 2011|access-date=4 September 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150403001746/http://www.slate.com/blogs/browbeat/2011/10/04/salo_five_film_critics_on_why_you_have_to_see_it.html|archive-date=3 April 2015|url-status=dead}}</ref>

===Scholarly analysis===
''Salò'' has received critical analysis from film scholars, critics and others for its converging depictions of sexual violence and cross-referencing of historical and sociopolitical themes. Commenting on the film's prevalent sexual themes, horror film scholar Stephen Barber writes: "The core of ''Salò'' is the [[anus]], and its narrative drive pivots around the act of [[sodomy]]. No scene of a sex act has been confirmed in the film until one of the libertines has approached its participants and sodomized the figure committing the act. The filmic material of ''Salò'' is one that compacts celluloid and feces, in Pasolini's desire to burst the limits of cinema, via the anally resonant eye of the film lens."{{sfn|Barber|2010|p=100}} Barber also notes that Pasolini's film reduces the extent of the storytelling sequences present in de Sade's ''The 120 Days of Sodom'' so that they "possess equal status" with the sadistic acts committed by the libertines.{{sfn|Barber|2010|pages=99–102}}

Pasolini scholar Gian Annovi notes in the book ''Pier Paolo Pasolini: Performing Authorship'' (2007) that ''Salò'' is stylistically and thematically marked by a "link between [[Marcel Duchamp|Duchamp]]'s [[Dada]] aesthetics and the perverse dynamics of desire", which, according to Annovi, became artistic points of interest for Pasolini in the early developments of ''Salò''.{{sfn|Annovi|2017|p=176}}

Dallas Marshall for ''Film Inquiry'' wrote in an article from 2021 that "''Salò'' is his [Pasolini's] strongest diatribe against consumerism",<ref name="filminquiry_com">{{cite web | url=https://www.filminquiry.com/salo-a-film-about-power/ | title=Why ''Salò'' Is an Important Film About Power and Not Just Malignant Smut | date=8 September 2021 | access-date=4 November 2023 | archive-date=4 November 2023 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231104145311/https://www.filminquiry.com/salo-a-film-about-power/ | url-status=live }}</ref> and other critics<ref>{{cite web |url=https://newhumanist.org.uk/articles/4908/salo-the-unseen-movie |title=Salò: The unseen movie |website=[[New Humanist]] |last=Salmon |first=Peter |date=27 July 2015 |access-date=4 November 2023 |archive-date=4 November 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231104150606/https://newhumanist.org.uk/articles/4908/salo-the-unseen-movie |url-status=live }}</ref> have brought up that the film is to be seen as symbolism; a metaphor for the modern society, capitalism, and especially consumerism, i.e., how "those in power can make people consume crap (ads, commercials, etc.) and those of the people who resist die, and the majority passively obeys and goes along with the system, and a few will collaborate with the rulers". The ''Film Inquiry'' article also mentions this metaphor: 
"Much like the citizens of fascist Italy and Nazi Germany, the prisoners were eating up the bullshit of dictators. But it goes deeper than that because one of the aspects of modern society that Pasolini had a great disdain for was mass consumerism. The man viewed consumerism, especially that of processed foods, to be pesticide on the human soul and eating literal excrement. He yearned for the days of classical beauty and good food that nourished the body. He viewed the modern world as a perversion and was thoroughly disgusted with its trajectory."<ref name="filminquiry_com" /> The BBFC notes that the film "is intended as a critique of both fascism and consumerism".<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.bbfc.co.uk/education/case-studies/salo-120-days-of-sodom | title=Salo/120 Days of Sodom | date=4 August 2020 | website=British Board of Film Classification | access-date=4 November 2023 | archive-date=4 December 2023 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231204232032/https://www.bbfc.co.uk/education/case-studies/salo-120-days-of-sodom | url-status=live }}</ref> 

Similarly, building on the film's reference to [[Pierre Klossowski]]'s ''Sade mon prochain'', art historian Michael Newell Witte in the book ''Klossowski, Pasolini, Gurrola'' (2026) argues that ''Salò'' translates Klossowski's concepts of "accursed virility" and "living currency" into filmic syntax. Witte posits that Pasolini uses bodily repetition and ceremonial torture as a political economic critique of consumer capitalism, where human bodies and language are reduced to standardized commodities.{{sfn|Witte|2026|pages=53–80}}

==Legacy==
''Salò'' has earned a reputation among some film scholars for being the "sickest film of all time,"{{sfn|Mathjis|Sexton|2011|p=106}}{{sfn|Mathjis|Mendik|2011|page=177}} with some citing it as an early progenitor of the [[extreme cinema]] subgenre, alongside the American film ''[[The Last House on the Left]]'' (1972).{{sfn|Hyland|Shorey|2013|page=176}}

Film scholar Matthias Frey notes that the cross-section between the film's thematic content and graphic visuals has resulted in it being considered both a horror film as well as an [[art film]]:

{{blockquote|[Films like ''Salò''], which are usually considered by critics as 'works' by the 'artists'&nbsp;... might be received in practice also by individuals who watch ''[[Saw (2004 film)|Saw]]'' or ''[[Hostel (2005 film)|Hostel]]'' or any 'popular' or cult horror film.{{sfn|Frey|2013|page=114}}}}

In 2006, the [[Chicago Film Critics Association]] named it the 65th-scariest film ever made.<ref name="Chicago Film Critics Association"/>

In 2008, [[Adam Chodzko]], an English artist, released a short film on [[Super 8 film]] called ''Reunion: Salò, 1998''. Chodzko placed advertisements in newspapers and in hopes of reuniting the sixteen actors who had portrayed the roles of the adolescents who were tortured to death in ''Salò''. Chodzko only found one of the actors, Antiniska Nemour, who was working in [[dentistry]] in Italy. Chodzko made the film with Nemour as the "guest of honor" and the other actors portrayed by [[double (occupation)|doubles]] who resembled the original cast members.<ref name=A>{{cite web|date=April 2009|page=149|magazine=[[Artforum]]|url=https://www.artforum.com/events/adam-chodzko-175923/|title=Adam Chodzko: The British School|author=Carboni, Massimo|access-date=22 August 2025|archive-date=23 August 2025|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250823190559/https://www.artforum.com/events/adam-chodzko-175923/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.adamchodzko.com/works/reunion-salo-1998/|website=adamchodzko.com|title=Reunion: Salò, 1998|access-date=15 April 2022|archive-date=2 December 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211202073955/https://www.adamchodzko.com/works/reunion-salo-1998/|url-status=live}}</ref>

In 2010, the [[Toronto International Film Festival]] placed it at no. 47 on its list of "The Essential 100 films."<ref name="Toronto International Film Festival">{{cite web|title=The Essential 100|url=http://tiff.net/essential/about/essential100|work=[[Toronto International Film Festival]]|publisher=Toronto International Film Festival Inc.|access-date=22 November 2013|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101226144923/http://tiff.net/essential/about/essential100|archive-date=2010-12-26}}</ref>
