'''''Under Milk Wood''''' is a 1954 [[radio drama]] by the Welsh poet [[Dylan Thomas]]. The [[BBC]] commissioned the play, which was later adapted for the stage. The first public reading was in New York City in 1953.

A [[Under Milk Wood (1971 film)|film version of the same name]], directed by [[Andrew Sinclair]], was released in 1971. A second adaptation of the play, directed by [[Pip Broughton]], was staged for television in 2014 for the 60th anniversary of the piece. 

An omniscient narrator invites the audience to listen to the dreams and innermost thoughts of the inhabitants of the fictional small Welsh fishing town, Llareggub, ([[Bugger#Bugger all|buggerall]] spelt backwards).

They include Mrs. Ogmore-Pritchard, relentlessly nagging her two dead husbands; Captain Cat, reliving his seafaring times; the two Mrs. Dai Breads; Organ Morgan, obsessed with his music; and Polly Garter, pining for her dead lover. Later, the town awakens, and, aware now of how their feelings affect whatever they do, we watch them go about their daily business.

==Origins and development==
===Background===
[[File:Coach & Horses , Tenby. July 2024.jpg|thumb|upright|The Coach & Horses in [[Tenby]], where Thomas is reputed to have been so drunk that he left his manuscript to ''Under Milk Wood'' on a stool]]

In 1931, the 17-year-old Thomas created a piece for the Swansea Grammar School magazine that included a conversation of ''Milk Wood'' stylings, between Mussolini and Wife, similar to those between Mrs. Ogmore-Pritchard and her two husbands that would later be found in ''Under Milk Wood''.<ref>{{cite web |last=Rees |first=Ian |title=Lunch at Mussolini's': Ethel Ross and Dylan Thomas |website=DylanThomas.com |date=21 January 2016 |url=http://www.dylanthomas.com/cy/blog-cy/ethel-ross/ |access-date=19 May 2023 |archive-date=19 May 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230519032315/http://www.dylanthomas.com/cy/blog-cy/ethel-ross/ |url-status=live }}</ref> 

In 1933, Thomas talked at length with his mentor and friend, Bert Trick,<ref>{{cite journal |last=Trick |first=Kerith |title=The Original Marx Brother: Bert Trick and Dylan Thomas |journal=[[New Welsh Review]] |number=54 |date=Autumn 2001}}</ref> about creating a play about a Welsh town:
{{blockquote|He read it to Nell and me in our bungalow at [[Caswell Bay|Caswell]] around the old Dover stove, with the paraffin lamps lit at night ... the story was then called Llareggub, which was a mythical village in South Wales, a typical village, with terraced houses with one [[outhouse|ty bach]] to about five cottages and the various characters coming out and emptying the slops and exchanging greetings and so on; that was the germ of the idea which ... developed into ''Under Milk Wood''.<ref>Thomas, D. N. (2003), ''Dylan Remembered 1914–1934'', Seren, p. 165.</ref>}}

In February 1937, Thomas outlined his plans for a Welsh Journey, following a route that would "be decided by what incidents arose, what people told me stories, what pleasant or unpleasant or curious things...I encountered in the little-known villages among the lesser-known people."<ref>Letters to David Higham, 9 February 1937 and Keidrych Rhys, 26 March 1937; and a telegram to Emily Holmes Coleman, 6 April 1937, in the ''Collected Letters''.</ref>

A year later, in March 1938, Thomas suggested that a group of Welsh writers should prepare a verse-report of their "own particular town, village, or district."<ref>Letter to Meurig Walters, written in Blashford, Hampshire, 28 March 1938 in ''Collected Letters''.</ref>

===Laugharne===
In May 1938, the Thomas family moved to [[Laugharne]], a small town on the estuary of the river Tâf in [[Carmarthenshire]], Wales. They lived there intermittently<ref>They also lived in Blashford (November 1938 to March 1939 and January 1940 to March 1940), Marshfield, Chippenham (July 1940 to November 1940), and Bishopston (December 1940 to April 1941) – see Thomas' ''Collected Letters''.</ref> for just under two years until July 1941. They did not return to live there until 1949.<ref>Thomas' ''Collected Letters'' show that the family lived for 18 months in Gosport Street and Sea View, Laugharne, between May 1938 and July 1940, and for three months in the Castle in 1941. They did not return to live in Laugharne until May 1949.</ref> 

The author [[Richard Hughes (British writer)|Richard Hughes]], who lived in Laugharne, has recalled that Thomas spoke to him in 1939 about writing a play about Laugharne in which the townsfolk would play themselves,<ref>Thomas, D. N. (2004), ''Dylan Remembered 1935–1953'', Seren, p. 75.</ref> an idea pioneered on the radio by Cornish villagers in the 1930s.<ref>This was a casting idea developed by [[Bernard Walke]] in his 1930 radio plays, featuring Cornish miners, fishermen, and farmers playing themselves. See ''Broadcasting Under Milk Wood'', Note 13, online at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/under-milk-wood-a-play-for-ears?authuser=0 Broadcasting Under Milk Wood] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250707014248/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/under-milk-wood-a-play-for-ears?authuser=0 |date=7 July 2025 }} It is also at D.N. Thomas (2020) ''Under Milk Wood: A Play for Ears: Reflections on T. Rowland Hughes, Philip Burton and Dylan Thomas'', May, ''New Welsh Review''.</ref> Four years later, in 1943, Thomas again met Hughes, and this time outlined a play about a Welsh village certified as 'mad' by government inspectors.<ref>Richard Hughes in Thomas, D. N. (2004), ''Dylan Remembered 1935–1953'', Seren, p. 75, and also Hughes' review of ''Under Milk Wood'' in the ''Sunday Times'', 7 March 1954.</ref>

Hughes believed that when Thomas "came to write ''Under Milk Wood'', he did not use actual Laugharne characters."<ref>The interview, carried out in the 1960s, is in Thomas, D. N. (2004), ''Dylan Remembered 1935–1953'', Seren, p. 75.</ref> Nevertheless, some elements of Laugharne are discernible in the play. A girl, age 14, named Rosie Probert ("Rosie Probert, thirty three Duck Lane. Come on up, boys, I'm dead.") was living in Horsepool Road in Laugharne at the 1921 census.<ref>Rosie Probert, born in Swansea, 1907. She was living in Horsepool Road with an aunt and uncle, Margaret and Harlford Francis, a ship's steward. It was a crowded household: also living with them were their son, daughter, son-in-law, step-son and nephew. 1921 census record transcription from FindmyPast.</ref> Although there is no-one of that name in Laugharne in the 1939 War Register,<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.findmypast.co.uk/1939register|title=Who Lived in Your House in 1939? – 1939 Register|website=www.findmypast.co.uk|access-date=6 May 2023|archive-date=19 May 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230519031122/https://www.findmypast.co.uk/1939register|url-status=live}}</ref> nor anyone named Rosie, Laugharne resident, Jane Dark, has described how she told Thomas about her.<ref>See the interview with Jane Dark in D. N. Thomas (2004) ''Dylan Remembered 1935–1953'', p179, Seren.</ref> Dark has also described telling Thomas about the ducks of Horsepool Road ("Duck Lane") and the drowning of the girl who went in search of them.<ref>D. N. Thomas (2004) ''Dylan Remembered 1935–1953'', p179, Seren.</ref>

Both Laugharne and Llareggub have a castle,<ref>Llareggub's Bessie Bighead has a cow called Fan from the Castle</ref> and, like Laugharne, Llareggub is on an estuary ("boat-bobbing river and sea"), with cockles, cocklers and Cockle Row. Laugharne also provides the clock tower of Myfanwy Price's dreams,<ref>"Miss Price, dressmaker and sweetshop-keeper, dreams of...her lover, tall as the town clock tower..."</ref> as well as Salt House Farm which may have inspired the name of Llareggub's Salt Lake Farm.<ref>For an alternative explanation of the name "Salt Lake Farm", see [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/dylans-sketch-of-llareggub?authuser=0 Dylan's sketch of Llareggub] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210727124740/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/dylans-sketch-of-llareggub?authuser=0 |date=27 July 2021 }}</ref> Llareggub's Butcher Beynon almost certainly draws on butcher and publican Carl Eynon, though he was not in Laugharne but in nearby St Clears.<ref>For more on Dylan drinking in Eynon's pub, see online at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood?authuser=0 Eynon butcher and publican] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211111164601/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood?authuser=0 |date=11 November 2021 }} which is based on an interview with Eynon: G. Burn (1972) "Dylan Thomas", ''Radio Times'', 26 October: "He [Dylan] used to come in here and sit in the room with the coracle men around an open fire and I'd watch him listening and memorising and writing down. He told me he was writing a play and that I was in it, but he'd put you off if you started fishing him...My fridges are down the lane out the back there and sometimes Dylan would see me walking back up to the shop carrying a cleaver, often with my little dog. It was a dachshund, see, a sausage dog, but in his play he calls it a corgi." (Burn 1972)</ref>

===New Quay===
In September 1944, the Thomas family moved to a bungalow called Majoda on the cliffs outside [[New Quay]], Cardiganshire ([[Ceredigion]]), Wales, and left in July the following year. Thomas had previously visited New Quay whilst living in nearby [[Talsarn]] in 1942–1943,<ref>For an account of Thomas' time in Gelli, Talsarn, see Thomas, D. N. (2000), ''A Farm, Two Mansions and a Bungalow'', Seren.</ref> and had an aunt and cousins living in New Quay.<ref>The aunt was Elizabeth Ann Williams who had married his mother Florence's brother, John Williams. Elizabeth came to live in New Quay in the 1920s with her daughter, Theodosia, Dylan's first cousin. Theodosia married Thomas Legg, a master mariner, in 1930. Their three children were born and brought up in New Quay. Thomas Legg's parents, Captain George Legg OBE and his wife, Margaret, also lived in New Quay. For more on Thomas' New Quay relations, see pp. 106–115 of D. N. Thomas (2002), ''The Dylan Thomas Trail'', Y Lolfa. George and Margaret Legg, and their daughter-in-law, Theodosia, are to be found in the 1945 Register of Electors for New Quay.</ref> He had written a New Quay pub poem, ''Sooner than you can water milk'', in 1943,<ref>This poem can be found on page 189 of Thomas, D. N. (2000), ''Dylan Thomas: A Farm, Two Mansions and a Bungalow'', Seren, and also in Jones, D. (1991), ''Dylan Thomas: The Poems'', Dent, with a note on its New Quay origins. A later version of the poem can be found in Thomas' letter of 28 May 1945 to T. W. Earp in the ''Collected Letters'', with note on its New Quay origins.</ref> which has several words and ideas that would later re-appear in ''Under Milk Wood''.<ref>For example, watered-down milk, fish heads, telescopes, bible black and prying preachers. Elements of the play can also be found in another work from 1943, in Thomas' broadcast, ''Reminiscences of Childhood'' e.g. Cherry Owen's fish-frail of bottles, whilst Thomas' reference in the broadcast to a far-off race of Mormons anticipates Utah Watkins of Salt Lake Farm.</ref> Thomas' bawdy letter-poem from New Quay to T. W. Earp, written just days after moving into Majoda,<ref>21 September 1944, Collected Letters.</ref> contains the name "No-good", anticipating Nogood Boyo of&nbsp;''Under Milk Wood''.

Thomas's wife, Caitlin, has described the year at Majoda as "one of the most important creative periods of his life...New Quay was just exactly his kind of background, with the ocean in front of him ... and a pub<ref>The Black Lion</ref> where he felt at home in the evenings."<ref>Thomas, C. (1986), ''Caitlin: Life with Dylan Thomas'', Secker and Warburg, p. 92.</ref> Thomas' biographers have taken a similar view. His time there, recalled [[Constantine FitzGibbon]], his first biographer, was "a second flowering, a period of fertility that recalls the earliest days … [with a] great outpouring of poems", as well as a good deal of other material.<ref>See C. FitzGibbon (1965) ''The Life of Dylan Thomas'', p. 266 Little Brown.</ref> Biographer [[Paul Ferris (Welsh writer)|Paul Ferris]] agreed: "On the grounds of output, the bungalow deserves a plaque of its own."<ref>P. Ferris (1999), ''Dylan Thomas: The Biography'', Dent, p. 4.</ref> Thomas' third biographer, [[George Tremlett]], concurred, describing the time in New Quay as "one of the most creative periods of Thomas's life."<ref>G. Tremlett (1993), ''Dylan Thomas: In the Mercy of his Means'', Constable, p. 95.</ref>

Some of those who knew him well, including FitzGibbon, have said that Thomas began writing ''Under Milk Wood'' in New Quay.<ref>FitzGibbon, C. (1965), ''The Life of Dylan Thomas'' pp. 266–67, Little Brown.</ref> The play's first producer, [[Douglas Cleverdon]], agreed, noting that Thomas "wrote the first half within a few months; then his inspiration seemed to fail him when he left New Quay."<ref>Cleverdon, D. (1954), in the ''Radio Times'', 22 January. It has to be said that Cleverdon is not consistent in his dating of work on the play.</ref> One of Thomas' closest friends and confidantes, Ivy Williams of Brown's Hotel, Laugharne, has said "Of course, it wasn't really written in Laugharne at all. It was written in New Quay, most of it."<ref>in Thomas D. N. (2004), ''Dylan Remembered 1935–1953'', Seren, p. 187.</ref>

The writer and puppeteer, [[Walter Wilkinson (puppeteer)|Walter Wilkinson]], visited New Quay in 1947, and his essay on the town captures its character and atmosphere as Thomas would have found it two years earlier.<ref>W. Wilkinson (1948), ''Puppets in Wales'', Bles, and online here [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/hand-puppets-in-new-quay-walter-wilkinson-1947?authuser=0 Wilkinson in New Quay] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220513162107/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/hand-puppets-in-new-quay-walter-wilkinson-1947?authuser=0 |date=13 May 2022 }}</ref> Photos of New Quay in Thomas' day, as well as a 1959 television programme about the town, can be found here.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/|title=Dylan Thomas: A Postcard from New Quay|website=sites.google.com|access-date=12 August 2018|archive-date=19 March 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220319073630/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/|url-status=live}}</ref>

There were many milestones<ref>Milestones:in particular, the broadcasts ''Quite Early One Morning'' (1945), ''The Londoner'' (1946), ''Margate – Past and Present'' (1946) and ''Return Journey'' (1947).</ref>  on the road to Llareggub, and these have been detailed by Professor Walford Davies in his Introduction to the definitive edition of ''Under Milk Wood''.<ref>Davies, W., and R. Maud, eds (1995), ''Under Milk Wood: the Definitive Edition'', Everyman.</ref> The most important of these was ''Quite Early One Morning'',<ref>The script can be read at R. Maud, ed. (1991) ''On the Air with Dylan Thomas: The Broadcasts'', New Directions.</ref> Thomas' description of a walk around New Quay, broadcast by the BBC in 1945, and described by Davies as a "veritable storehouse of phrases, rhythms and details later resurrected or modified for ''Under Milk Wood''."<ref>Davies, W., and R. Maud, eds (1995), ''Under Milk Wood: the Definitive Edition'', p. xviii, Everyman.</ref> For example, the "done-by-hand water colours" of ''Quite Early One Morning'' appear later as the "watercolours done by hand" of ''Under Milk Wood''.<ref>In First Voice's "To begin at the beginning."</ref>

Another striking example from the 1945 broadcast is Mrs Ogmore-Pritchard who later appears as a major character in ''Under Milk Wood'':

:Open the curtains, light the fire, what are servants for?
:I am Mrs. Ogmore-Pritchard and I want another snooze.
:Dust the china, feed the canary, sweep the drawing-room floor;
:And before you let the sun in, mind he wipes his shoes.

Mrs Ogmore Davies<ref>Annie Ogmore Davies was the wife of Thomas Ogmore Davies whose drapery shop, Bon Marché, stood at the top of Church Street, next to the Queens Hotel</ref> and Mrs Pritchard-Jones<ref>Eluned Pritchard Jones was the wife of Barclays bank manager, Richard Pritchard-Jones, Bank House, Church Street</ref> both lived on Church Street in New Quay.<ref>Church Street:1945 Register of Electors, with a photo online at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/a-postcard-from-new-quay?authuser=0 Mrs Ogmore Pritchard]</ref> Mrs Pritchard-Jones was constantly cleaning, recalled one of her neighbours, "a real matron-type, very strait-laced, house-proud, ran the house like a hospital ward."<ref>Phyllis Cosmo-Jones, interviewed c1999, in D. N. Thomas, ''A Postcard from New Quay'', in H. Ellis, ed. (2014) ''Dylan Thomas: A Centenary Celebration'', Bloomsbury, and online at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/a-postcard-from-new-quay?authuser=0 Pritchard-Jones].</ref> In her book on New Quay, Mrs Pritchard-Jones' daughter notes that her mother had been a Queen's Nurse before her marriage and afterwards "devoted much of her time to cleaning and dusting our home ... sliding a small mat under our feet so we would not bring in any dirt from the road."<ref>C. Edwards-Jones (2013), ''New Quay Wales Remembered'', pp. 60–61, Book Guild Publishing.</ref>

Jack Lloyd, a New Quay postman and the Town Crier, also lived on Church Street.<ref>Jack Lloyd was John Lloyd Evans and he lived at Noddfa (now Trenova) at the upper end of Church Street, opposite the Queens Hotel. See S. Passmore (2015), ''The Streets of New Quay'', p. 199, Lulu Press, and the Register of Electors 1945.</ref> He provided the character of Llareggub's postman Willy Nilly, whose practice of opening letters, and spreading the news, reflects Lloyd's role as Town Crier, as Thomas himself noted on a work sheet for the play: "Nobody minds him opening the letters and acting as [a] kind of town-crier. How else could they know the news?"<ref>See Davies, W. and Maud, R. eds.(1995),&nbsp;''Under Milk Wood: the Definitive Edition'', p. xxxvi, Everyman. In her book on New Quay, former resident, Catrin Edwards-Jones, gives examples of the local news that Jack Lloyd would cry out e.g. forthcoming local events such as a singing concert or a meeting in the Memorial Hall (''New Quay Wales Remembered'' (2013), Book Guild Publishing, pp. 28–29).</ref> It is this note, together with our knowledge that Thomas knew Jack Lloyd ("an old friend"),<ref>"Jack the Post is an old friend..." Letter to Margaret Taylor of 29 August 1946 in ''Collected Letters''</ref> that establish the link between Willy Nilly and Lloyd.<ref>For more on Jack Lloyd, see note 9 in ''A Postcard from New Quay'' online at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/a-postcard-from-new-quay?authuser=0 Postcard from New Quay]</ref>

Third Drowned's question at the beginning of the play, "How's the tenors in Dowlais?", reflects the special relationship that once existed between New Quay and [[Dowlais]], an industrial town in South Wales. (2000), ''A Farm, Two Mansions and a Bungalow'', Seren, p. 82.</ref> as does the stopped clock in the bar of the Sailors' Arms.<ref>Thomas writes about the stopped clock in the Black Lion bar in his letter about New Quay of 29 August 1946, to Margaret Taylor. See ''Collected Letters''.</ref><ref>For more on New Quay names in the play, see Thomas, D. N. (2000), op. cit. pp. 206–216, and online at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/a-postcard-from-new-quay?authuser=0 New Quay names] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221016172226/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/a-postcard-from-new-quay?authuser=0 |date=16 October 2022 }}</ref>

Walford Davies has concluded that New Quay "was crucial in supplementing the gallery of characters Thomas had to hand for writing ''Under Milk Wood''.<ref>Davies, W., and R. Maud, eds (1995),&nbsp;''Under Milk Wood: the Definitive Edition'', p. xvii, Everyman.</ref> FitzGibbon had come to a similar conclusion many years earlier, noting that Llareggub "resembles New Quay more closely [than Laugharne] and many of the characters derive from that seaside village in Cardiganshire..."<ref>The ''Life of Dylan Thomas'', 1965 p. 237, Little Brown</ref> John Ackerman has also suggested that the story of the drowned village and graveyard of Llanina, that lay in the sea below Majoda, "is the literal truth that inspired the imaginative and poetic truth" of ''Under Milk Wood''.<ref>Ackerman, J. (1979), ''Welsh Dylan'', Seren, p. 127. For more on Llanina, see the Wikipedia entry for New Quay.</ref> Another part of that literal truth were the 60 acres of cliff between New Quay and Majoda, including Maesgwyn farm, that collapsed into the sea in the early 1940s.<ref>See Thomas, D. N. (2000), pp. 212–213, ''Dylan Thomas: A Farm, Two Mansions and a Bungalow'', Seren, and Passmore S. C. (2015), p. 470, ''The Streets of New Quay'', Lulu Press, with a photo of Maesgwyn on p. 468. Passmore dates the collapse of Maesgwyn to 1940.</ref>

===Elba, South Leigh and Prague===
In April 1947, Thomas and family went to Italy. He intended to write a radio play there, as his letters home make clear.<ref>See the letters of 24 May 29 May, 5 June, ''Collected Letters''.</ref>  Several words and phrases that appear in ''Under Milk Wood'' can be found in some of Thomas' letters from the island of [[Elba]], where he stayed for three weeks. The "fishers and miners" and "webfooted waterboys" <ref name="auto">See his letter of 3 August 1947 from Elba, ''Collected Letters''.</ref> of the letters become the "fishers" and "webfoot cocklewomen" of the first page of ''Under Milk Wood''.<ref>The "cocklewomen webfoot" also appear in Thomas' letter to Margaret Taylor written in South Leigh in October 1948, ''Collected Letters''.</ref> The "sunblack" and "fly-black" adjectives of Elba anticipate the "crowblack" and "bible-black" descriptions of Llareggub. The play's Fourth Drowned, Alfred Pomeroy Jones, "died of blisters", and so, almost, did Thomas, as he vividly describes in a letter home.<ref name="auto"/>  And, in time, the island's "blister-biting blimp-blue bakehouse sea" would re-appear as Llareggub's "slow, black, crowblack, fishingboat-bobbing sea."<ref>See his letter from Elba of 3 August 1947, Collected Letters.</ref>

On their return from Italy in August 1947, the Thomases moved to [[South Leigh]], near [[Witney]] in Oxfordshire, where Thomas declared his intent to work further on the play.<ref>See his letter to his parents, 19 July 1947, which reads: "I want very much to write a full-length – hour to an hour & a half – broadcast play; & hope to do it, in South Leigh, this autumn." And another to John Ormond 6 March 1948: "A radio play I am writing has Laugharne, though not by name, as its setting."</ref>  It was here that he knocked the play into shape, as one biographer described it.<ref>Lycett, A. (2004), ''Thomas Untutored'' in ''Oxford Today'', 16, 2, Hilary.</ref> There are various accounts of his work on the play at South Leigh, where he lived until May 1949.<ref>Thomas, D. N. (2004), ''Dylan Remembered 1934–1953'', Seren, pp. 294–295, and also at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood South Leigh.] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260415044526/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood |date=15 April 2026 }}</ref> He also worked on filmscripts here, including ''[[The Three Weird Sisters]]'', which includes the familiar Llareggub names of Daddy Waldo and Polly Probert.

Just a month or so after moving to South Leigh, Thomas met the [[BBC]] producer, [[Philip Burton (theatre director)|Philip Burton]], in the Café Royal in London, where he outlined his ideas for "''The Village of the Mad''…a coastal town in south Wales which was on trial because they felt it was a disaster to have a community living in that way… For instance, the organist in the choir in the church played with only the dog to listen to him…A man and a woman were in love with each other but they never met… they wrote to each other every day…And he had the idea that the narrator should be like the listener, blind.…"<ref>(1) Burton, P. (1953), untitled, ''Dylan Thomas Memorial Number'' in ''Adam International Review''. (2) Tape recorded interview in the Jeff Towns Collection. (3) Letters to Douglas Cleverdon, 9 October 1967 and 26 February 1968, in the Cleverdon archive, Lilly Library, University of Indiana, and reproduced at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/under-milk-wood-a-play-for-ears?authuser=0 Burton and Thomas.] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250707014248/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/under-milk-wood-a-play-for-ears?authuser=0 |date=7 July 2025 }}</ref> Burton's friendship with Thomas, and his influence on the play, has been set within the context of the work done by Burton and [[Thomas Rowland Hughes|T. Rowland Hughes]] in developing community portraiture on the radio.<ref>Thomas, D. N. (2020), ''Under Milk Wood: A Play for Ears. Some Reflections on T. Rowland Hughes, Philip Burton and Dylan Thomas'', ''New Welsh Review'', May. And online at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/under-milk-wood-a-play-for-ears Burton and Thomas]</ref>

Thomas went to [[Prague]] in March 1949 for a writers' conference. His guide and interpreter, [[Jiřina Hauková]], has recalled that, at a party, Thomas "narrated the first version of his radio play ''Under Milk Wood''". She mentions that he talked about the organist who played to goats and sheep, as well as a baker with two wives.<ref>The lines about Organ Morgan playing for nobody and playing for sheep are both found at the very end of the play. See Davies and Maud op. cit. p. 61.</ref> Another at the party remembered that Thomas also talked about the two Voices.<ref>Thomas,  D. N. (2004), ''Dylan Remembered 1934–1953'', Seren, pp. 160–164 and 295–296, and also at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood/ milk wood Prague] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221023094233/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood/ |date=23 October 2022 }}. Taken from Hauková's Memoirs: ''Záblesky života'' (1996), H&H, Jinočany, and translated at Thomas, D. N. (2004), p. 163. The information about Thomas reading the first version of ''Under Milk Wood'' in Prague in March 1949 was first published by Fitzgibbon in his 1965 biography of Thomas (p. 304), after receiving a letter from Hauková.</ref>

The testimony from Prague, when taken with that of Burton about the meeting in the Café Royal in 1947, indicates that several of the characters of the play were already in place by the time Thomas had moved to the Boat House in Laugharne in May 1949: the organist, the two lovers who never met but wrote to each other, the baker with two wives, the blind narrator and the Voices.

The first known sighting of a script for the play was its first half, titled ''The Town that was Mad'', which Thomas showed to the poet [[Allen Curnow]] in October 1949 at the Boat House.<ref>Curnow, A. (1982), ''Images of Dylan'', ''NZ Listener'', 8 December.</ref>

A draft first half of the play was delivered to the BBC in late October 1950.<ref>''Collected Letters'', p. 860.</ref> It consisted of thirty-five handwritten pages containing most of the places, people and topography of Llareggub, and which ended with the line "Organ Morgan's at it early…" A shortened version of this first half was published in ''[[Botteghe Oscure]]'' in May 1952 with the title ''Llareggub. A Piece for Radio Perhaps''. By the end of that year, Thomas had been in Laugharne for just over three years, but his half-play had made little progress since his South Leigh days. On 6 November 1952, he wrote to the editor of ''Botteghe Oscure'' to explain why he hadn't been able to "finish the second half of my piece for you." He had failed shamefully, he said, to add to "my lonely half of a looney maybe-play".<ref>See his various letters 1950–1953 about failing to work on the play e.g. his 6 January 1953 letter to Gwyn Jones: "I've been terribly busy failing to write one word of a more or less play set in a Wales that I'm sad to say never was…" He wrote to Charles Fry in February 1953, complaining that, apart from the 'Prologue' for ''Collected Poems'', he had not been able to write anything for a whole year. A memo from David Higham, his agent, also in February 1953, noted tersely: "He hasn't made any progress on the Llareggub things."</ref>

=== America ===
Thomas gave a reading of the unfinished play to students at [[Cardiff University]] in March 1953.<ref>On 10 March, at the university. For an account by the journalist Alan Road, see Thomas, D. N. (2004), ''Dylan Remembered 1935–1953'', p. 302.</ref> He  travelled to the United States in April to give the first public readings of the play, even though he had not yet written its second half. He gave a solo reading of the first half on 3 May at the [[Harvard Art Museums#Fogg Museum|Fogg Museum, Harvard]], sponsored by The Poets' Theatre, where the audience responded enthusiastically.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.poetstheatre.org/our-history |title=Our History |publisher=Poets Theatre|access-date=7 Oct 2024}}</ref><ref>For an account, see Thomas, D. N. ''op. cit.'' p. 304.</ref>  

Rehearsals for the play's official premiere on 14 May in New York City had already started but with only half the play, and with Thomas unavailable as he left to carry out a series of poetry readings and other engagements. He was up at dawn on 14 May to work on the second half, and he continued writing on the train between Boston and New York, as he travelled to the [[92nd Street Y]]'s Poetry Center for the premiere. With the performance just 90 minutes away, the "final third of the play was still unorganised and but partially written."<ref>Brinnin, J. (1955), ''Dylan Thomas in America'', Avon, p. 213.</ref> The play's producer, Liz Reitell, locked Thomas in a room to continue work on the script, the last few lines of which were handed to the actors as they were preparing to go on stage.<ref>Brinnin, J. (1955), ''Dylan Thomas in America'', Avon, pp. 211–213.</ref> Thomas subsequently added some 40 new lines to the second half for the play's next reading in New York on 28 May.

On his return to Laugharne, Thomas worked in a desultory fashion on ''Under Milk Wood'' throughout the summer.<ref>Desultory: Davies and Maud, ''op.cit''. p. 82.</ref> His daughter, Aeronwy, noticed that his health had "visibly deteriorated. ... I could hear his racking cough. Every morning he had a prolonged coughing attack. ... The coughing was nothing new but it seemed worse than before."<ref>A. Thomas (2009), ''My Father's Places'', Constable, p. 199.</ref> She also noted that the blackouts that Thomas was experiencing were "a constant source of comment" amongst his Laugharne friends.<ref>A. Thomas ''op.cit''. p204.</ref>

Thomas gave readings of the play in [[Porthcawl]] and [[Tenby]],<ref>For information on both these readings, see Thomas, D. N. (2004) ''op.cit.'' p. 306.</ref> before travelling to London to catch his plane to New York for another tour, including three readings of ''Under Milk Wood''. He stayed with the comedian [[Harry Locke]], and worked on the play, re-writing parts of the first half, and writing Eli Jenkins' sunset poem and Waldo's chimney sweep song for the second half.<ref>Thomas, D. N. ''op. cit.'' p. 306.</ref> Locke noticed that Thomas was very chesty, with "terrible" coughing fits that made him go purple in the face.<ref>Thomas, D. N. 2008, p. 46</ref>

On 15 October 1953, Thomas delivered another draft of the play to the [[BBC]], a draft that his producer, Douglas Cleverdon, described as being in "an extremely disordered state...it was clearly not in its final form."<ref>Cleverdon, D. (1969), ''The Growth of Milk Wood'', pp. 35–38.</ref> On his arrival in New York on 20 October 1953, Thomas added a further 38 lines to the second half, for the two performances on 24 and 25 October.

Thomas had been met at the airport by Liz Reitell, who was shocked at his appearance: "He was very ill when he got here."<ref name="Thomas, D. N. 2008 p57">Thomas, D. N. (2008), p. 57.</ref> Thomas's agent [[John Brinnin]], deeply in debt and desperate for money, also knew Thomas was very ill, but did not cancel or curtail his programme. He had a punishing schedule of four rehearsals and two performances of ''Under Milk Wood'' in just five days, as well as two sessions of revising the play.<ref>Thomas, D. N. (2008), ''Fatal Neglect: Who Killed Dylan Thomas?'', Seren, pp. 58–67.</ref> After the first performance on 24 October, Thomas was close to collapse, standing in his dressing room, clinging to the back of a chair. The play, he said, "has taken the life out of me for now."<ref>Becker, R., ''A Memoir'', in the Constantine Fitzgibbon archive, Harry Ransom Centre, [[University of Texas at Austin]].</ref>

At the next performance, the actors realised that Thomas was very ill and had lost his voice: "He was desperately ill … we didn't think that he would be able to do the last performance because he was so ill … Dylan literally couldn't speak he was so ill … still my greatest memory of it is that he had no voice."<ref>Nancy Wickwire, one of the cast. See [https://sites.google.com/view/dylanthomasdeathbyneglect/a-chronology-of-a-death?authuser=0 Thomas desperately ill]</ref> After a cortisone injection, he recovered sufficiently to go on stage. The play's cast noticed Thomas's worsening illness during the first three rehearsals, during one of which he collapsed. Brinnin was at the fourth and was shocked by Thomas' appearance: "I could barely stop myself from gasping aloud. His face was lime-white, his lips loose and twisted, his eyes dulled, gelid, and sunk in his head."<ref>Brinnin, J. (1955), ''Dylan Thomas in America'', Avon, p. 258.</ref>

Through the following week, Thomas continued to work on the script for the version that was to appear in ''[[Mademoiselle (magazine)|Mademoiselle]]'', and for the performance in [[Chicago]] on 13 November. However, he collapsed in the early hours of 5 November and died in hospital on 9 November 1953.

===Inspiration===
The sources for the play have generated intense debate. Thomas himself declared on two occasions that his play was based on Laugharne,<ref>Letters to John Ormond 6 March 1948 and Princess Caetani, October 1951.</ref> but this has not gone unquestioned. The towns of [[Llansteffan]], [[Ferryside]] and particularly New Quay also have made their claims. An examination of these respective claims was published in 2004.<ref>Thomas, D. N. (2004), ''Dylan Remembered 1935–1953'', chapter 2, Seren, and at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood the play's inspiration] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260415044526/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood |date=15 April 2026 }}</ref> Surprisingly little scholarship has been devoted to Thomas and Laugharne, and about the town's influence on the writing of ''Under Milk Wood''.<ref>But see (1) Davies, J. A. (2000), ''Dylan Thomas's Swansea, Gower and Laugharne'', University of Wales Press. (2) Tregenna, J. (2014), ''If He Were Still With Us (Dylan's Laugharne – Still Strange),'' Bloomsbury. (3) Lewis, M. (1967), ''Laugharne and Dylan Thomas'', Dobson.</ref> Thomas's four years at the Boat House were amongst his least productive, and he was away for much of the time. As his daughter, Aeronwy, has recalled, "he sought any pretext to escape."<ref>Of his four years at the Boat House, more than a quarter were spent in America and Persia, and much of the rest in London or travelling the country to read poetry or to broadcast on the radio. Aeronwy's comment: A. Thomas (2009), ''My Father's Places'', Constable, p. 21.</ref>

Douglas Cleverdon has suggested that the [[topography]] of Llareggub "is based not so much on Laugharne, which lies on the mouth of an estuary, but rather on New Quay, a seaside town...with a steep street running down to the harbour."<ref>Cleverdon, D. (1969), ''The Growth of Milk Wood'', Dent, p. 4.</ref> The various topographical references in the play to the 'top of the town,' and to its 'top and sea-end' are also suggestive of New Quay, as are Llareggub's terraced streets and hill of windows.<ref>There are photos of the terraces and the windowed-hill at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/majoda-and-other-photos?authuser=0 Photos of New Quay.]</ref> The play is true to even the minor topographical details of New Quay. For example, Llareggub's lazy fishermen walk ''uphill'' from the harbour to the Sailors' Arms.

Thomas drew a sketch map of the fictional town, which is now held by the [[National Library of Wales]] and can be viewed online.<ref>[http://www.llgc.org.uk/index.php?id=dylanthomasandthemapofllar Dylan's Llareggub map at The National Library of Wales's website] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101206055829/http://www.llgc.org.uk/index.php?id=dylanthomasandthemapofllar |date=6 December 2010 }}.</ref> The Dylan Thomas scholar, James Davies, has written that "Thomas's drawing of Llareggub is... based on New Quay"<ref>Davies. J. A. (2000), ''Dylan Thomas's Swansea, Gower and Laugharne'', pp. 103–104, University of Wales Press.</ref> and there has been very little disagreement, if any, with this view. An examination of the sketch has revealed some interesting features: Thomas uses the name of an actual New Quay resident, Dan Cherry Jones, for one of the people living in Cockle Street. The Rev. Eli Jenkins is not in the sketch, however, and there are also three characters in the sketch who do not appear in the draft of the play given by Thomas to the BBC in October 1950.<ref>See [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/dylans-sketch-of-llareggub?authuser=0 Analysis of Dylan's Sketch of Llareggub] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210727124740/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/dylans-sketch-of-llareggub?authuser=0 |date=27 July 2021 }} First published on the Discover Dylan Thomas website, September 2021.</ref>

Thomas seems to have drawn on New Quay in developing Llareggub's profile as an ocean-going, schooner and harbour town, as he once described it.<ref>On a work sheet for the play, quoted in Davies and Maud (1999), p. 100.</ref> Captain Cat lives in Schooner House. He and his sailors have sailed the clippered seas, as First Voice puts it. They have been to San Francisco, Nantucket and more, bringing back coconuts and parrots for their families. The Rev. Eli Jenkins' White Book of Llareggub has a chapter on shipping and another on industry, all of which reflect New Quay's history of both producing master mariners<ref>In the 1939 War Register, for example, there were 58 sailors (of whom 30 were merchant master mariners) listed for New Quay but only four sailors for Laugharne, none of whom was a master mariner. Over the long-term (1850–1927), 1380 master mariner and mate certificates were issued by the Board of Trade to New Quay men, but only 21 to men from Laugharne. (These are the numbers of certificates issued, not the number of men. A sailor might acquire a number of certificates on his way to becoming a master. In Laugharne, for example, the 21 certificates were gained by ten men.) For more on this see Thomas, D. N. (2019)&nbsp;''Llareggub and the 1939 War Register'', at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/Llareggub-and-the-1939-war-register?authuser=0 Llareggub-and-the-1939-War-Register.] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220522162454/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/Llareggub-and-the-1939-war-register?authuser=0 |date=22 May 2022 }}</ref> and building ocean-going ships, including schooners.<ref>Ship building in New Quay: mainly brigs, barques and schooners. For example, some 99 schooners were built in New Quay between 1848 and 1870. Overall, more than 200 ships were built there between 1779 and 1890. There were six shipyards on the beach at New Quay, with as many smithies in the town, a foundry, and sail and rope makers. There are a number of publications about the town's maritime history and residents: (1) S. Passmore (2012)&nbsp;''Farmers and Figureheads: the Port of New Quay and its Hinterland'', Grosvenor House. (2) R. Bryan (2012)&nbsp;''New Quay: A History in Pictures'', Llanina Books. (3) W. J. Lewis (1987), ''New Quay and Llanarth'', Aberystwyth.</ref> In his 1947 visit to New Quay, Walter Wilkinson noted that the town "abounds" in sea captains <ref>Wilkinson, W. (1948), ''Puppets in Wales'', p. 131, Bles.</ref> The following year, another writer visiting New Quay noted that there were "dozens of lads who knew intimately the life and ways of all the great maritime cities of the world."<ref>D. Parry-Jones (1948), ''Welsh Country Upbringing'', Batsford.</ref>

Llareggub's occupational profile as a town of seafarers, fishermen, cockle gatherers and farmers has also been examined through an analysis of the returns in the [[National Registration Act 1939|1939 War Register]] for New Quay, Laugharne, Ferryside and Llansteffan. This analysis also draws upon census returns and the Welsh Merchant Mariners Index. It shows that New Quay and Ferryside provide by far the best fit with Llareggub's occupational profile.<ref>Thomas, D. N. (2019), [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/Llareggub-and-the-1939-war-register Llareggub-and-the-1939-war-register ''Llareggub and the 1939 War Register''.] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190419173223/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/Llareggub-and-the-1939-war-register |date=19 April 2019 }} First published on the Discover Dylan Thomas website, September 2019</ref>

Thomas is reported to have commented that ''Under Milk Wood'' was developed in response to the [[Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki|atomic bombing of Hiroshima]], as a way of reasserting the evidence of beauty in the world.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.berkeleydailyplanet.com/issue/2010-12-22/article/37024?headline=Review-Under-Milk-Wood-at-the-Library--By-Dorothy-Snodgrass|title=Review: Under Milk Wood at the Library|last=Snodgrass|first=Dorothy|date=21 December 2010|website=The Berkeley Daily Planet|access-date=22 March 2016|archive-date=2 February 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170202001111/http://www.berkeleydailyplanet.com/issue/2010-12-22/article/37024?headline=Review-Under-Milk-Wood-at-the-Library--By-Dorothy-Snodgrass|url-status=live}}</ref> It is also thought that the play was a response by Thomas both to the Nazi concentration camps, and to the internment camps that had been created around Britain during World War II.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/dylan-and-the-town-that-was-mad|title=Dylan Thomas: A Postcard from New Quay – From Laugharne to Rio Marina: Towns that were Mad|website=sites.google.com|access-date=13 August 2018|archive-date=19 April 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190419181031/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/dylan-and-the-town-that-was-mad|url-status=live}}</ref>

==Llareggub==
[[File:Llareggub boat (cropped).jpg|thumb|A boat bearing the name of the fictional location of ''Under Milk Wood'']]
The fictional name ''Llareggub'' was derived by reversing the phrase "[[bugger all]]".<ref>{{Cite book|last=Brinnin |first=J. |title=Dylan Thomas in America|publisher=Avon |year=1955|pages=132}}</ref> In some published editions of the play,<ref>See, for example, the Project Gutenberg edition online.</ref> it is often rendered (contrary to Thomas's own use – see below) as ''Llaregyb'' or similar. It is pronounced {{IPA|cy|ɬaˈrɛɡɪb|}}.<ref>For example see {{youTube|JgMRD84MTQY|Richard Burton reads Dylan Thomas – from 'Under Milk Wood': Rev. Eli Jenkins' poem}} by Welsh actor [[Richard Burton]], at time 0:39.</ref> The name bears some resemblance to many actual [[List of towns in Wales#L|Welsh place names]], which often begin with ''Llan'', meaning church or, more correctly, [[Llan (placename)|sanctified enclosure]], although a double g is not used in written [[Welsh language|Welsh]].

The name Llareggub was first used by Thomas in two short stories published in 1936. They were ''The Orchards''<ref>Dated October 1934 in the Red Notebook and published in ''The Criterion'' in July 1936.</ref> ("This was a story more terrible than the stories of the reverend madmen in the Black Book of Llareggub.") and ''The Burning Baby''<ref>''The Burning Baby''. Dated October 1934 in the "Red Notebook" and first published in the magazine ''Contemporary Poetry and Prose,'' issue for May 1936. This information listed by [[Walford Davies]] in ''Dylan Thomas: Collected Stories''. Phoenix, 2000.</ref> ("Death took hold of his sister's legs as she walked through the calf-high heather up the hill... She was to him as ugly as the sowfaced woman Llareggub who had taught him the terrors of the flesh.")

Thomas' first known use of the name Llareggub in relation to ''Under Milk Wood'' was at a recitation of an early version of the play at a party in London in 1945.<ref>Davin, D. (1985), ''Closing Times'', Oxford University Press, p. 126, and online at [https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood "The Birth of Under Milk Wood".] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260415044526/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood |date=15 April 2026 }}</ref>

Thomas had also referred to the play as ''The Village of the Mad'' or ''The Town that was Mad''.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood?authuser=0|title=The Birth of Under Milk Wood|website=A Postcard from New Quay|access-date=11 November 2021|archive-date=11 November 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211111164601/https://sites.google.com/site/dylanthomasandnewquay/birth-of-under-milk-wood?authuser=0|url-status=live}}</ref> By the summer of 1951, he was calling the play ''Llareggub Hill''<ref>J. Brinnin (1955), ''Dylan Thomas in America'', Avon, p. 132.</ref> but by October 1951, when the play was sent to ''Botteghe Oscure,''<ref>Letter to Princess Caetani, October 1951, ''Collected Letters.''</ref> its title had become ''Llareggub. A Piece for Radio Perhaps''. By the summer of 1952, the title was changed to ''Under Milk Wood'' because John Brinnin thought ''Llareggub Hill'' would be too thick and forbidding to attract American audiences.<ref>{{Cite book|last=Brinnin|first=J. |title=Dylan Thomas in America|publisher=Avon |year=1955|pages=187}}</ref>

In the play, the Rev Eli Jenkins writes a poem that describes Llareggub Hill and its "mystic [[tumulus]]". This was based on a lyrical description of [[Twmbarlwm]]'s "mystic tumulus" in [[Monmouthshire (historic)|Monmouthshire]] that Thomas imitated from [[Arthur Machen]]'s autobiography ''Far Off Things'' (1922).<ref>Dylan Thomas, ''Under Milk Wood, The Definitive Edition'' (Dent: 1995), p. 91.</ref>

The town's name is thought to be the inspiration for the country of Llamedos (sod 'em all) in Terry Pratchett's ''[[Discworld]]'' novel ''[[Soul Music (novel)|Soul Music]]''.<ref>[[Soul Music (novel)|Soul Music wiki]]</ref> In this setting, Llamedos is a parody of Wales.

==Plot==
The play opens at night, when the citizens of Llareggub are asleep. The narrator (First Voice/Second Voice) informs the audience that they are witnessing the townspeople's dreams.

Captain Cat, the blind sea captain, is tormented in his dreams by his drowned shipmates, who long to live again and enjoy the pleasures of the world. Mog Edwards and Myfanwy Price dream of each other; Mr. Waldo dreams of his childhood and his failed marriages; Mrs. Ogmore-Pritchard dreams of her deceased husbands. Almost all of the characters in the play are introduced as the audience witnesses a moment of their dreams.

Morning begins. The voice of a guide introduces the town, discussing the facts of Llareggub.  The Reverend Eli Jenkins delivers a morning sermon on his love for the village. Lily Smalls wakes and bemoans her pitiful existence. Mr. and Mrs. Pugh observe their neighbours; the characters introduce themselves as they act in their morning. Mrs. Cherry Owen merrily rehashes her husband's drunken antics. Butcher Beynon teases his wife during breakfast. Captain Cat watches as Willy Nilly the postman goes about his morning rounds, delivering to Mrs. Ogmore-Pritchard, Mrs. Pugh, Mog Edwards and Mr. Waldo.

At Mrs. Organ-Morgan's general shop, women gossip about the townspeople. Willy Nilly and his wife steam open a love letter from Mog Edwards to Myfanwy Price; he expresses fear that he may be in the poor house if his business does not improve. Mrs. Dai Bread Two swindles Mrs. Dai Bread One with a bogus fortune in her crystal ball. Polly Garter scrubs floors and sings about her past paramours. Children play in the schoolyard; Gwennie urges the boys to "kiss her where she says or give her a penny." Gossamer Beynon and Sinbad Sailors privately desire each other.

During dinner, Mr. Pugh imagines poisoning Mrs. Pugh. Mrs. Organ-Morgan shares the day's gossip with her husband, but his only interest is the organ. The audience sees a glimpse of Lord Cut-Glass's insanity in his "kitchen full of time". Captain Cat dreams of his lost lover, Rosie Probert, but weeps as he remembers that she will not be with him again. Nogood Boyo fishes in the bay, dreaming of Mrs. Dai Bread Two and geishas.

On Llareggub Hill, Mae Rose Cottage spends a lazy afternoon wishing for love. Reverend Jenkins works on the White Book of Llareggub, which is a history of the entire town and its citizens. On the farm, Utah Watkins struggles with his cattle, aided by Bessie Bighead. As Mrs. Ogmore-Pritchard falls asleep, her husbands return to her. Mae Rose Cottage swears that she will sin until she explodes.

[[File:The Seahorse Inn, previously known as the Sailor's Home Arms - Geograph-2675847.jpg|thumb|The Sailor's Home Arms, [[New Quay]], now known as the Seahorse Inn, which provided the name for the Sailors Arms<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.newquay-westwales.co.uk/trail.htm|title=The Dylan Thomas Trail at New Quay|website=www.newquay-westwales.co.uk|access-date=8 June 2012|archive-date=30 May 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140530050100/http://newquay-westwales.co.uk/trail.htm|url-status=dead}}</ref>]]

As night begins, Reverend Jenkins recites another poem. Cherry Owen heads to the Sailor's Arms, where Sinbad still longs for Gossamer Beynon. The town prepares for the evening, to sleep or otherwise. Mr. Waldo sings drunkenly at the Sailors Arms. Captain Cat sees his drowned shipmates—and Rosie—as he begins to sleep. Organ-Morgan mistakes Cherry Owen for [[Bach|Johann Sebastian Bach]] on his way to the chapel. Mog and Myfanwy write to each other before sleeping. Mr. Waldo meets Polly Garter in a forest. Night begins and the citizens of Llareggub return to their dreams again.

==Characters==
*'''Captain Cat''' – The old blind sea captain who dreams of his deceased shipmates and lost lover Rosie Probert. He is one of the play's most important characters as he often acts as a narrator. He comments on the goings-on in the village from his window.
*'''Rosie Probert''' – Captain Cat's deceased lover, who appears in his dreams.
*'''Myfanwy Price''' – The sweetshop-keeper who dreams of marrying Mog Edwards.
*'''Mr. Mog Edwards''' – The draper, enamoured of Myfanwy Price.  Their romance, however, is restricted strictly to the letters they write one another and their interactions in their dreams.
*'''Jack Black''' – The cobbler, who dreams of scaring away young couples.
*'''Evans the Death''' – The [[undertaker]], who dreams of his childhood.
*'''Mr. Waldo''' – Rabbit catcher, barber, [[herbalist]], cat doctor, [[Quackery|quack]], dreams of his mother and his many unhappy, failed marriages. He is a notorious alcoholic and general troublemaker and is involved in an affair with Polly Garter.
*'''Mrs. Ogmore-Pritchard''' – The owner of a guesthouse, who dreams of nagging her two late husbands. She refuses to let anyone stay at the guesthouse because of her extreme penchant for neatness.
*'''Mr. Ogmore''' – Deceased, [[Linoleum]] salesman, late of Mrs. Ogmore-Pritchard.
*'''Mr. Pritchard''' – Deceased, failed [[bookmaker]], late of Mrs. Ogmore-Pritchard.  He committed suicide "ironically" by ingesting disinfectant.
*'''Gossamer Beynon''' – The schoolteacher (daughter of Butcher Beynon), dreams of a fox-like illicit love.  During the day, she longs to be with Sinbad Sailors, but the two never interact.
*'''Organ Morgan''' – The church organ player has perturbed dreams of music and orchestras within the village.  His obsession with music bothers his wife intensely.
*'''Mrs. Organ Morgan''' – A shop owner who dreams of "silence," as she is disturbed during the day by Organ Morgan's constant organ-playing.
*'''Mr. & Mrs. Floyd''' – The cocklers, an elderly couple, seemingly the only couple to sleep peacefully in the village. They are mentioned only during the dream sequence and when Mrs Floyd is "talking flatfish" with Nogood Boyo.
*'''Utah Watkins''' – The farmer, dreams of counting sheep that resemble his wife.
*'''Ocky Milkman''' – The [[milkman]], dreams of pouring his milk into a river, 'regardless of expense'.
*'''Mr. Cherry Owen''' – Dreams of drinking and yet is unable to, as the tankard turns into a fish, which he drinks.
*'''Mrs. Cherry Owen''' – Cherry Owen's devoted wife, who cares for him and delights in rehashing his drunken antics.
*'''Police Constable Attila Rees''' – The policeman, relieves himself into his helmet at night, knowing somehow he will regret this in the morning.
*'''Mr. Willy Nilly''' – The postman, dreams of delivering the post in his sleep, and physically knocks upon his wife as if knocking upon a door. In the morning they open the post together and read the town's news so that he can relay it around the village.
*'''Mrs. Willy Nilly''' – who, because of her husband's knocking upon her, dreams of being spanked by her teacher for being late for school. She assists Willy Nilly in steaming open the mail.
*'''Mary Ann Sailors''' – 85 years old, dreams of the Garden of Eden.  During the day she announces her age ("I'm 85 years, 3 months and a day!") to the town.
*'''Sinbad Sailors''' – The barman, dreams of Gossamer Beynon, whom he cannot marry because of his grandmother's disapproval.
*'''Mae Rose Cottage''' – Seventeen and never been kissed, she dreams of meeting her "Mr. Right". She spends the day in the fields daydreaming and unseen, draws lipstick circles around her nipples.
*'''Bessie Bighead''' – Hired help, dreams of the one man that kissed her "because he was dared".
*'''Butcher Beynon''' – The butcher, dreams of riding pigs and shooting wild [[giblets]]. During the day he enjoys teasing his wife about the questionable meat that he sells.
*'''Mrs. Butcher Beynon''' – Butcher Beynon's wife, dreams of her husband being 'persecuted' for selling "owl's meat, dogs' eyes, manchop."
*'''Rev. Eli Jenkins''' – The reverend, poet and preacher, dreams of [[Eisteddfod]]au. Author of the White Book of Llareggub.
*'''Mr. Pugh''' – Schoolmaster, dreams of poisoning his domineering wife. He purchases a book named "Lives of the Great Poisoners" for ideas on how to kill Mrs. Pugh; however, he does not do it.
*'''Mrs. Pugh''' – The nasty and undesirable wife of Mr. Pugh.
*'''Dai Bread''' – The bigamist baker who dreams of harems.
*'''Mrs. Dai Bread One''' – Dai Bread's first wife, traditional and plain.
*'''Mrs. Dai Bread Two''' – Dai Bread's second wife, a mysterious and sultry gypsy.
*'''Polly Garter''' – has affairs with married men of the village, and a young mother, who dreams of her many babies. During the day, she scrubs floors and sings of her lost love.
*'''Nogood Boyo''' – A lazy young fisherman who dreams peevishly of "nothing", though he later fantasises about Mrs. Dai Bread Two in a wet corset. He is known for causing shenanigans in the wash house.
*'''Lord Cut-Glass''' – A man of questionable sanity, who dreams of the 66 clocks that he keeps in his house, all telling different times.
*'''Lily Smalls''' – Dreams of love and a fantasy life. She is the Beynons' maid, but longs for a more exciting life.
*'''Gwennie''' – A child in Llareggub, who insists that her male schoolmates "kiss her where she says or give her a penny".

==Publication and translation==

The first publication of ''Under Milk Wood'', a shortened version of the first half of the play, appeared in [[Botteghe Oscure]] in April 1952.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.peterharrington.co.uk/blog/the-town-that-was-mad-dylan-thomas-under-milk-wood/|title=The Town That Was Mad: Dylan Thomas' Under Milk Wood|date=14 February 2020|access-date=6 March 2023|archive-date=6 March 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230306165313/https://www.peterharrington.co.uk/blog/the-town-that-was-mad-dylan-thomas-under-milk-wood/|url-status=live}}</ref> Two years later, in February 1954, both [[The Observer]] newspaper and [[Mademoiselle (magazine)|Mademoiselle]] magazine published abridged versions.<ref>R. Maud (1970), ''Dylan Thomas in Print'', Pittsburgh</ref> The first publications of the complete play were also in 1954: J. M. Dent in London in February and New Directions in America in April.

An Acting Edition of the play was published by Dent in 1958. The Definitive Edition, with one Voice, came out in 1995, edited by Walford Davies and [[Ralph Maud]] and published by Dent. A Project Gutenberg of Australia eBook for free use went online in November 2006, produced by Colin Choat.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://gutenberg.net.au/ebooks06/0608221h.html|title=Under Milk Wood|website=gutenberg.net.au|access-date=6 March 2023|archive-date=6 March 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230306165311/http://gutenberg.net.au/ebooks06/0608221h.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

The first translation was published in November 1954 by Drei Brücken Verlag in Germany, as ''Unter dem Milchwald'', translated by [[Erich Fried]]. A few months later, in January 1955, the play appeared in the French journal ''[[Les Lettres Nouvelles]]'' as ''Le Bois de Lait'', translated by [[Roger Giroux]], with two further instalments in February and March.<ref>For details on the play's publication in ''Botteghe Oscure'', ''The Observer'', ''Mademoiselle'' and ''Les Lettres Nouvelles'', see R. Maud (1970), ''Dylan Thomas in Print'', which also gives details of Erich Fried's translation.</ref>

Over the next three years, ''Under Milk Wood'' was published in Dutch, Polish, Danish, Estonian, Norwegian, Finnish, Swedish, Japanese and Italian. It's estimated that it has now been translated into over thirty languages, including Welsh with a translation by [[T. James Jones]], (Jim Parc Nest), published in 1968 as ''Dan Y Wenallt''.<ref>For an interview with Jones, see [https://www.walesartsreview.org/t-james-jones-on-dylan-thomas/ Jones] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190810133733/https://www.walesartsreview.org/t-james-jones-on-dylan-thomas/ |date=10 August 2019 }} See also Jones' article on his translation: ''Llareggub's Cyfarwydd'' in ''New Welsh Review'', no. 54, Autumn 2001.</ref>

''Under Milk Wood'', along with all other published works by Thomas, entered the public domain in the United Kingdom on 1 January 2024.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Copyright Notice: Duration of copyright (term) |url=https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/copyright-notice-duration-of-copyright-term/copyright-notice-duration-of-copyright-term |access-date=6 February 2024 |website=GOV.UK |language=en |archive-date=25 April 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250425120410/https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/copyright-notice-duration-of-copyright-term/copyright-notice-duration-of-copyright-term |url-status=live }}</ref>

==Production history==
===Audio===
The play was first heard in public on 14 May 1953, at The Poetry Center at the [[92nd Street Y]], New York.<ref>''Dylan Thomas: The Collected Letters'' edited by Paul Ferris. Macmillan 1985. Footnote by editor.</ref> It was one of three public readings of the play Thomas gave there;<ref>"On the Air", ''The Bookseller'', 16 January 1954, p. 24</ref> he read the parts of the First Voice and the Reverend Eli Jenkins. The performance was recorded on a single-microphone tape recording (the microphone was laid at front centre on the stage floor) and later issued by [[Caedmon Records]].<ref>{{oclc|1120033265}}</ref> It is the only recorded performance of ''Under Milk Wood'' with Thomas in the cast. A studio recording, planned for 1954, was precluded by Thomas's death in November 1953.<ref>"Dylan Thomas Unabridged: The Caedmon Collection", [http://www.greenmanreview.com/book/book_thomas_caedmonaudio.html ''Green Man Review''.] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140830090321/http://www.greenmanreview.com/book/book_thomas_caedmonaudio.html |date=30 August 2014 }}</ref>

The [[BBC]] first broadcast ''Under Milk Wood'', described as "a new Play for Voices", on the [[Third Programme]] on 25 January 1954, two months after Thomas's death; several sections were omitted.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://genome.ch.bbc.co.uk/34c9dd713c62428c970a490ef1d43746 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220728141402/https://genome.ch.bbc.co.uk/34c9dd713c62428c970a490ef1d43746 |url-status=dead |archive-date=28 July 2022 |title=Under Milk Wood|date=25 January 1954|issue=1576 |pages=19|via=BBC Genome}}</ref> The play, produced by [[Douglas Cleverdon]], was recorded with an all-Welsh cast including [[Richard Burton]] as First Voice. A repeat was broadcast two days later.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Under Milk Wood|date=27 January 1954 |url=https://genome.ch.bbc.co.uk/5be189401bf14feb93a4e56e0728a7fd |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210128085717/https://genome.ch.bbc.co.uk/5be189401bf14feb93a4e56e0728a7fd |url-status=dead |archive-date=28 January 2021 |issue=1576 |pages=31|via=BBC Genome}}</ref> [[Daniel Jones (composer)|Daniel Jones]], the Welsh composer who was a lifelong friend of Thomas's (and his literary trustee), wrote the music; this was recorded separately, on 15 and 16 January, at Laugharne School. The play won the [[Prix Italia]] award for radio drama that year.<ref>{{Cite web |title=PRIX ITALIA |url=https://www.rai.it/prixitalia/|archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20120303114656/http://www.prixitalia.rai.it/2008/pdf/vincitori_edizionipassate_en.pdf|url-status=dead |archivedate=3 March 2012|website=www.rai.it}}</ref>

On 10 March 1954 the BBC German Service, broadcast a translation by [[Erich Fried]] as {{lang|de|Unter dem Milchwald}}; this was followed on 20 September by the first broadcast on German radio itself.<ref>S. W. Lawrie (1994), Dylan Thomas's Work in German Translation online at [http://www.geocities.ws/alfred_carol/web-fredi/Fried-gedichte Lawrie]</ref> 

In 1963 Cleverdon, the original radio producer, revisited the play and recorded the complete text, which was broadcast on the Third Programme on 11 October 1963.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://genome.ch.bbc.co.uk/14d603f882ed468495cb583e624f829e|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171207114440/http://genome.ch.bbc.co.uk/14d603f882ed468495cb583e624f829e|url-status=dead|archive-date=7 December 2017|title=Under Milk Wood|date=11 October 1963|issue=2082|pages=61|via=BBC Genome}}</ref>

In 1988 [[George Martin]] produced an album version, featuring more of the dialogue sung, with music by Martin and [[Elton John]], among others; [[Antony Hopkins]] played the part of "First Voice".<ref>{{Cite book|title=Under Milk Wood: a play for voices|date=21 April 2016|oclc=952475860 }}</ref>

In November 2003, as part of their commemoration of the fiftieth anniversary of Thomas's death, BBC radio broadcast a new production of the play, combining new actors with the original 1954 recording of Burton playing "First Voice". (Broadcast 15 November 2003, [[BBC Radio 4]]; repeated 24 December 2004.) Digital noise reduction technology allowed Burton's part to be incorporated unobtrusively into the new recording, which was intended to represent Welsh voices more realistically than the original.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://johnhardymusic.com/project/2003/under-milk-wood|title=Under Milk Wood|website=John Hardy Music|date=October 2003|access-date=6 March 2023|archive-date=6 March 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230306122956/https://johnhardymusic.com/project/2003/under-milk-wood|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Stage===
Theatre readings of the play in February 1954 included extracts read at the Dylan Thomas Memorial Recital at the [[Royal Festival Hall]], London.<ref>"Evening of Homage to Dylan Thomas", ''Western Mail'', 4 January 1954, p. 6</ref> and a complete reading at the [[Old Vic]] with Burton, [[Meredith Edwards (actor)|Meredith Edwards]], [[Jessie Evans (actress)|Jessie Evans]], [[Clifford Evans (actor)|Clifford Evans]], [[Rachel Roberts (actress)|Rachel Roberts]], [[Sybil Thorndike]] and [[Emlyn Williams]]; it was adapted and presented by [[Philip Burton (theatre director)|Philip Burton]].<ref>"under Milk Wood", ''The Stage'', 28 January 1954, p. 8</ref>

Reviewing the BBC broadcast, ''The Stage'' commented, "Every moment  belongs to the medium of radio … There is definitely no need here for scenery and props"<ref>"On the Air", ''The Stage'', 28 January 1954, p. 10</ref> Nonetheless, the play has subsequently been staged in many theatres, beginning in November 1954 at the Théâtre de la Cour Saint-Pierre, Geneva, by Phoenix Productions, with sound effects lent by the BBC.<ref>"Wales in Geneva", ''The Stage'', 18 November 1954, p. 13</ref> The impresario [[Henry Sherek]] obtained the theatrical rights and his production, directed by Cleverdon and [[Edward Burnham]], was seen at the [[Edinburgh Festival]] in August 1956 before transferring the following month to the [[West End theatre|West End]], where it ran at the [[Noël Coward Theatre|New Theatre]] for 250 performances.<ref>Herbert, p. 1320; and "Under Milk Wood Success at Edinburgh Festival", ''The Stage'', 30 August 1956, p. 9</ref>

In 1968 the [[Royal Shakespeare Company]] presented a production of the play as part of its "Theatregoround" programme in which small casts played to audiences in parts of the UK in which theatre was rarely or never seen. [[Terry Hands]] directed and the seven-strong cast included [[Emrys James]], [[Bruce Myers (actor)|Bruce Myers]] and [[Susan Fleetwood]].<ref>"Theatregoround 'Under Milk Wood' comes to the Aldwych", ''The Stage'', 7 March 1968, p. 16</ref> This was Hands's second production of the play: at the [[Everyman Theatre, Liverpool]] in 1965 he had directed a similarly small-cast production, in which both Myers and Fleetwood appeared.<ref>"Theatre Notes", ''Liverpool Daily Post'', 25 June 1965, p. 14</ref> He directed a third small-scale production in 1999 for the [[Theatr Clwyd]].<ref>"Theatre", ''Liverpool Daily Post'', 20 December 1999, p. 21</ref> In April 1970 a new company, Topaz Productions, staged the play at the [[May Fair Theatre]], London. The cast included [[Windsor Davies]], [[Philip Madoc]], [[David Jason]], [[Ruth Madoc]] and [[Clive Merrison]].<ref>"Under Milk Wood", ''The Stage'', 16 April 1970, p. 8</ref>

[[Guy Masterson]] of Theatre Tours International produced and performed a solo version of the play, given more than 2000 times since its world premiere in 1994. It has been performed at the Edinburgh Festival in 1994, 1996, 2000, 2003, 2007 and 2010; in [[Adelaide]], Australia, in 2006, 2007, 2008, 2009 and 2012; and in the West End of London at the [[Arts Theatre]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.theatretoursinternational.com/|title=Theatre Tours International: Olivier Award Winning Powerhouse UK based -International Touring Theatre Company|website=www.theatretoursinternational.com}}</ref> Martin's 1988 audio adaptation was subsequently produced as a one-off stage performance (as ''An Evening with Dylan Thomas'') for [[The Prince's Trust]], to commemorate the opening in December 1992 of the new [[AIR Studios]] at Lyndhurst Hall. It was produced by Martin and directed by Hopkins, who once again played the First Voice. Other roles were played by [[Harry Secombe]], [[Freddie Jones]], [[Catherine Zeta-Jones]], [[Siân Phillips]], [[Jonathan Pryce]], [[Alan Bennett]], and [[Tom Jones (singer)|Tom Jones]].<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.gettyimages.co.uk/detail/news-photo/an-evening-with-dylan-thomas-a-one-off-stage-performance-news-photo/944099240|title=An Evening with Dylan Thomas, a one off stage performance for The...|website=Getty Images|date=9 April 2018 }}</ref>

In 2009 and 2010 a Dutch translation by [[Hugo Claus]] was performed on stage by Jan Decleir and Koen De Sutter on a theatre tour in Belgium and the Netherlands (e.g. the Zeeland Late-Summer Festival, the Vooruit in Ghent, etc.).<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://inesminten.com/2010/04/12/fijnproeven-van-taal-en-spel-recensie-onder-het-melkwoud-van-jan-decleir-en-koen-de-sutter/|title=Fijnproeven van taal en spel – Recensie 'Onder het melkwoud' van Jan Decleir en Koen De Sutter|date=12 April 2010}}</ref> In 2010 a one-woman production of the text was performed at the Sidetrack Theatre in [[Sydney]], presented by Bambina Borracha Productions and directed by Vanessa Hughes. [[Zoe Norton Lodge]] performed all 64 characters in the play.<ref>[http://www.ozbabyboomers.com.au/theatre/2010/07_10/milk/milk.html Reviewed by John Rozentals] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110308040210/http://www.ozbabyboomers.com.au/theatre/2010/07_10/milk/milk.html |date=8 March 2011 }}, Oz Baby Boomers, 5 July 2010.</ref> In July 2011 Progress Youth Theatre ([[Reading, Berkshire]], UK) performed a stage adaptation of the radio script. All visual aspects, such as stage directions, costume, set and lighting design were therefore devised entirely by the youth theatre. The voice parts were shared equally among seven actors, with other actors playing multiple "named" parts (with the exception of Captain Cat, who remained on stage throughout the production).<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.uktw.co.uk/Play/Under-Milk-Wood/S5404/|title=Under Milk Wood (Play) archive [PLAY]|first=UK Theatre|last=Web|website=UK Theatre Web}}</ref>

In 2012 the [[Sydney Theatre Company]] staged a production starring [[Jack Thompson (actor)|Jack Thompson]] as First Voice and [[Sandy Gore]] as Second Voice, with a cast including [[Bruce Spence]], [[Paula Arundell]], [[Drew Forsythe]], [[Alan John]], Drew Livingston and [[Helen Thomson (actress)|Helen Thomson]].<ref>[https://www.crikey.com.au/2012/05/30/under-milk-wood-drama-theatre-sydney/ "Review: Under Milk Wood {{!}} Drama Theatre, Sydney"] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200613003311/https://www.crikey.com.au/2012/05/30/under-milk-wood-drama-theatre-sydney/ |date=13 June 2020 }}, ''Crikey'', 30 May 2012.</ref> The production was staged in the Drama Theatre of the [[Sydney Opera House]].<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.stagewhispers.com.au/reviews/under-milk-wood-0|title=Under Milk Wood &#124; Stage Whispers|website=www.stagewhispers.com.au|access-date=6 March 2023|archive-date=6 March 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230306123004/https://www.stagewhispers.com.au/reviews/under-milk-wood-0|url-status=live}}</ref>

In 2021, as part of its post-[[COVID-19]] programme, the [[Royal National Theatre|National Theatre]] presented an adaptation of ''Under Milk Wood'' with Michael Sheen reprising his role as First Voice. This new adaptation included additional material from Siân Owen extending Thomas's original script with framing scenes set in a care home. The adaptation tells the story of First Voice trying to connect with his father, played by [[Karl Johnson (actor)|Karl Johnson]], who has [[dementia]], by inventing stories of characters from the village his father lived in as a boy.<ref>"Under Milk Wood- National Theatre", [https://www.nationaltheatre.org.uk/shows/under-milk-wood ''Under Milk Wood''] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210702162627/https://www.nationaltheatre.org.uk/shows/under-milk-wood |date=2 July 2021 }}, 2021.</ref> In 2026 Kate Wasserberg directed a new production for [[Theatr Clwyd]].<ref>{{Cite web |last=Lawson |first=Mark |date=21 March 2026 |title=Under Milk Wood review – dark fairytales swirl around Dylan Thomas’s evergreen village |url=https://www.theguardian.com/stage/2026/mar/21/under-milk-wood-review-dylan-thomas-theatr-clwyd |website=The Guardian}}</ref>

===Screen===
The first attempt to bring the play to the screen was in May 1957 when the BBC broadcast a televised version narrated by [[Donald Houston]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p00l4p1c|title=Under Milk Wood |access-date=7 November 2015|work=BBC.co.uk|date=14 October 2011 }}</ref> 

A [[Under Milk Wood (1971 film)|1971 film adaptation]], with Burton reprising his role, also featured [[Elizabeth Taylor]], [[Peter O'Toole]], [[Glynis Johns]], [[Vivien Merchant]] and other well-known actors, including [[Ryan Davies]] as the "Second Voice".<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www2.bfi.org.uk/films-tv-people/4ce2b6b96335f|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160527111015/http://www.bfi.org.uk/films-tv-people/4ce2b6b96335f|url-status=dead|archive-date=27 May 2016|title=Under Milk Wood (1972)|website=BFI}}</ref> It was filmed on location in [[Fishguard]], Pembrokeshire, and at [[Lee International|Lee International Film Studios]] in London.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.walesonline.co.uk/lifestyle/nostalgia/hollywood-took-over-seaside-town-7270293|title=When Hollywood took over the seaside town of Fishguard|first=Rachael|last=Misstear|date=16 June 2014|website=WalesOnline}}</ref>

In 1992 Brightspark Productions released a 50-minute animation version, using an existing BBC recording with Burton as narrator. This was commissioned by [[S4C]] (a Welsh-language public service broadcaster). Music was composed specially by Trevor Herbert and performed by the [[Treorchy Male Voice Choir]] and the Welsh Brass Consort.<ref>Producer Robert Lyons. Director, Les Orton. It was made by Siriol Productions in association with Onward Productions and BBC Pebble Mill. This was released on DVD in October 2008. DVD ref: 5 037899 005798.</ref>

In 2014 [[Pip Broughton]] directed an adaptation of the play for the BBC, starring [[Michael Sheen]], [[Tom Jones (singer)|Tom Jones]], and [[Jonathan Pryce]].<ref>{{Citation|title=Under Milk Wood (TV Movie 2014)|url=https://www.imdb.com/title/tt3769244/?ref_=nm_flmg_dr_3|access-date=22 August 2017|archive-date=16 August 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210816230947/https://www.imdb.com/title/tt3769244/?ref_=nm_flmg_dr_3|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Musical adaptations===
[[Stan Tracey]] composed an ''Under Milk Wood Jazz Suite'' released on record in 1965. and subsequently presented in public with Tracey's quartet and a narrator.<ref>"Jazz Suite", Columbia 33SC1774 and SCX3589, WorldCat {{oclc| 58477451}}; and "Theatres", ''Liverpool Daily Post'', 23 February 1978, p. 2</ref>

In 1997 the pianist and composer [[Tony Gould]]'s adaptation of ''Under Milk Wood'' for narrator and chamber orchestra was first performed by the actor [[John Stanton (actor)|John Stanton]] and the [[Queensland Philharmonic Orchestra]].<ref>{{Cite web | url=http://www.tonygould.com.au/biography.htm | title=Tony Gould – Biography | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110902000315/http://www.tonygould.com.au/biography.htm | archive-date=2 September 2011| website=www.tonygould.com.au}}</ref>

In 2006 Akos Banlaky composed an opera with the [[libretto]] based on the German translation by Fried, performed at [[Tiroler Landestheater Innsbruck|Tiroler Landestheater]] in [[Innsbruck]], Austria).<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.tycerdd.org/under-milk-wood-cd|title=Under Milk Wood CD|website=tycerdd}}</ref>

In 2008 {{ill|François Narboni|fr}} composed an opera, {{lang|fr|Au Bois lacté}}, based on his own translation and adaptation of ''Under Milk Wood''. It was created at the [[Opéra-Théâtre de Metz Métropole|Opéra-Théâtre]] in [[Metz]], France, with staging by Antoine Juliens. The work is for 12 singers, large mixed choir, children choir, accordion, dancer and electronics sounds.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.operabase.com/works/au-bois-lacte-2015/en|title=Au bois lacté by Narboni &#124; Opera|website=Operabase}}</ref>

In 2008 a ballet version of ''Under Milk Wood'' by [[Independent Ballet Wales]] toured the UK. It was choreographed by Darius James with music by [[Thomas Hewitt Jones]]. A suite including music from the ballet was recorded by Court Lane Music in 2009.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.recordsinternational.com/cd.php?cd=04L108|title=Thomas Hewitt Jones (b.1984): Ballet Suite Under Milk Wood, String Music, Fantasy (Court Lane Ensemble), 4 Piano Miniatures (Christine Stevenson), Child of the Stable's Secret Birth for Choir (Choir of Clare College, Cambridge; Timothy Brown). – Records International|website=www.recordsinternational.com}}</ref>

In 2012 Gould's 1997 adaptation for narrator and chamber orchestra was again performed by Stanton as part of the [[Victorian College of the Arts Secondary School]]'s inaugural performance at the [[Melbourne Recital Centre]]. Gould played piano and worked with the students as a musical mentor. In 2014 the Welsh-born composer [[John Metcalf (composer)| John Metcalf]] wrote the music for ''[[Under Milk Wood: An Opera]]'', a one-act chamber piece using Thomas's text as its libretto. The work was recorded and released on the Ty Cerdd label as a double CD set in 2016.<ref> Metcalf, J. "Under Milk Wood, An Opera", TY Cerdd CD set TCR 013 {{oclc|990302976}}</ref> A DVD made by the original cast days before the premiere was released in 2014.<ref>{{oclc|1275038591}}</ref>

===Other===
In October 2014 the BBC launched an interactive [[ebook]] entitled ''Dylan Thomas: The Road To Milk Wood'', written by Jon Tregenna and Robin Moore. It deals with Thomas's journey from [[Swansea]] via the BBC to New York and beyond.<ref>{{cite book|url=https://books.apple.com/gb/book/bbc-dylan-thomas-the-road-to-milk-wood/id928536953?ls=1|title=BBC Dylan Thomas: The Road to Milk Wood by BBC on iBooks |date=22 October 2014 }}</ref>

In 2020 a production starred [[Kenneth Welsh]] and featured a deaf sign language performer, Missy Keast.<ref>{{Cite AV media |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8W_4Hjn9Ix4&ab_channel=ArizonaTheatreMatters |title=UNDER MILK WOOD |date=2023-12-02 |last=Arizona Theatre Matters |access-date=2024-08-18 |via=YouTube}}</ref>

==Quotations==
[[File:Dylan Thomas mug.jpg|thumb|One of many articles celebrating the work of Dylan Thomas. This mug bears a quotation from the prayer of the Rev Eli Jenkins in 'Under Milk Wood'.]]
*''To begin at the beginning: It is spring, moonless night in the small town, starless and bible-black, the cobblestreets silent and the hunched, courters'-and-rabbits' wood limping invisible down to the sloeblack, slow, black, crowblack, fishingboatbobbing sea.'' – opening lines, spoken by First Voice
*''We are not wholly bad or good, who live our lives under Milk Wood'' – prayer of the Reverend Eli Jenkins
*''Black as a chimbley!'' – Third neighbour
*'' Me, No-Good Boyo is up to no good... in the wash house.'' –  Nogood Boyo
*''I am going into the darkness of the darkness forever. I have forgotten that I was ever born.'' – Rosie Probert<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=-BG_DAAAQBAJ&pg=PA182|title = Dylan Thomas: His Life and Work|isbn = 9781349243662|last1 = Ackerman|first1 = John|date = 27 July 2016| publisher=Springer }}</ref>

*[[Frank Zappa]] quotes, though not precisely, the passage about Mr. Pugh's plans to poison his wife during a 1979 London performance of "Nanook Rubs It," which appears as part of a "Don't Eat the Yellow Snow" medley on the live album ''[[You Can't Do That on Stage Anymore, Vol. 1]]'' (1988).<ref>{{cite web|title=You Can't Do That On Stage Anymore Vol. 1|website=www.globalia.net |url=http://www.globalia.net/donlope/fz/lyrics/You_Can't_Do_That_On_Stage_Anymore_Vol_1.html#Yellow}}</ref>
*[[The Decemberists]]' song "[[Billy Liar (song)|Billy Liar]]" (2004) includes references to the character of Nogood Boyo and his fantasy of Mrs. Dai Bread Two and the corset.<ref>{{Cite web |url= https://songmeanings.com/songs/view/3530822107858497283/ |title=The Decemberists – Billy Liar Lyrics |website=SongMeanings |date=14 April 2012 }}</ref>
*[[Terry Pratchett]]'s [[Discworld]] country of Llamedos is a reference to Llareggub.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://wiki.lspace.org/Llamedos |title=Llamedos |website=wiki.lspace.org}}</ref>
*The Japanese rock group [[LSD March]] released an album titled Under Milk Wood (2009).<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.allmusic.com/album/under-milk-wood-mw0000804985|title=Under Milk Wood – LSD-March &#124; Songs, Reviews, Credits |website=AllMusic |via=www.allmusic.com}}</ref>
*The indie-rock band [[Evans The Death]], formed in London in 2011, take their name from the undertaker.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.barryanddistrictnews.co.uk/leisure/14619368.meet-the-band-evans-the-death/|title=Meet the Band: Evans the Death|website=Barry And District News|date=14 July 2016|access-date=21 April 2021|archive-date=21 April 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210421105442/https://www.barryanddistrictnews.co.uk/leisure/14619368.meet-the-band-evans-the-death/|url-status=live}}</ref>
*[[Paul McCartney]] has described how ''Under Milk Wood'' was one of the inspirations for the lyrics in [[the Beatles]] classic song, “[[Penny Lane]].”<ref>{{Cite web |date=15 January 2022 |title=In my ears and in my eyes {{!}} Hannah Gal |url=https://thecritic.co.uk/in-my-ears-and-in-my-eyes/ |access-date=6 February 2024 |website=The Critic Magazine |language=en-GB |archive-date=7 March 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230307101938/https://thecritic.co.uk/in-my-ears-and-in-my-eyes/ |url-status=live }}</ref>
* The prayer recited by Reverend Eli Jenkins in the play, known as the "Sunset Poem", is referenced in two episodes of the [[BBC One]] television series ''[[Our Girl]]''. It is then recited by the character [[Captain James|Captain Charles James]] at a funeral in the episode "Heroes" (Season One, Episode Five, 19 October 2014).<ref>{{cite web |last1=Grounds |first1=Tony |title=Our Girl Post Production Script |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/writers/documents/our-girl-s1-ep5-post-production.pdf |website=bbc.co.uk |publisher=BBC |access-date=11 July 2026}}</ref>
