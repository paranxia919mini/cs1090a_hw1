'''''Lone Star'''''<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://texasarchive.org/2008_00439|title=Interviews with Cast and Director of Lone Star (1996)|website=Texas Archive of the Moving Image|access-date=November 17, 2019}}</ref> is a 1996 American [[Western (genre)#Contemporary Western|neo-Western]] [[mystery film]] written, edited, and directed by [[John Sayles]]. Set in a small town in [[South Texas]], the film deals with a sheriff's (played by [[Chris Cooper]]) investigation into the murder of one of his predecessors ([[Kris Kristofferson]]) decades earlier. The cast also stars [[Joe Morton]], [[Elizabeth Peña]], [[Clifton James]], [[Ron Canada]], [[Frances McDormand]] and [[Matthew McConaughey]].

The film premiered at the 1996 [[South by Southwest|South by Southwest Festival]] and received widespread critical acclaim, with critics regarding it as a high point of 1990s independent cinema as well as one of Sayles's best films. Sayles's screenplay was nominated for an [[Academy Award]], [[BAFTA Award]], and [[Golden Globe Award for Best Screenplay|Golden Globe Award]]. The film was also nominated for four [[Independent Spirit Awards]], with Elizabeth Peña winning [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Supporting Female|Best Supporting Female]].

''Lone Star'' was recognized by the [[American Film Institute]] in [[AFI's 10 Top 10]] list in 2008 as a nominated Western Film.<ref name=":0" />

==Plot==
Two off-duty sergeants discover a human skeleton on an old U.S. Army rifle shooting range along with a Masonic ring, a Rio County sheriff's badge, and, later, an expended .45 pistol bullet. Sam Deeds, [[Sheriffs in the United States|sheriff]] of Rio County, Frontera, [[Texas]], begins an investigation. [[Forensic science|Forensics]] backs up the identity of the skeleton as Charlie Wade, the infamously corrupt sheriff who preceded Sam's recently deceased father, Buddy. Wade mysteriously disappeared in 1957, along with {{US$|10000|link=yes}}{{efn|{{Inflation|US|10000|1957|fmt=eq|r=-2}}.}} in county funds. Buddy's esteemed reputation for justice and subsequent election as Sheriff after Wade's disappearance led to the widespread belief that Buddy confronted Wade and ran him out of town.

Frontera, on the banks of the Rio Grande, is a [[border town]] with [[Racial discrimination|racial]] strife among the [[Tejanos|Tejano]], [[African Americans|African American]], [[Native Americans in the United States|Native American]], and European American  populations. There, the White population is no longer the majority. As a teenager, Sam hated his tyrannical father and left town as soon as he was old enough. He only returned to the town two years prior.

The town is enlarging and renaming their courthouse in Buddy's honor and proposing the building of an unneeded new prison. As a teenager, Sam had been in love with Tejano girl Pilar Cruz. However, their relationship was strongly opposed by both Buddy and Pilar's mother Mercedes, who took steps to separate them.

After a chance meeting, the now divorced Sam and the widowed Pilar, now a teacher, begin to rekindle their lingering passion. Mercedes is again against this. Pilar mistakenly believes that Mercedes objects to Sam because of his "[[Anglo]]" ethnicity. Despite being of Mexican origin, however, Mercedes devalues Mexicans, claiming she is of Spanish descent.

Colonel Delmore Payne has returned to town as the commander of the local U.S. [[Military base|Army base]]. Delmore has been estranged from his father Otis "Big O" Payne, a local nightclub owner and leader of the Black community, since childhood, when the serial womanizer abandoned Delmore and his mother. When a quarrel involving a soldier from the base results in a shooting at Otis's club—witnessed by Delmore's own resentful underage son who is surreptitiously at the club to scout out his grandfather—Delmore confronts Otis and threatens to make his establishment "off-limits." Otis counters that his establishment is the only place in town where Black soldiers are welcome.

Despite being warned by Mayor Hollis Pogue and other prominent local figures not to look into events from forty years before, Sam doggedly investigates the events leading up to Wade's murder. Wade terrorized the local Black and Mexican communities, [[Extortion|extorting]] money from business owners and committing numerous murders{{efn|one page of a list shown in the film includes five people murdered by Wade.}} by setting up his victims and shooting them for "[[resisting arrest]]". In front of a horrified Deputy Sheriff Hollis, Wade murdered Eladio Cruz, Mercedes's husband, who was running a [[People smuggling|migrant smuggling]] operation across the border without [[Kickback (bribery)|kickbacks]] to Wade.

Uncovering secrets about his father's term as sheriff, Sam discovers Buddy's own corruption, kickbacks, and use of [[Penal labour|prison labor]] for personal building projects. Buddy forcibly evicted residents of a small community to make a lake, with Buddy and Hollis receiving lakefront property, which made Frontera a popular tourist destination. Sam eventually discovers love letters from Buddy's longtime mistress—Mercedes Cruz.

Sam confronts Hollis and Otis about Wade's murder. Upon discovering Otis's clandestine gambling operation at the nightclub, a furious Wade ordered Otis to hand over extortion money. Wade was about to use his "resisting arrest" setup to kill Otis. Buddy arrived just as Hollis shot Wade to prevent Otis's murder. The three buried the body and took $10,000 from the county as an alibi for Wade's "abscondence". They gave the money to Mercedes—who was destitute after Wade killed Eladio—to buy her restaurant. Buddy and Mercedes later got involved. Sam decides to drop the issue, saying that Wade's murder will remain unsolved. Hollis is concerned that people will assume that Buddy killed Wade to take his job. Sam replies that Buddy's legend can handle it.

Showing Pilar an old photo of Buddy embracing Mercedes, Sam says that Eladio died 18 months before she was born, revealing that Buddy is Pilar's father. Both are appalled over the years of deception and repercussions, but since Pilar cannot have any more children, they decide to continue their romantic relationship, despite the knowledge that they are [[incest|half-siblings]].

==Themes==
The film deconstructs the concept of borders, both in the literal and figurative sense.<ref name="TPR">{{cite news |last1=Cone |first1=Nathan |title=Borders dissolve in the Texas classic, 'Lone Star |url=https://www.tpr.org/arts-culture/2024-02-27/borders-dissolve-in-lone-star-a-texas-classic-finally-on-blu-ray |access-date=16 July 2024 |work=[[Texas Public Radio]] |date=2024-02-27}}</ref><ref name="Seitz">{{cite news |last1=Seitz |first1=Matt Zoller |author1-link=Matt Zoller Seitz |date=2024-01-18 |title=Director John Sayles on the Making of 'Lone Star' |url=https://texashighways.com/culture/director-john-sayles-on-the-making-of-lone-star/ |access-date=16 July 2024 |work=Texas Highways}}</ref> The film takes place in a [[border town]] (its name Frontera is the Spanish word for "border"),<ref name="Perez" /> along the [[Mexico–United States border|U.S.-Mexico border]]. In Frontera, figurative barriers exist between different racial and cultural groups, specifically the Anglo, Black, and Tejano communities. The film goes on to subvert the idea of borders, as it depicts literal border crossings (such as Mercedes and Eladio immigrating to Texas from Mexico), as well as border crossings in a racial, generational, and cultural sense.<ref name="Perez" /><ref name="Seitz" />

Through its cross-cultural and time-spanning narrative, the film explores how people from disparate ethnicities and cultures are intertwined, past and present. Nuances and differences within and among specific communities are shown. For instance, Otis is African-American but is also of [[Black Indians in the United States|Indigenous]] descent.<ref name=Perez/> Pilar believes she is fully Tejano, only to discover she is half-white. The Tejano community itself has Mexican, Chicano, Mexican American, Spanish, Hispano, American and/or Indigenous ancestry.<ref>{{cite web |title=Understanding and Celebrating Tejano History |url=https://liberalarts.tamu.edu/blog/2021/09/15/understanding-and-celebrating-tejano-history/ |website=College of Arts & Sciences |publisher=Texas A&M University |access-date=16 July 2024 |date=2021-09-15 |archive-date=August 8, 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240808042602/https://liberalarts.tamu.edu/blog/2021/09/15/understanding-and-celebrating-tejano-history/ |url-status=dead }}</ref> Mercedes is herself an immigrant from Mexico, but over the years has chosen to distance herself from that heritage, choosing to identify as "Spanish" and looking down on other Mexican immigrants.<ref name=Perez/><ref>{{cite web |title=The Women of Lone Star |url=https://citedatthecrossroads.net/ids330f19/2019/11/23/the-women-of-lone-star/ |website=US Ethnic Representations in Film |access-date=16 July 2024 |date=2019-11-23}}</ref>

The moral border between "good people" and "bad people" is likewise complicated, as Sam learns the truth about his father and some of his unsavory dealings. Sam is intent on unraveling the inflated myths that surround Buddy as an upstanding sheriff.<ref name="filmmaker" /> He ultimately discovers that the father he despises is neither as bad as he has always believed him to be, nor as good as his burdensome legend depicts.<ref name=filmmaker>{{cite web |last1=Ratner |first1=Megan |title=Borderlines |url=https://filmmakermagazine.com/archives/issues/summer1996/sayles.php |date=1996 |magazine=[[Filmmaker (magazine)|Filmmaker Magazine]] |publisher=[[The Gotham Film & Media Institute|The Gotham]] |access-date=15 December 2023}}</ref> Intergenerational borders that divide Sam and Buddy, as well as Otis and Delmore Payne and Mercedes and Pilar Cruz, are eventually bridged as characters learn various truths about their parents and repair the fissures in strained relationships.<ref name=Criterion>{{cite web |title=Lone Star |url=https://www.criterion.com/films/29021-lone-star |website=The Criterion Collection |access-date=15 December 2023}}</ref>

Other themes include [[historical revisionism]], mythmaking, and how legends are used to obscure inconvenient truths. The question of who gets to interpret history and why is most evident in the competing stories about Buddy, as well as in the school board meeting scene in which parents and teachers argue over the appropriate version of Texas history to teach high school students. In historical accounts of the [[Battle of the Alamo]], it is often the heroic feats of Anglo figures like [[Davy Crockett]] and [[James Bowie|Jim Bowie]] that are celebrated, while the contributions of Mexicans, African-Americans, and Native Americans are relegated to the margins.<ref name=Perez/> Sayles stated he wanted the film to address the concept of historical revisionism, saying "One of the things that ''Lone Star'' is about, to me, is the way in which American culture has always, always been many cultures. [But] in many places, the dominant culture gets to write the history."<ref name=ArtsBeat>{{cite web|url=https://www.artsbeatla.com/2020/04/john-sayles-lone-star/ |title=John Sayles interviewed for "Lone Star" |last=Adamek |first=Pauline |date=June 1996 |website=ArtsBeatLA |access-date=July 16, 2024}}</ref><ref name="filmmaker" />

The dismantling of myth is encapsulated with the film's final lines, "Forget the Alamo." "[[Battle of the Alamo|Remember the Alamo]]" is a famous battle cry that honors Texans' loss to the Mexicans at the Battle of the Alamo. The "Forget" line deconstructs the mythmaking behind the Alamo story and the barriers that separate Pilar and Sam—the racial barrier, as well as the barrier of their blood relation.<ref name="Perez">{{cite web |last1=Perez |first1=Domino Renee |title=''Lone Star'': Past Is Present |url=https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/8358-lone-star-past-is-present |website=[[The Criterion Collection]] |access-date=16 July 2024 |date=2024-01-16}}</ref> In an essay for [[The Criterion Channel]], Domino Renee Perez writes, "The Alamo, both as a historical site and as a symbol, looms large in Texas mythmaking. But Sayles's film is more about revealing the dark secrets behind, rather than building up, a myth—the myth of Buddy Deeds. Pilar and Sam's resolve to forget represents a turn away from that legacy as they attempt to write their own futures, ones not beholden to any history."<ref name="Perez" />

Writers noted that the school board scene of parents challenging teachers has taken on a prescient meaning in light of the contemporary political climate and controversy over [[critical race theory]].<ref name=Perez/><ref name="Shaffer">{{cite news |last1=Shaffer |first1=Marshall |title=Interview: John Sayles on Capturing Past and Present in ''Lone Star'' |url=https://www.slantmagazine.com/film/john-sayles-interview-lone-star/ |access-date=16 July 2024 |work=[[Slant Magazine]] |date=2024-01-22}}</ref>

== Production ==

=== Background ===
[[John Sayles]] decided to make a film about the Texas border after going there in 1978 to shoot a cameo for an earlier film he wrote, and then visiting [[the Alamo]] in [[San Antonio]], and coming up with a script that "had elements of a Western, but it was more of a detective story. It was one of those rare instances where I wrote it and we got the money to make it right away."<ref name="Grdn2024">{{cite news |last1=Hoad |first1=Phil |date=26 February 2024 |title='I recently went back to the Texas border – and urinated on the wall': how we made Lone Star |url=https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2024/feb/26/texas-border-wall-lone-star-conflict |access-date=9 April 2024 |work=The Guardian}}</ref>

=== Casting ===
Sayles cast newcomer [[Matthew McConaughey]], whose biggest role prior to ''Lone Star'' was in [[Richard Linklater]]'s ''[[Dazed and Confused (film)|Dazed and Confused]]'', in a major role because "I needed a guy who didn't have any star weight but who had the presence to play off against Kristofferson."<ref name="Grdn2024" />

=== Filming ===
Filming took place on-location throughout southwestern [[Texas]] and along the [[Rio Grande]], mainly in the towns of [[Del Rio, Texas|Del Rio]], [[Eagle Pass, Texas|Eagle Pass]] and [[Laredo, Texas|Laredo]].<ref>{{cite web |title=The Border Towns of 'Lone Star' |url=https://gov.texas.gov/film/trail/border-towns-of-lone-star |access-date=15 December 2023 |website=Texas Film Commission |publisher=State of Texas, Office of the Governor}}</ref>

Sayles did not want to film the flashback scenes with visible cuts to the present-day scenes, and instead used [[Panning (camera)|pans]] so the transitions occurred within a single camera shot.<ref name="Seitz" /><ref name="Shaffer" /><ref name="Grdn2024" /> These allowed the transitions to "feel like it was just a flow like time or like [a] river".<ref name="Chaw">{{cite web |last1=Chaw |first1=Walter |date=2024-01-15 |title=We're So Short on Time: John Sayles on Lone Star |url=https://www.rogerebert.com/interviews/were-so-short-on-time-john-sayles-on-lone-star |access-date=16 July 2024 |website=[[RogerEbert.com]]}}</ref> An example is the scene where a present-day Sam is seen in the same place in present-day where he and Pilar have just strolled together discussing their past, and where Sam lingers to recollect a scene that took place on the same spot 23 years before between his 15-year-old self and a 14-year old Pilar.<ref name="Chaw" />

=== Music ===
[[Mason Daring]]{{'}}s soundtrack uses music from a variety of genres, including [[juke joint]] music, [[conjunto]], and [[country music]], to highlight the melting pot of cultures in Rio County.<ref>{{cite web |last1=McDonald |first1=Steven |title=Lone Star Original Soundtrack |url=https://www.allmusic.com/album/lone-star-mw0000184679 |access-date=16 July 2024 |website=[[AllMusic]]}}</ref>

==Reception==
===Box office===
''Lone Star'' premiered at [[South by Southwest]] on March 14, 1996. It later screened in the [[Directors' Fortnight]] section at the [[Cannes Film Festival]] on May 10, 1996.<ref name="Cannes">{{cite news |last1=Turan |first1=Kenneth |title=Master of the Possible: Director John Sayles Exhibits a Determined Vision |url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1996-05-13-ca-3700-story.html |access-date=16 July 2024 |work=[[Los Angeles Times]] |date=May 13, 1996}}</ref> It was released in North American theaters on June 21, 1996, and ultimately made $13 million at the box office<ref name="NUM" /> on an estimated budget of $3–4.5 million.<ref name="filmmaker" />

===Critical response===
{{Rotten Tomatoes prose|91%|8.8|140|Smart and absorbing, ''Lone Star'' represents a career high point for writer-director John Sayles – and '90s independent cinema in general.|access-date=July 17, 2024}}<ref>{{Cite web |title=Lone Star |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/1074022-lone_star |access-date=July 17, 2024 |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]}}</ref> On [[Metacritic]], it has a score of 79 out of 100 based on reviews from 23 critics, indicating "generally favorable" reviews.<ref>{{cite web |title=Lone Star |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/lone-star |access-date=July 17, 2024 |website=[[Metacritic]]}}</ref>

Writing at the time of release, [[Janet Maslin]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' said, "This long, spare, contemplatively paced film, scored with a wide range of musical styles and given a sun-baked clarity by Stuart Dryburgh's cinematography, is loaded with brief, meaningful encounters... And it features a great deal of fine, thoughtful acting, which can always be counted on in a film by Mr. Sayles".<ref name=Maslin>{{cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1996/06/21/movies/film-review-sleepy-texas-town-with-an-epic-story.html |last=Maslin |first=Janet |author-link=Janet Maslin |website=[[The New York Times]] |title=FILM REVIEW;Sleepy Texas Town With an Epic Story |date=21 June 1996|access-date=2014-08-19 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101203143650/https://www.nytimes.com/1996/06/21/movies/film-review-sleepy-texas-town-with-an-epic-story.html |archive-date=2010-12-03 |url-status=live}}</ref> "All the film's characters are flesh and blood", Maslin added, pointing particularly to the portrayals by Kristofferson, Canada, James, Morton and Colón.<ref name=Maslin/>

The ''[[Los Angeles Times]]''{{'}}s [[Kenneth Turan]] praised the film, writing its triumph is "how well it integrates Sayles' [social] concerns with the heightened tension and narrative drive the thriller form provides".<ref name=Turan>{{cite news|url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1996-06-21-ca-16993-story.html |title=Human Relations the Star of Sayles' 'Lone' Mystery |last=Turan |first=Kenneth |author-link=Kenneth Turan |work=Los Angeles Times |date=June 21, 1996 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210620112652/https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1996-06-21-ca-16993-story.html |archive-date=2021-06-20 |url-status=live}}</ref> Film critics Dennis West and Joan M. West of ''[[Cineaste (magazine)|Cineaste]]'' praised the psychological aspects of the film, writing, "''Lone Star'' strikingly depicts the personal [[personal boundaries|psychological boundaries]] that confront many citizens of Frontera as a result of living in such close proximity to the border".<ref>{{cite magazine |last1=West |first1=Dennis |first2=Joan M. |last2=West |magazine=[[Cineaste (magazine)|Cineaste]] |volume=22 |number=3 |date=Summer 1996 |pages=34–36 |jstor= 41688927  <!-- Keeping link to unavailable document for reference: http://www.lib.berkeley.edu/MRC/lonestar.html --> |title=Lone Star by R. Paul Miller, Maggie Renzi, John Sayles}}</ref>

[[Roger Ebert]] awarded the film 4 out of 4 stars. His review read, "''Lone Star'' is a great American movie, one of the few to seriously try to regard with open eyes the way we live now. Set in a town that until very recently was rigidly segregated, it shows how Chicanos, blacks, whites and Indians shared a common history, and how they knew one another and dealt with one another in ways that were off the official map. This film is a wonder -- the best work yet by one of our most original and independent filmmakers -- and after it is over, and you begin to think about it, its meanings begin to flower."<ref>{{Cite web |last=Ebert |first=Roger |author-link=Roger Ebert |date=July 3, 1996 |title=Lone Star |url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/lone-star-1996 |access-date=July 17, 2024 |website=[[Chicago Sun-Times]] |via=RogerEbert.com}}</ref>

[[Ann Hornaday]], then writing for the ''[[Austin American-Statesman]]'', declared it "a work of awesome sweep and acute perception", judging it "the most accomplished film of [Sayles'] 17-year career".<ref>{{cite news |title='Lone Star' shines brightly |first=Ann |last=Hornaday |author-link=Ann Hornaday |date=28 June 1996 |newspaper=[[Austin American-Statesman]] |page=E1 |url= <!-- Keeping link to search for reference: http://nl.newsbank.com/nl-search/we/Archives?p_product=AASB&p_theme=aasb&p_action=search&p_maxdocs=200&p_topdoc=1&p_text_direct-0=0EA21383C44FB99D&p_field_direct-0=document_id&p_perpage=10&p_sort=YMD_date:D -->}}</ref> ''[[The Washington Post]]'' writer [[Hal Hinson]] characterized it as "a carefully crafted, unapologetically literary accomplishment."<ref>{{cite news |first=Hal |last=Hinson |author-link=Hal Hinson |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/1996/07/12/lone-star-stagnant-sayles/2ad08c90-fad4-496d-a49e-132c396a8758/ |title='Lone Star': Stagnant Sayles | date=12 July 1996 |page=F6 |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |access-date=2014-08-19}}</ref>

In 2004, William Arnold of the ''[[Seattle Post-Intelligencer]]'' said that the film was "widely regarded as Sayles' masterpiece", declaring that it had "captured the zeitgeist of the '90s as successfully as "[[Chinatown (1974 film)|Chinatown]]" did the '70s".<ref>{{cite news |url=http://www.seattlepi.com/ae/movies/article/John-Sayles-timely-political-lampoon-aims-1154322.php |title=John Sayles' timely political lampoon aims squarely at George W. Bush |first=William |last=Arnold |date=16 September 2004 |website=[[Seattle Post-Intelligencer]] |access-date=2014-08-19}}</ref>

In 2020, Hornaday compiled a list for ''The Washington Post'' titled "The 34 Best Political Movies Ever Made", in which she ranked ''Lone Star'' at number 10.<ref name=Hornaday/> Describing ''Lone Star'' as Sayles's masterwork, she wrote, "A simultaneously epic and finely drawn intergenerational and time-shifting murder mystery set on the Texas-Mexico border, ''Lone Star'' interrogates history, narrative and tidal shifts in power through the lens of race and immigration, but never at the expense of their complexities. Timely when it first came out, today it feels more relevant than ever."<ref name=Hornaday>{{cite news|url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/arts-entertainment/2020/01/23/34-best-political-movies-ever-made/ |first=Ann |last=Hornaday |title=The 34 best political movies ever made |newspaper=The Washington Post |date=January 23, 2020 |access-date=16 July 2024}}</ref>

In 2024, ''Lone Star'' was rereleased in a brand new 4K restoration leading to further critical analysis of the film. Writing in ''[[The Guardian]]'', [[Peter Bradshaw]] lavished the film with praise, calling it an "overlooked strand of indie movie-making and myth-making in 90s Hollywood, distinct from the brilliant ironies and shocks of Tarantino or the literary noir of the Coen brothers." He gave the film five stars saying "it is thoughtful and complex and grownup."<ref name="Guardian 2024-08-14">{{cite news |last=Bradshaw |first=Peter |author-link=Peter Bradshaw |date=2024-08-14 |title=Lone Star review – John Sayles's powerful crime drama is an extraordinary relic of 90s film-making |url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/article/2024/aug/14/lone-star-review-john-sayles-chris-cooper-kris-kristofferson-matthew-mcconaughey |accessdate=2024-08-22 |work=The Guardian }}</ref>

The film received an [[Academy Award]] nomination for [[Best Screenplay, Original|Best Screenplay]] for [[John Sayles]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=2014-10-05 |title=The 69th Academy Awards {{!}} 1997 |url=https://www.oscars.org/oscars/ceremonies/1997 |access-date=2024-07-17 |website=www.oscars.org |language=en}}</ref> The film also won the [[Grand Prix (Belgian Film Critics Association)|Belgian Grand Prix]],<ref>{{Cite web |title=Cinéma - Lundi 12 janvier 1998 |url=http://archives.lesoir.be/cinema_t-19980112-Z0EPW7.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://archive.today/20130115031808/http://archives.lesoir.be/cinema_t-19980112-Z0EPW7.html |archive-date=January 15, 2013 |access-date=July 17, 2024 |website=Le Soir |language=fr}}</ref> and the awards for Best Director and Best Screenplay from the [[Society of Texas Film Critics Awards]]. The screenplay was nominated for a [[Writers Guild of America Award for Best Original Screenplay|Writers Guild of America Award]], a [[BAFTA Award for Best Original Screenplay|BAFTA Award]],<ref>{{Cite web |title=Film in 1997 {{!}} BAFTA Awards |url=https://awards.bafta.org/award/1997/film |access-date=2024-07-17 |website=awards.bafta.org}}</ref> and a [[Golden Globe Award for Best Screenplay|Golden Globe Award]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=Lone Star |url=https://goldenglobes.com/film/lone-star/ |access-date=2024-07-17 |website=Golden Globes |language=en-US}}</ref>

[[Elizabeth Peña]] won the [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Supporting Female]]<ref name="Indie">{{Cite web |title=1997 Nominees and Winners |url=https://s3.amazonaws.com/SA_SubForm_etc/2021_SA_ALLNomineesWinners_063021.pdf |access-date=July 17, 2024 |website=Film Independent |page=44}}</ref> and the [[ALMA Award|BRAVO Award]] for Outstanding Actress in a Feature Film.<ref>{{Cite web |title=1996 NCLR Bravo Award Winners |url=http://www.almaawards.com/wmspage.cfm?parm1=117 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20020911183105/http://www.almaawards.com/wmspage.cfm?parm1=117 |archive-date=September 11, 2002 |access-date=July 17, 2024 |website=ALMA Awards}}</ref> The film was nominated for three Independent Spirit Awards: [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Male Lead|Best Male Lead]] ([[Chris Cooper]]), [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Film|Best Film]], and [[Independent Spirit Award for Best Screenplay|Best Screenplay]].<ref name="Indie" />

The film is recognized by the [[American Film Institute]] in [[AFI's 10 Top 10]] list in 2008 as a nominated Western Film.<ref name=":0">{{cite web|url=http://connect.afi.com/site/DocServer/10top10.pdf?docID=381&AddInterest=1781 |title=AFI's 10 Top 10 Nominees |format=PDF |access-date=2016-08-19 |url-status=bot: unknown |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110716071937/http://connect.afi.com/site/DocServer/10top10.pdf?docID=381&AddInterest=1781 |archive-date=2011-07-16 }}</ref>

==Home media==
In 2023, [[The Criterion Collection]] announced they were releasing a [[4K resolution|4K]] digital restoration of ''Lone Star'', supervised by Sayles and cinematographer [[Stuart Dryburgh]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.blu-ray.com/news/?id=33414 |title=Criterion Announces January Releases |date=October 16, 2023 |website=blu-ray.com |access-date=July 16, 2024}}</ref> The restoration was released on Blu-ray disc on January 16, 2024. The edition includes a new interview with Dryburgh and a conversation between Sayles and filmmaker [[Gregory Nava]].<ref name=Criterion/>
