'''''Raintree County''''' is a 1957 American [[Epic film|epic]] [[historical romance]] [[Western film|western]] [[war film]] adapted from [[Raintree County (novel)|the 1948 novel of the same name]] by [[Ross Lockridge Jr.]] The film was directed by [[Edward Dmytryk]] and distributed by [[Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer]]. Set in both 
the [[Midwestern United States|American Midwest]] and the [[Southern United States|American South]] against the backdrop of the [[Antebellum South]] and the [[American Civil War]], the film tells the story of a small-town Midwestern teacher and poet named John Shawnessy, who meets and marries a beautiful Southern belle named Susanna Drake; however, her emotional instability leads to the destruction of their marriage. The leading roles are played by [[Montgomery Clift]], [[Elizabeth Taylor]], [[Eva Marie Saint]], [[Nigel Patrick]] and [[Lee Marvin]].

In July 1947, Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer had purchased the film rights of Ross Lockridge Jr.'s unpublished manuscript for ''Raintree County'' for $150,000. [[Carey Wilson (writer)|Carey Wilson]] was immediately signed as producer, and prominent actors were considered for leading roles. In January 1948, the book was released and became a national bestseller. In the following year, filming was delayed for two years as the project had grown too expensive. In 1955, the project was revived with Taylor and Clift signed for the two leading roles. Filming began in April 1956 and [[Wrap (filmmaking)|wrapped]] in October 1956. It would also mark the first use of a 65-millimeter widescreen process originally called [[Ultra Panavision 70|MGM Camera 65]].

''Raintree County'' had its world premiere at the [[Brown Theatre]] in [[Louisville, Kentucky]] on October 2, 1957. In December 1957, the film was given a wide release in the United States, where it received negative reviews from film critics, who praised the production values and the performances of Clift and Taylor, but were critical of the film's plot. The film was nominated for four [[Academy Awards]] for [[Academy Award for Best Actress|Best Actress]] (Taylor), [[Academy Award for Best Production Design|Art Direction–Set Decoration]], [[Academy Award for Best Costume Design|Best Costume Design]], and [[Academy Award for Best Original Score|Best Score]].

==Plot==
In 1859, idealist John Wickliff Shawnessey is living in Raintree County, Indiana. He's fascinated by the legend of the county's magnificent [[Samanea saman|raintree]], and at one point, he ventures into the large, dangerous swamp where he believes it can be found. His quest is unsuccessful, and he almost dies in the effort.

Shawnessey has a college sweetheart, Nell Gaither, but he's soon distracted by Susanna Drake, a rich [[New Orleans]] [[Southern belle]]. He has a brief and passionate affair with Susanna while she is visiting his community. Following her return to the South, she returns to Indiana to tell Shawnessey she is pregnant with his child. John marries her out of honor and duty, leaving Nell heartbroken.

They travel south to visit Susanna's family. He learns that Susanna's mother went insane and died in a suspicious fire, along with Susanna's father and Henrietta, a slave implied to be the father's [[concubine]]. Susanna suspects that Henrietta may have been her biological mother. Gradually, Susanna appears to be suffering from mental illness. She tells John that she faked pregnancy to trick him into marriage.

As an abolitionist in the antebellum South, Shawnessey does not fit in with Susanna's family, and they return to Raintree County before the outbreak of the [[American Civil War|Civil War]]. John works as a teacher and they have a child, Jimmy, born at the outbreak of the war. In the war's third year, Susanna develops severe [[paranoia]] and delusions. She flees Indiana with Jimmy and seeks refuge among her extended family in Georgia.

Shawnessey is determined to find her and recover his son. He enlists in the Union Army in hopes of encountering his wife and child. After fighting in numerous battles in the Western Theater of the war, he finds Jimmy at an old plantation and learns that Susanna has been placed in an insane asylum. He is wounded while carrying Jimmy back to the Northern lines and is discharged from the Union Army. Johnny searches for Susanna, finding her kept in terrible conditions at the asylum. He brings her back with him to Raintree County.

After the end of the war and President [[Assassination of Abraham Lincoln|Abraham Lincoln's assassination]], Shawnessey considers his future. Nell urges him to run for political office. Recognizing that Nell and John still love each other, Susanna sacrifices herself and deludedly enters the swamp in the middle of the night to find the legendary raintree. Four-year-old Jimmy follows her. The search party eventually finds her body. John and Nell find Jimmy alive and carry him out of the swamp. Focused on the child, the two fail to notice the tall, spectacular raintree glowing in the early morning sunlight.

==Production==
===Development===
[[File:Harbor Drive-In Ad - 6 March 1958, National City, CA.jpg|thumb|[[Drive-in theater|Drive-in]] advertisement from 1958.]] 
On July 15, 1947, [[Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer]] had awarded first-time author [[Ross Lockridge Jr.]] a $150,000 prize for his novel, ''Raintree County'', which was to be published by [[Houghton Mifflin Harcourt|Houghton Mifflin]].<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1947/07/15/archives/books-authors.html|title=Books—Author|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The New York Times|page=21|date=July 15, 1947|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref> Two days later, the studio had purchased the film rights for $150,000. With a preliminary budget of $6 million, screenwriter [[Carey Wilson (writer)|Carey Wilson]] had been signed as the producer. He stated he had [[Lana Turner]], [[Ava Gardner]], and [[Janet Leigh]] in mind for the female roles while [[Robert Walker (actor, born 1918)|Robert Walker]] and [[Keenan Wynn]] were being considered for the leading male roles. On the studio's shortlist, [[Van Heflin]] and [[Gene Kelly]] were also being considered.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1947/07/17/archives/metro-will-film-raintree-county-studio-will-spend-5000000-in.html|title=Metro Will Film 'Raintree County'|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The New York Times|page=16|date=July 17, 1947|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref> Less than a year later, in March 1948, Lockridge Jr. was found dead inside his garage. His death was ruled a [[suicide]] from [[carbon monoxide]] poisoning. It was also reported that he had been working with the studio on the film adaptation.<ref>{{cite news|title=Lockridge, Author, Suicide at 33; Worn by Writing 'Raintree County'|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1948/03/08/archives/lockridge-author-suicide-at-33-worn-by-writing-raintree-county.html|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The New York Times|pages=1, 15|date=March 8, 1948|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref> However, in August 1949, Wilson wrote a letter addressed to Lockridge's widow that the project had been delayed for two years. He wrote, "The book, to be filmed as I want it filmed and as the quality of the writing demands it be filmed, will cost more than $6,000,000 and the situation today does not permit such an expenditure."<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/newspage/551405982/|title='Raintree County' Filming Delayed At Least 2 Years|url-access=subscription|newspaper=[[Reporter Times|The Martinsville Daily Reporter]]|page=1|date=August 6, 1949|access-date=April 21, 2020|via=[[Newspapers.com]]}}</ref>

In August 1955, the project was revived by MGM production head executive [[Dore Schary]] with [[David Lewis (producer)|David Lewis]] hired as producer. Lewis said he is the one who suggested the project to Schary, and suggested the film version focus on the story of Susannah; Schary agreed.<ref name="Lewis p 212">Lewis p 212</ref>

[[Elizabeth Taylor]] was cast as Susannah Drake and [[Millard Kaufman]] was hired to write the script.<ref>{{cite news|last=Pryor|first=Thomas M.|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1955/08/29/archives/mgm-film-role-for-miss-taylor-actress-to-star-in-raintree-county.html|title=M-G-M Film Role for Miss Taylor|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The New York Times|page=16|date=August 29, 1955|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref><ref>Schallert, Edwin. (September 26, 1955). "[https://www.newspapers.com/newspage/381288085/ 'Raintree County' May Cost $8,000,000; Curtis Company Formed]." ''Los Angeles Times''. Part III, pg. 9 – via Newspapers.com.</ref> Lewis claimed MGM fought the casting of Taylor as "They felt that she was neither a good actress nor a potential box office star."<ref name="Lewis p 212"/>

In December of the same year, [[Montgomery Clift]] had signed a three-picture deal with Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer with ''Raintree County'' to be filmed first.<ref>{{cite news|last=Pryor|first=Thomas M.|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1955/12/09/archives/metro-gets-clift-for-three-films-actors-first-multiplemovie.html|title=Metro Gets Clift For Three Films|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The New York Times|page=32|date=December 9, 1955|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref> By January 1956,  [[Eva Marie Saint]] had been offered $100,000 (~${{Format price|{{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=100000|start_year=1956}}}} in {{Inflation/year|US-GDP}}) to co-star in ''Raintree County''.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://archive.org/details/variety201-1956-01/page/n521/|title=Salary Going Up|magazine=Variety|page=4|date=January 11, 1956|access-date=April 21, 2020|via=Internet Archive}}</ref> [[Rod Taylor]] actively campaigned for his role in the film.<ref name="rod"/>

Lewis wrote the choice of director was between [[George Sidney]] and [[Edward Dmytryk]], and he chose the latter "as the lesser of two evils. I had wanted [[Fred Zinnemann]] and he had liked the script. In fact, he had convinced Clift that he should do it [the film], but he refused to shoot it on the MGM lot with their production department; many of the better directors were refusing to work at MGM by this time. But it was essential to have the film shot on the lot — nothing else was scheduled for the months ahead and something had to be working in order to absorb all the overhead."<ref>Lewis p 214</ref>

Screenwriter Millard Kaufman said he was "very taken with" the novel. "It was very complicated in terms of literary form. It was complex and turned on itself in a number of ways which at that time which was unusual."<ref name="millard">{{cite web|first=Stephen|last=Vagg|url=https://stephenvagg.substack.com/p/millard-kaufman-on-raintree-county?utm_source=publication-search|title=Interview with Millard Kaufman on 3 December 2003|website=Stephen Vagg Substack|date=26 March 2025}}</ref> Kaufman said later however that he felt "I included too much of the novel" in the screenplay and "I went on too many tangents."<ref name="millard"/>

===Filming===
''Raintree County'' was shot at various locations, including [[Dunleith]] and Elms Court
[[antebellum architecture|antebellum]] mansions, [[Windsor Ruins]], in [[Natchez, Mississippi]]; [[Reelfoot Lake]] in northwest [[Tennessee]] near the [[Kentucky]] border;<ref>{{cite web |url=http://usmarshals.warnerbros.com/mainframe.html |title=U.S. Marshals |access-date=2011-12-16 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120114172215/http://usmarshals.warnerbros.com/mainframe.html |archive-date=2012-01-14 }}</ref> and two locations in [[Kentucky]], the Liberty Hall Historic Site on Wilkinson Street in [[Frankfort, Kentucky|Frankfort]] and settings in and near [[Danville, Kentucky|Danville]]. The Reelfoot Lake locations were used for the swamp scenes.

During filming, Montgomery Clift was injured and nearly killed in a serious automobile accident. On the evening of May 12, 1956, feeling tired and suffering from a hangover, he drove into a telephone pole and wrecked his car. He broke his nose, cut his lip, and fractured his jaw in three places, which had to be wired back together by surgery.<ref name="tvguide">{{cite web|url= https://www.tvguide.com/movies/raintree-county/2030080944|title=Raintree County|publisher=TV Guide|access-date=October 15, 2022}}</ref> As a result, the studio announced that production had been shut down for two to three weeks. At the time, the filming crew had moved to do two months of location shooting throughout most of the [[Deep South]].<ref>{{cite news|last=Pryor|first=Thomas M.|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1956/05/15/archives/filming-delayed-by-clifts-mishap-mgm-halts-production-of-raintree.html|title=Filming Delayed by Clift's Mishap|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The New York Times|page=26|date=May 15, 1956|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref> A week later, after Clift had been discharged from the hospital, filming was further delayed until early July.  The studio believed he (Clift) would be able to return to work within three weeks.<ref>{{cite news|last=Pryor|first=Thomas M.|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1956/05/24/archives/raintree-county-put-off-to-july-4-injuries-to-clift-in-may-13-car.html|title='Raintree County' Put Off Until July 4|newspaper=The New York Times|page=27|date=May 23, 1956|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref> Although he spent weeks in surgery and recovery, he returned to finish the film. The physical damage to his face was apparent in several scenes filmed after the accident; the left side of his face was partially paralyzed.<ref>{{cite news |url=http://nla.gov.au/nla.news-article41855145 |title=Clift upsets Hollywood. |newspaper=[[The Australian Women's Weekly]] | date=20 June 1956 |access-date=3 June 2012 |page=46 |publisher=National Library of Australia}}</ref>  His performance in the scenes shot after the accident was also markedly different from those shot before; he began to drink heavily and take a concoction of drugs which made his face more haggard and gave his eyes a furtive look, and affected his attitude and posture.<ref name="tvguide"/>

Edward Dmytryk later revealed in his autobiography ''It's a Hell of a Life but Not a Bad Living'' that he found  "a hundred containers" of every kind of drug and "a beautiful leather case fitted with needles and syringes" in Clift's hotel room and once found him so drunk that his cigarette had burned itself out between his fingers.<ref name="tvguide"/> During filming in Danville, Clift's behavior grew increasingly erratic and bizarre, ordering his steak "blue-rare" (nearly uncooked) and adding masses of butter and pepper and eating it with his fingers, and running naked through the town, which resulted in a policeman being stationed outside his hotel room door to prevent his leaving during the night.<ref name="tvguide"/>

Kaufman said the director "shot the script as it was. Most of these crazy bastards won’t to that. Unfortunately it would have been better if he hadn’t.”<ref name="millard"/> The writer called David Lewis "a very nice rather inept man.”<ref name="millard"/>
 
Elizabeth Taylor had problems during the production with her period clothing, and on one occasion she collapsed from hyperventilation and was treated with Clift's bottle of Demerol and a syringe, delivered by the doctor. She took over a week to also recover from [[tachycardia]] following the incident.<ref name="tvguide"/> On set she was often late for filming and preoccupied with her romance with [[Mike Todd]], who hired a commercial airliner to personally deliver some expensive presents to her in Danville.<ref name="tvguide"/>

The "Raintree" isn't actually a [[Rain tree]] or Samanea saman: The literary source: In the original novel by Ross Lockridge Jr., the legend refers to the [[Goldenrain tree]] (Koelreuteria paniculata). This tree sheds its bright yellow petals in the summer, creating the illusion of golden rain. However, this species only grows to about 10 to 15 meters tall and doesn't appear as monumental as the giant tree in the film. The tree in the swamp (in the film finale): The scene at the end of the film was shot in the real swamps of [[Reelfoot Lake]] in Lake County, Tennessee. The huge, gnarled tree is a [[Bald cypress]] (Taxodium distichum), which is typical of these swampy landscapes. To create the magical effect of "golden rain," the film crew artificially decorated the tree with golden leaves and blossoms.

''Raintree County'' was the first film shot in a 65-millimeter anamorphic widescreen process originally called MGM Camera 65, later renamed [[Ultra Panavision 70]], which yielded an extra-wide image of 2.76:1; it was also used for MGM's 1959 version of ''[[Ben-Hur (1959 film)|Ben Hur]]''. Although MGM expected to release the film in [[70mm film|70mm]], the studio ultimately opted not to.  The only projectors at the time were being used to screen Mike Todd's ''[[Around the World in 80 Days (1956 film)|Around the World in 80 Days]]''.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://widescreenmuseum.com/widescreen/wingup2.htm|title=Widescreen Museum - The Ultra Panavision Wing 2|website=widescreenmuseum.com|access-date=2018-02-18}}</ref> Due to the major delays in filming due to the issues with Clift and Taylor and the extravagant sets, the film was the most expensive US-based film in MGM's history, and Dmytryk never directed for MGM again. Though a success at the box office, it did not recoup its cost.<ref name="rod">Stephen Vagg, ''Rod Taylor: An Aussie in Hollywood'', Bear Manor Media, 2010 p54</ref>

==Release==
On January 24, 1957, Dmytryk, Lewis, [[Joseph Vogel (executive)|Joseph Vogel]], and numerous studio executives drove out to [[Santa Barbara, California]] to preview the film at the Granada Theater. The film ran for three hours and six minutes, and it had reportedly received a favorable response from an audience of 1,100 spectators.<ref>{{cite news|last=Pryor|first=Thomas R.|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1957/01/25/archives/mgm-chiefs-go-on-preview-trek-ride-80-miles-for-first-public.html|title=M-G-M Chiefs Go To Preview|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The New York Times|page=17|date=January 25, 1957|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref> However, by March 1957, following numerous previews, MGM announced that reshoots would begin that same month so "that certain dramatic points will be emphasized by re-shooting in [[close-up]] and that extra footage will be added to achieve smoother transitions in the sprawling drama."<ref>{{cite news|last=Pryor|first=Thomas M.|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1957/03/19/archives/metro-will-add-footage-to-movie-studio-to-reshoot-scenes-in.html|title=MGM Will Add Footage to Movie|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The New York Times|page=44|date=March 19, 1957|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref>

About 5,000 attendees arrived in [[Louisville, Kentucky]] for the film's world premiere at the [[Brown Theatre]] on October 2, 1957. It was the climax of two days of festivities which included a parade of limousines featuring stars from the film, receptions, and a costume ball held at the [[Freedom Hall|Freedom Hall Coliseum]].<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1957/10/03/archives/raintree-county-has-festive-debut-movie-based-on-lockridge-novel.html|title='Raintree County' Has Festive Debut; Movie Based on Lockridge Novel Attracts 5,000 to Louisville Ceremonies|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The New York Times|page=32|date=October 3, 1957|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref> The next month, it was reported that fifteen minutes would be cut from the film for its general release.<ref>{{cite news|last=Thomas|first=Bob|url=https://www.newspapers.com/newspage/581020680|title=Next Oscar Fete 'Best'|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The Valley Times|page=4|date=November 30, 1957|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref> For the film's wide release, [[Panavision]] president [[Robert Gottschalk]] stated ''Raintree County'' would be released in certain theaters on large-format [[70mm film]] prints for [[Roadshow theatrical release|roadshow theatrical engagements]] and on standard [[35 mm movie film|35mm film]] prints.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1957/10/30/archives/film-concern-formed-panavision-plans-features-in-widescreen-process.html|title=Film Concern Raised; Panavision Plans Features in Wide-Screen Process|url-access=subscription|newspaper=The New York Times|page=34|date=October 30, 1957|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref>

==Reception==
===Critical reaction===
[[Bosley Crowther]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' wrote "It has a beautiful, costly production that is projected onto the screen in a sharp photographic process called Camera 65. It has a fancy cast of performers, headed by Elizabeth Taylor, Montgomery Clift, Eva Marie Saint and Nigel Patrick. And it has a busy musical score by Johnny Green. But Millard Kaufman's screenplay is a formless amoeba of a thing, and therein lies the fatal weakness of this costly, ambitious film."<ref>{{cite news|last=Crowther|first=Bosley|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1957/12/21/archives/screen-mgms-raintree-county-film-of-lockridge-book-has-2theatre.html|title=Screen: M-G-M's 'Raintree County'|newspaper=The New York Times|page=22|date=December 21, 1957|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref>

''[[Harrison's Reports]]'' felt "The picture is, in fact, so verbose that one loses its drift of the story unless he pays close attention to the dialogue. One of the shortcomings in the story is the fact that the motivations of some of the characters, particularly the hero, are not too clear, making for a vagueness that diminishes its dramatic impact...The acting of the entire cast is first-rate, and there are numerous scenes that are most effective dramatically, but on the whole the story falls short of the production's impressive visual beauty."<ref>{{cite magazine|url=https://archive.org/details/harrisonsreports39harr/page/n189/mode/2up/|title="Raintree County" with Montgomery Clift, Elizabeth Taylor and Eva Marie Saint|magazine=Harrison's Reports|volume=39|issue=41|page=162|date=October 12, 1957|access-date=April 21, 2020|via=[[Internet Archive]]}}</ref>

Similarly, ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' described the film as "a big picture in terms of pictorial size but the story doesn't always match the scope of the production. Chief story fault lies in its vagueness—the not truly specified motivations of the principals and in the conflicts involved in Clift's search for happiness alternately with Miss Saint and Miss Taylor."<ref>{{cite news|url=https://archive.org/details/variety208-1957-10/page/n85/|title=Raintree County (Color–Camera 65)|magazine=Variety|page=6|date=October 9, 1957|access-date=April 21, 2020|via=Internet Archive}}</ref>

Among more recent reviews, [[Geoff Andrew]] of ''[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out]]'' called the film an "elephantine bore".<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.timeout.com/london/film/raintree-county|title=Raintree County|date=10 September 2012 |publisher=Time Out|access-date=June 21, 2013}}</ref> [[Pauline Kael]] was not enthusiastic: "A full-scale mess{{nbsp}}... the script is so literary and chaotic it seems demented{{nbsp}}... this is the kind of movie in which a heavenly choir is heard while Taylor and Clift talk about the legendary magical rain tree."<ref>{{cite book |last1=Kael |first1=Pauline |title=5001 Nights at the Movies |date=1991 |publisher=A William Abrahams/Owl Book |isbn=0-8050-1366-0}}</ref>

On the review aggregate website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film has an approval rating of 10% based on 10 reviews with an average rating of 5.20/10.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/raintree_county/|title=Raintree County|publisher=Rotten Tomatoes|access-date=June 21, 2014}}</ref>

===Box office===
According to Lewis, "the film opened in a few spots, but business was not good. Everyone at M-G-M began denying anything to do with it, but when it opened soon after in its general run, the business was enormous. Then the stampede began to grab credit." He wrote studio executive [[Eddie Mannix]] "was amused with the whole situationand had a good laugh with me. He was still the only one at MGM who had purpose, ability, and human understanding."<ref>Lewis p 215</ref>

In January 1959 ''Variety'' estimated the film would make $6 million in North America.<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=Variety|url=https://archive.org/details/variety213-1959-01/mode/1up?|date=7 January 1959|title=Top grossers of 1958|page=48}}</ref> According to MGM records, the film earned $5,830,000 in North America and $3,250,000 internationally. However, because of its high cost, the movie recorded a loss of $484,000.<ref name="Mannix"/><ref>{{cite magazine|url=https://archive.org/details/variety213-1959-01/page/n45/|title=Top Grossers of 1958|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=January 7, 1959|page=48|via=[[Internet Archive]]|access-date=April 21, 2020}}</ref>
