'''''Once Upon a Time… in Hollywood'''''{{efn|name=stylized|Stylized in promotional materials as '''''Once Upon a Time in... Hollywood''''' or known simply as '''''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'''''.}} is a 2019 [[comedy-drama]] film written and directed by [[Quentin Tarantino]], who produced it with [[David Heyman]] and [[Shannon McIntosh (filmmaker)|Shannon McIntosh]]. Produced by [[Columbia Pictures]] in association with [[Bona Film Group]], [[Heyday Films]], and Visiona Romantica, and distributed by [[Sony Pictures Releasing]], it is a co-production between the [[Cinema of the United States|United States]], [[Cinema of the United Kingdom|United Kingdom]], and [[Cinema of China|China]]. 

It features an [[ensemble cast]] led by [[Leonardo DiCaprio]] as Rick Dalton, an aging Western hero; [[Brad Pitt]] as his stunt double Cliff Booth; and [[Margot Robbie]] as actress [[Sharon Tate]], wife of Roman Polanski. Set in 1969 Los Angeles, the [[Period drama|period]] film follows a fading actor (DiCaprio) and his [[stunt double]] (Pitt) as they navigate the [[New Hollywood|rapidly changing film industry]]. The threat of the [[Tate–LaBianca murders#Tate murders|Tate murders]] is looming.

Announced in July 2017, this is Tarantino's first film made without producers [[Bob Weinstein|Bob]] and [[Harvey Weinstein]]. Tarantino ended his partnership with the brothers following the [[Harvey Weinstein sexual abuse cases|sexual abuse allegations against Harvey Weinstein]]. 

After a bidding war, the film was distributed by Sony Pictures Releasing, which met Tarantino's demands, including [[final cut privilege]]. Pitt, DiCaprio, Robbie, [[Zoë Bell]], [[Kurt Russell]] and others joined the cast between January and June 2018. [[Principal photography]] lasted from June to November around Los Angeles. ''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'' is the final film to feature [[Luke Perry]], who died on March 4, 2019; it is dedicated to his memory.

''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'' premiered at the [[2019 Cannes Film Festival|Cannes Film Festival]] on May 21, 2019. There it was shown as a [[35 mm movie film|35 mm]] film print.<ref name=":1">{{Cite web |last=Heidsiek |first=von Birgit |date=2019-11-19 |title=Immer noch Retro |url=https://www.filmundtvkamera.de/technik/immer-noch-retro/ |access-date=2026-07-12 |website=Film + TV Kamera |language=de-DE}}</ref> The film was released theatrically in the United States on July 26, and in the United Kingdom on August 14. It grossed over $393&nbsp;million worldwide and received acclaim from critics, although historical inaccuracies and the portrayals of some artists were criticized. == Plot ==
<!-- Per WP:FILMPLOT, plot summaries for feature films should be between 400 and 700 words only.--><!--Character names with quotations appear so because they appear as such in the film's credits-->
In February 1969, [[Cinema of the United States|Hollywood]] actor Rick Dalton, a former [[Westerns on television|Western TV star]], copes with a fading career, his most recent roles being [[guest appearance]]s as TV villains. Agent Marvin Schwarz suggests working in [[spaghetti western|spaghetti Western]]s in Italy, which Rick considers inferior.

Rick's best friend, [[stunt double]], personal assistant, and driver is Cliff Booth, a [[World War II]] special ops veteran living in a [[mobile home|trailer]] with his [[pit bull]] Brandy. Cliff struggles to find stunt work due to a past altercation with [[Bruce Lee]] and rumors that he murdered his wife. Although he does not know them personally, Rick is aware that young actress [[Sharon Tate]] and her husband, director [[Roman Polanski]], live [[10050 Cielo Drive|next door]]. Rick longs to befriend them, thinking it could reignite his career.

While fixing the TV antenna on Rick's roof, Cliff notices a hippie arriving at the Polanski residence. The man, [[Charles Manson]], asks for music producer [[Terry Melcher]], but Tate's friend, [[Jay Sebring]], says Melcher has moved. Later, Tate watches herself in [[The Wrecking Crew (1968 film)|''The Wrecking Crew'']] at a local [[Fox Bruin Theater|film theater]]. Cliff gives hitchhiker [[Ruth Ann Moorehouse|Pussycat]] a ride to [[Spahn Ranch]], a former Western film set where Cliff did stunt work.

Cliff checks on [[George Spahn]], the ranch's nearly blind owner, seeking to make sure the [[Manson Family|hippies living there]] are not exploiting him. Cliff physically forces ranch hippie [[Clem Grogan|Clem]] to change his car tire after it was deliberately punctured. The hippies' leader [[Tex Watson|Tex]] is summoned to deal with the situation, but he does not arrive until Cliff is driving away.

While filming an episode for the TV series [[Lancer (TV series)|''Lancer'']], Rick struggles to deliver his lines, causing him to have an emotional breakdown in his trailer. He later gives a powerful performance during his character's speech, impressing director [[Sam Wanamaker]] and his co-star Trudi, boosting his confidence.

Schwarz books Rick to star in a [[Sergio Corbucci]] spaghetti Western. Cliff accompanies Rick for the six-month shoot in Italy, where Rick films three additional films and marries Italian starlet Francesca Capucci. Before returning to the US, a troubled Rick tells Cliff that he can no longer afford his salary, which Cliff amicably understands.

Returning to Los Angeles on August 8, 1969, Rick and Cliff go drinking to commemorate their time together. Returning to Rick's house, Cliff smokes an [[LSD|acid]]-laced cigarette and takes Brandy for a walk, while Rick makes [[margarita]]s. Manson's followers Tex, [[Susan Atkins|Sadie]], [[Patricia Krenwinkel|Katie]] and [[Linda Kasabian|Flowerchild]] arrive to [[Tate–LaBianca murders#Tate murders|murder the Tate house occupants]]. Hearing a car's loud [[muffler]], an enraged Rick orders the group off the private street. Recognizing him, the family members decide to kill him instead, when Sadie reasons that through television shows such as Rick's, Hollywood has "taught them to murder".

Flowerchild deserts the group, fleeing with their car. Breaking into Rick's house armed with knives and a gun, the remaining group confronts Capucci and Cliff, with Tex announcing that he is “the Devil and here to do the Devil’s business”. Cliff recognizes them from Spahn Ranch and orders Brandy to attack. Brandy mauls Tex and bites off his genitals, and Cliff finishes him off by stomping him to death. He is stabbed by Katie, but swiftly beats her to death using random objects around the home. Brandy attacks and disfigures Sadie, who crashes through the back garden and startles Rick, who has been listening to music and was totally unaware of the fighting inside. As Sadie attempts to shoot him, Rick retrieves a working stunt [[flamethrower]] from a shed and incinerates her with it.

After the police have taken statements and an ambulance brings Cliff to the hospital, Sebring and Tate invite Rick to Tate's house for a drink.

==== Rick Dalton ====
*Dalton is an actor who starred in the fictitious [[Westerns on television|television Western]] series ''Bounty Law'' from 1959 to 1963,<ref name=OUATIH-novel/>{{rp|page11}} inspired by real-life series ''[[Wanted Dead or Alive (TV series)|Wanted Dead or Alive]]'', starring [[Steve McQueen]].<ref name="Bounty Law Wasnt a Real TV Show">{{cite news|last=Lachenal|first=Jessica|url=https://www.bustle.com/p/bounty-law-wasnt-a-real-tv-show-but-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-borrowed-from-this-classic-series-18180457|title='Bounty Law' Wasn't A Real TV Show, But 'Once Upon A Time In Hollywood' Borrowed From This Classic Series|work=[[Bustle (magazine)|Bustle]]|access-date=August 11, 2019|date=July 24, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190812040954/https://www.bustle.com/p/bounty-law-wasnt-a-real-tv-show-but-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-borrowed-from-this-classic-series-18180457|archive-date=August 12, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> After ''Bounty Law'', Dalton began to appear in supporting film roles, leading to a four-picture contract with [[Universal Pictures]], ending in 1967. His film career never took off, and in 1967 he started to guest star on TV series as villains.<ref name=OUATIH-novel/>{{rp|10–18}}

== Production ==
=== Writing and development ===
[[File:Quentin Tarantino by Gage Skidmore.jpg|thumb|right|upright|Director [[Quentin Tarantino]] developed the idea over the course of several years, looking to tell a "[[fairy tale]]" set in 1960s [[Hollywood, Los Angeles|Hollywood]].]]
The screenplay for ''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'' was developed slowly over several years by [[Quentin Tarantino]]. While he knew he wanted it to be titled ''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'', evoking the idea of a [[fairy tale]], he publicly referred to the project as ''[[Magnum opus]]''.<ref name="esquire may2019">{{cite web|url=https://www.esquire.com/entertainment/movies/a27458589/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-leonardo-dicaprio-brad-pitt-quentin-tarantino-interview/|title=Quentin Tarantino, Brad Pitt, and Leonardo DiCaprio Take You Inside 'Once Upon a Time...In Hollywood'|first=Michael|last=Hainey|date=May 21, 2019|access-date=January 19, 2020|work=[[Esquire (magazine)|Esquire]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191004074617/https://www.esquire.com/entertainment/movies/a27458589/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-leonardo-dicaprio-brad-pitt-quentin-tarantino-interview/|archive-date=October 4, 2019|url-status=live|url-access=limited}}</ref> The life of the work for the first five years was as a [[Once Upon a Time in Hollywood (novel)|novel]],<ref name="esquire may2019"/> which Tarantino considered to be an exploratory approach to the story, not yet having decided if it would be a screenplay. Tarantino tried other writing approaches: the early scene between Rick Dalton and Marvin Schwarz was originally written as a one-act play.<ref name="gamespot inspiration"/>

Tarantino discovered the centerpiece for the work about 10 years previously while filming his 2007 film ''[[Death Proof]]'' with [[Kurt Russell]] who had been working with the same stunt double, John Casino, for several years. Even though there was only a small bit for Casino to do, Tarantino was asked to use him, and agreed. The relationship fascinated Tarantino and inspired him to make a film about Hollywood.<ref name="Ellis">{{cite podcast|last=Ellis|first=Bret Easton|author-link=Bret Easton Ellis|url=https://www.patreon.com/posts/b-e-e-podcast-7-53618146|title=Quentin Tarantino Returns|work=[[Bret Easton Ellis#Podcast|The B.E.E.]]|via=[[Patreon]]|date=July 12, 2021|access-date=August 4, 2021|url-access=subscription|time=around 1:54:00|archive-date=August 4, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210804160837/https://www.patreon.com/posts/b-e-e-podcast-7-53618146|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name=recut>{{cite web|last=Thompson|first=May|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2019/05/quentin-tarantino-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-recut-longer-cannes-1202143936/|title=Quentin Tarantino Says He May Recut 'Once Upon a Time in Hollywood' to Make it Longer|website=[[IndieWire]]|access-date=September 9, 2019|date=May 23, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190529175013/https://www.indiewire.com/2019/05/quentin-tarantino-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-recut-longer-cannes-1202143936/|archive-date=May 29, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> Tarantino stated, while Casino may have been a perfect double for Russell years earlier, when he met them, "this was maybe the last or second-to-last thing they'd be doing together".<ref name="gamespot inspiration">{{cite web|url=https://www.gamespot.com/articles/quentin-tarantino-reveals-inspiration-behind-once-/1100-6472880/|title=Quentin Tarantino Reveals Inspiration Behind Once Upon A Time In Hollywood|first=James|last=O'Conner|date=January 19, 2020|access-date=January 19, 2020|work=[[GameSpot]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200120073855/https://www.gamespot.com/articles/quentin-tarantino-reveals-inspiration-behind-once-/1100-6472880/|archive-date=January 20, 2020|url-status=live}}</ref>

Tarantino first created stuntman Cliff Booth, giving him a massive backstory. Next, he created actor [[Rick Dalton]] for whom Booth would stunt double. Tarantino decided to have them be [[Sharon Tate]]'s next-door neighbors in 1969. The first plot point he developed was the ending, moving backwards from there, this being the first time Tarantino had worked this way. He thought of doing an [[Elmore Leonard]]-type story, but realized he was confident enough in his characters to let them drive the film and let it be a [[day in the life]] of Booth, Dalton, and Tate. He would use sequences from Dalton's films for the action, inspired by [[Richard Rush (director)|Richard Rush]]'s 1980 film ''[[The Stunt Man]]'', which used the scenes from the [[WWI]] film they were making within the film as the action.<ref>{{cite podcast|last=Lattanzio|first=Ryan|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2019/08/quentin-tarantino-paul-thomas-anderson-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-podcast-1202168301|title=Listen to Quentin Tarantino and Paul Thomas Anderson Talk 'Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'|website=[[IndieWire]]|access-date=September 5, 2019|date=August 24, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190829013436/https://www.indiewire.com/2019/08/quentin-tarantino-paul-thomas-anderson-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-podcast-1202168301/|archive-date=August 29, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> Further, to get his mind into Dalton, Tarantino wrote five episodes of the fictional television show ''Bounty Law'', in which Dalton had starred, having become fascinated with the amount of story crammed into half-hour episodes of 1950s western shows.<ref name=Tarantino-Deadline-Interview/>

Tarantino kept the only copy of the third act of the script in a safe to prevent it from being released prematurely.<ref>{{cite web|last=Lattanzio|first=Ryan|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2020/03/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-ending-william-paul-clark-1202219531/|title=The 'Once Upon a Time in Hollywood' Script's Ending Was Kept in a Safe in the Accounting Department|access-date=February 1, 2021|date=March 21, 2020|work=[[IndieWire]]|archive-date=November 14, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201114195337/https://www.indiewire.com/2020/03/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-ending-william-paul-clark-1202219531/|url-status=live}}</ref> DiCaprio, Robbie, and Pitt were the only other people who read the entire script.<ref>{{cite web|last=Sharf|first=Zack|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2019/05/tarantino-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-novel-script-1202143604/|title=Tarantino Spent Five Years Writing 'Hollywood' As a Novel, Only Pitt and DiCaprio Read Entire Script|access-date=February 1, 2021|date=May 11, 2019|work=[[IndieWire]]|archive-date=November 25, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201125175030/https://www.indiewire.com/2019/05/tarantino-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-novel-script-1202143604/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name=Tarantino-Deadline-Interview/> In an interview with [[Adam Sandler]], Pitt revealed that the only other copy of the script was burned by Tarantino.<ref>{{cite web|last=Setoodeh|first=Ramin|url=https://variety.com/2019/film/features/brad-pitt-adam-sandler-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-uncut-gems-1203400765/|title=Brad Pitt and Adam Sandler Have an Unlikely Movie Bromance|date=November 12, 2019|access-date=April 16, 2021|work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|archive-date=November 20, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201120231454/https://variety.com/2019/film/features/brad-pitt-adam-sandler-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-uncut-gems-1203400765/|url-status=live}}</ref>

Tarantino aimed to portray 1960s Hollywood as seen through the eyes of a child, stating:{{blockquote|the jumping off point was going to be my memory{{snd}} as a six-year old sitting in the passenger seat of my stepfather's [[Karmann Ghia]]. And even that shot, that kind of looks up at Cliff as he drives by the [[Earl Scheib]], and all those signs, that's pretty much my perspective, being a little kid&nbsp;...<ref name="New Bev Interview"/>}}

[[Principal photography]] began on June 18, 2018, in Los Angeles, California, and [[Wrap (filmmaking)|wrapped]] on November 1, 2018.<ref>{{cite web|last=Weddle|first=Adam|url=https://www.pastemagazine.com/movies/quentin-tarantino/first-trailer-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood/|title=Watch the First Teaser for Quentin Tarantino's ''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood''|date=March 20, 2019|access-date=June 17, 2021|work=[[Paste (magazine)|Paste]]|archive-date=June 18, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210618012259/https://www.pastemagazine.com/movies/quentin-tarantino/first-trailer-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood/|url-status=live}}</ref> Tarantino's directive was to turn Los Angeles of 2018 into Los Angeles of 1969 without [[computer-generated imagery]].<ref name="afar"/> For this, he tapped into previous collaborators for production: editor [[Fred Raskin]], cinematographer [[Robert Richardson (cinematographer)|Robert Richardson]], sound editor [[Wylie Stateman]] and makeup artist Heba Thorisdottir. He also brought first-time collaborators, production designer [[Barbara Ling]], based on her work recreating historical settings in ''[[The Doors (film)|The Doors]]'', and costume designer [[Arianne Phillips]].<ref name="variety making 60s">{{cite web|url=https://variety.com/2020/artisans/production/quentin-tarantino-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-2-1203484354/|title=Quentin Tarantino on Re-Creating 1960s L.A. for 'Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'|first=Jazz|last=Tangcay|date=January 29, 2020|access-date=January 29, 2020 |work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200129201909/https://variety.com/2020/artisans/production/quentin-tarantino-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-2-1203484354/|archive-date=January 29, 2020|url-status=live}}</ref> Despite Tarantino's intent, the production wound up using more than 75 digital visual effects shots by [[Luma Pictures]] and [[Lola VFX]], mainly to cover up modern billboards and erasing non-1960s buildings from driving shots.<ref>{{cite web|last=Sanai|first=Charag|url=http://vfx-online.com/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-visual-effects-breakdowns/|title=Once Upon a Time in Hollywood Visual Effects Breakdowns|year=2019|access-date=November 17, 2020|website=VFX Online: Visual Effects and Animation Directory|archive-date=November 19, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201119021141/http://vfx-online.com/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-visual-effects-breakdowns/|url-status=live}}</ref>

To film at the [[Pussycat Theater]], production designer Barbara Ling and her team covered [[Ritz Theatre (Hollywood, California)|the building]]'s [[LED]] signage and reattached the theater's iconic logo, rebuilding the letters and neon. Ling said the lettering on every marquee in the film is historically accurate. To restore [[Larry Edmunds Bookshop]], she reproduced the original storefront sign and tracked down period-appropriate merchandise, even recreating book covers. Her team restored the [[Fox Bruin Theater|Bruin]] and [[Fox Theater, Westwood Village|Fox Village]] theaters, including their marquees, and the storefronts around them. Stan's Donuts, across the street from the Bruin, got a complete makeover.<ref name="afar">{{cite news|last=Wakim|first=Marielle|url=https://www.afar.com/magazine/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-filming-locations-you-can-visit-in-los-angeles|title='Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood' Filming Locations You Can Visit in Los Angeles|website=[[Afar (magazine)|Afar]]|access-date=September 2, 2019|date=July 26, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190903051130/https://www.afar.com/magazine/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-filming-locations-you-can-visit-in-los-angeles|archive-date=September 3, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref>
[[File:Playboy Mansion North Side 2007.jpg|thumb|left|upright=1|There was a lengthy negotiation period to secure permission to film at the [[Playboy Mansion]].]]

The [[Playboy Mansion]] scene was shot at the actual mansion.<ref name=recreating-hollywood/> Tarantino was adamant about filming there, but obtaining permission took a while, since the mansion had been sold to a private owner following [[Hugh Hefner]]'s death. Tarantino and Ling met with the new owner to discuss the parts they wanted to use, but he was reluctant since the property was in the middle of a renovation. After long negotiations, he agreed, and Ling was able to dress the vacant mansion, front courtyard, and backyard for the party scene, evoking as much of the 1960s appearance of the mansion as possible.<ref name=recreating-hollywood/> The dance sequence for the scene was choreographed by [[Toni Basil]] who knew Sharon Tate and once dated Jay Sebring.<ref name=Basil/> She also choreographed Dalton's [[Hullabaloo (TV series)|''Hullabaloo'']] scene.<ref name=Basil>{{cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2019/08/06/arts/dance/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-tarantino-toni-basil.html|title=Quentin Tarantino's Goddess of Go-Go|first=Debra|last=Levine|work=[[The New York Times]]|date=August 6, 2019|access-date=May 18, 2021|archive-date=August 6, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190806163003/https://www.nytimes.com/2019/08/06/arts/dance/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-tarantino-toni-basil.html|url-status=live|url-access=limited}}</ref> Though the film is set in 1969, the mansion was actually not acquired by Playboy until 1971, resulting in an obvious anachronism.

Several important scenes were shot at the [[Musso & Frank Grill]], which was a "must have" location for Tarantino according to Rick Schuler, supervising location manager. "I feel so lucky that there's a place like the Musso & Frank Grill, one that exists now exactly how it has always been," Tarantino said. "It was fantastic being able to shoot at an iconic landmark that is so authentic and connected to Hollywood."<ref>{{cite web|last=Folven|first=Edwin|date=July 25, 2019|title=Musso and Frank celebrated on the silver screen|url=https://beverlypress.com/2019/07/musso-and-frank-celebrated-on-the-silver-screen/|access-date=October 14, 2022|website=Beverly Press & Park Labrea News|archive-date=October 14, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221014114957/https://beverlypress.com/2019/07/musso-and-frank-celebrated-on-the-silver-screen/|url-status=live}}</ref>

The scenes involving the [[10050 Cielo Drive|Tate–Polanski house]] were not filmed at Cielo Drive, the winding street where the 3,200 square-foot house once stood. The house was razed in 1994 and replaced with a mansion nearly six times the size. Scenes involving the house were filmed at three different locations around Los Angeles, one for the interior, one for the exterior, and a [[Universal City, California|Universal City]] location for the scenes depicting the iconic cul-de-sac driveway.<ref>{{cite web|last=Kiefer|first=Peter|url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/recreate-sharon-tate-s-benedict-canyon-house-quentin-tarantino-turned-a-la-tour-guide-1227436|title=To Re-create Sharon Tate's Benedict Canyon House, Quentin Tarantino Turned to an L.A. Tour Guide|access-date=April 10, 2020|date=July 27, 2020|work=[[The Hollywood Reporter]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200203050926/https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/recreate-sharon-tate-s-benedict-canyon-house-quentin-tarantino-turned-a-la-tour-guide-1227436|archive-date=February 3, 2020|url-status=live}}</ref>

Film poster artist [[Steven Chorney]] created the poster for ''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'' as a reference to ''[[The Mod Squad]]''.<ref name=Poster>{{cite web|last=Combemale|first=Leslie|url=https://www.mpaa.org/2019/08/artist-steve-chorney-on-crafting-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywoods-poster-more/|title=Artist Steve Chorney on Crafting ''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood''{{'}}s Poster & More|website=[[Motion Picture Association of America]]|access-date=September 8, 2019|date=August 12, 2019|archive-date=November 9, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201109213310/https://www.motionpictures.org/2019/08/artist-steve-chorney-on-crafting-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywoods-poster-more/|url-status=live}}</ref> He and [[Renato Casaro]] created the posters for the films within the film, ''Nebraska Jim'', ''Operation Dyn-O-Mite'', ''Uccidimi Subito Ringo Disse il Gringo'', ''Hell-Fire Texas'', and ''Comanche Uprising'', which was reprinted for Dalton's home parking spot.<ref name=Poster/> [[Mad (magazine)|''Mad'']] magazine caricaturist [[Tom Richmond (illustrator)|Tom Richmond]] created the covers of ''Mad'' and ''[[TV Guide]]'' featuring Dalton's Jake Cahill modeled after the art of [[Jack Davis (cartoonist)|Jack Davis]].<ref name=caricaturist>{{cite web|last=Minow|first=Nell|url=https://www.mpaa.org/2019/08/meet-the-caricaturist-who-created-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywoods-classic-mad-magazine-cover/|title=Meet the Caricaturist who Created ''Once Upon a Time in... Hollywood''{{'}}s Classic MAD Magazine Cover|website=[[Motion Picture Association of America]]|access-date=September 8, 2019|date=August 1, 2019|archive-date=November 9, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201109213311/https://www.motionpictures.org/2019/08/meet-the-caricaturist-who-created-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywoods-classic-mad-magazine-cover/|url-status=live}}</ref>

Tarantino told Richardson, who was serving as cinematographer on a Tarantino film for the sixth time, "I want [it] to feel retro, but I want [it] to be contemporary." They wanted the vibe of the 1960s era to be reflected in the image. Tarantinos goal was to emulate a Technicolor look with vibrant colours <ref name=":1" />. So Richardson decided to shoot on [[Kodak]] Vision3 35 mm with [[Panavision]] cameras and old anamorphic lenses (C- and E-series)<ref name=":1" />. For ''Bounty Law'', they shot in black and white, and brief sequences in [[Super 8 film|Super 8]] and 16 mm [[Ektachrome]]. In the film, ''Lancer'' was shot on a retrofitted Western street backlot at [[Universal Pictures|Universal Studios]], designed by Ling. Richardson crossed ''Lancer'' with ''[[Alias Smith and Jones]]'' for the retro-future look Tarantino wanted. The way they filmed ''Lancer'' was not possible in 1969, but Tarantino wanted his personal touch on it. The daily footage review ('dailies') was conducted in Los Angeles at Fotokem, where the material was developed and transferred to negative film. And some reviews were done directly on-site to save time.<ref name=":1" />

Richardson said that shooting the film touched him personally: "The film speaks to all of us... We are all fragile beings with a limited time to achieve whatever it is we desire... that at any moment that place will shift... so take stock in life and have the courage to believe in yourself."<ref name="recreating-hollywood">{{cite web|last=Han|first=Karen|url=https://www.polygon.com/2019/7/28/8932339/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-quentin-tarantino-playboy-mansion|title=What it took to get the Playboy Mansion for Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood|website=[[Polygon (website)|Polygon]]|access-date=September 3, 2019|date=July 28, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190906142247/https://www.polygon.com/2019/7/28/8932339/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-quentin-tarantino-playboy-mansion|archive-date=September 6, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|last=Desowitz|first=Bill|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2019/07/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-cinematographer-robert-richardson-quentin-tarantino-1969-1202159937/|title='Once Upon a Time in Hollywood': How Cinematographer Robert Richardson captured 1969 in 2019|website=[[IndieWire]]|access-date=September 3, 2019|date=July 22, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190723131120/https://www.indiewire.com/2019/07/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-cinematographer-robert-richardson-quentin-tarantino-1969-1202159937/|archive-date=July 23, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> In order to build the ''Lancer'' set Ling watched "Enormous amounts of episodes" of the series. She built a western town filled with [[adobe]] buildings. For ''Bounty Law'', she went for a dusty, dirty, early ''[[Deadwood (TV series)|Deadwood]]'' look, to separate it from the "Moneyed ''Lancer'' world".<ref name="recreating-hollywood" />

Spahn Ranch was recreated in detail over about a three-month period.<ref name=recreating-hollywood/> A wildfire completely destroyed the ranch in 1970, so the scenes for the film were filmed at nearby [[Corriganville Movie Ranch]] in [[Simi Valley]], which was also a [[movie ranch]] at one time.<ref>{{cite web|last=Desowitz|first=Bill|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2019/12/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-quentin-tarantino-spahn-ranch-scary-set-piece-1202162881/|title='Once Upon a Time Hollywood': Quentin Tarantino Channels 'Night of the Living Dead' at Spahn Ranch|date=December 2, 2019|access-date=May 28, 2021|work=[[IndieWire]]|archive-date=April 8, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210408050909/https://www.indiewire.com/2019/12/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-quentin-tarantino-spahn-ranch-scary-set-piece-1202162881/|url-status=live}}</ref> Tarantino made sure to use dogs in the scenes. He said in real life, many dogs lived on the ranch and made it feel alive. He even made sure some dogs were moving around in every shot. He was inspired to use the dogs in this manner from the way [[Francis Ford Coppola]] used helicopters in ''[[Apocalypse Now]]'' during the [[Robert Duvall]] scenes.<ref>{{cite podcast|last=Anderson|first=Paul Thomas|author-link=Paul Thomas Anderson|url=https://soundcloud.com/thedirectorscut/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-with-quentin-tarantino-and-paul-thomas-anderson-ep-216|title=Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood with Quentin Tarantino (Ep. 215)|work=[[The Director's Cut (radio show)|The Director's Cut]]|via=[[SoundCloud]]|date=August 23, 2019|access-date=September 22, 2021|time=14:00-16:00|archive-date=July 2, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210702125439/https://soundcloud.com/thedirectorscut/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-with-quentin-tarantino-and-paul-thomas-anderson-ep-216|url-status=live}}</ref>

To improve the use of [[practical effects]], Leonardo DiCaprio was allowed to light [[stunt coordinator]]s on fire while shooting scenes with a flamethrower.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.cinemablend.com/news/2549187/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-15-cool-behind-the-scenes-facts-about-quentin-tarantinos-movie|title=Once Upon A Time In Hollywood: 15 Cool Behind-The-Scenes Facts About Quentin Tarantino's Movie|date=July 2, 2020|first=Jason|last=Wiese|website=[[CinemaBlend]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201112135252/https://www.cinemablend.com/news/2549187/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-15-cool-behind-the-scenes-facts-about-quentin-tarantinos-movie|archive-date=November 12, 2020|url-status=live|access-date=November 11, 2020}}</ref> The exterior of the [[Van Nuys]] drive-in theater scene was filmed at the [[Paramount, California|Paramount]] Drive-in theater, since the Van Nuys Drive-in theater no longer exists.<ref>{{cite web|last=Tyler|first=Adrienne|url=https://screenrant.com/once-upon-time-hollywood-tarantino-all-filming-locations/|title=Where Once Upon A Time In Hollywood Was Filmed: Every Real-Life Location|access-date=October 24, 2020|date=August 1, 2020|work=[[Screen Rant]]|archive-date=November 9, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201109213310/https://screenrant.com/once-upon-time-hollywood-tarantino-all-filming-locations/|url-status=live}}</ref> As the camera rises up over the theater, the shot transitions to a miniature set with toy cars.<ref>{{cite podcast|last=O'Connell|first=Sean|url=https://www.cinemablend.com/news/2477166/cool-tricks-quentin-tarantino-used-to-recreate-1969-la-for-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood|title=Cool Tricks Quentin Tarantino Used to Recreate 1969 L.A. For Once Upon a Time in Hollywood|date=July 26, 2019|access-date=November 9, 2019|work=Reelblend|publisher=[[CinemaBlend]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191109202340/https://www.cinemablend.com/news/2477166/cool-tricks-quentin-tarantino-used-to-recreate-1969-la-for-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood|archive-date=November 9, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref>{{rp|36:00–39:00}} For some of the driving scenes, the [[Hollywood Freeway]] and [[California State Route 90|Marina Freeway]] in Los Angeles were shut down for hours to fill them with vintage cars.<ref name="RB QT1"/> The scene depicting Bruce Lee training Jay Sebring was filmed at Sebring's actual house.<ref name="New Bev Interview">{{cite interview|last=Morgan|first=Kim|url=https://thenewbev.com/blog/2019/09/tarantino-on-hollywood/|title=Tarantino on Hollywood|work=[[New Beverly Cinema]]|date=September 10, 2019|access-date=July 10, 2021|archive-date=April 3, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210403030153/https://thenewbev.com/blog/2019/09/tarantino-on-hollywood/|url-status=live}}</ref>

The scene in which Rick Dalton flubs his lines in ''Lancer'' was not in the screenplay, but rather an idea DiCaprio had on set while filming. Afterwards Tarantino came up with the idea for Dalton's "freakout" scene in his trailer, taking inspiration from [[Robert De Niro]]'s performance in [[Martin Scorsese]]'s ''[[Taxi Driver]]''. Tarantino stated, "It's got to be like [[Travis Bickle]] when he's in his apartment by himself." DiCaprio improvised the entire scene.<ref>{{cite web|last=Lindahl|first=Chris|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2019/11/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-leonardo-dicaprio-freakout-scene-1202187123/|title=Leonardo DiCaprio Improvised 'Hollywood' Freakout Scene, and Tarantino Never Saw Him More Nervous|work=[[IndieWire]]|date=November 4, 2019|access-date=July 9, 2021|archive-date=July 2, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210702002320/https://www.indiewire.com/2019/11/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-leonardo-dicaprio-freakout-scene-1202187123/|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Story, themes and character symbolism ===
Dan Schindel of ''[[Hyperallergic]]'' wrote of the symbolism in the film's [[nostalgia]]. He wrote the detail is almost "microscopic", in its use of "hundreds of period [[ephemera]]" across various media, most of which are unrecognizable to most viewers. Schindel writes that these forgotten memories surround the character of Rick Dalton because he too is a piece of forgotten nostalgia. Schindel also writes about the dynamics between the characters. Dalton and Booth represent the [[Dualism in cosmology|duality]] of attitudes towards "their seeming impending [[obsolescence]]." Booth being relaxed and accepting it and Dalton being fragile and insecure about it. Critic Raphael Abraham extends this view, noting that Tarantino's use of nostalgia in the film reaches beyond character to reimagine history itself. By turning the tragic Manson murders into a kind of fairytale, Tarantino uses revisionist storytelling to create a "joyride" through the darker moments of Hollywood's past, allowing characters like Dalton to find symbolic redemption amid the backdrop of a reimagined 1969.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Abraham |first=Raphael |date=2019 |title=Cannes: Once Upon a Time in . . . Hollywood — Quentin Tarantino's nostalgia-fuelled joyride through the Manson murders |url=https://www.ft.com/content/96fb9dea-7c73-11e9-81d2-f785092ab560 |work=Financial Times}}</ref> As Dalton's whole life is about how he is perceived, he is obsessed with how he wishes to be perceived. [[Sharon Tate]], also an actor, is filled with joy when she is able to see herself entertain a theater audience. But, Schindel says, that scene also humanizes her, making her a person, rather than the "victim" she has become. He also expresses that Dalton and Booth represent [[Old Hollywood]], while Tate represents [[New Hollywood]] and the future. Schindel states that Tarantino uses darkness, both for Booth and his questionable past as well as in the [[Manson Family]]. While Booth's possible crimes shade the nostalgia, the Manson clan shades the future. In the end, however, not only are Booth and Dalton able to save the future, but Dalton becomes the hero he always wanted to be.<ref name="Schindel">{{cite web|last=Schindel|first=Dan|url=https://hyperallergic.com/511528/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-quentin-tarantino/|title=The Dark Nostalgia of Tarantino's ''Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood''|work=[[Hyperallergic]]|date=July 31, 2019|access-date=September 6, 2021|archive-date=November 27, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201127063342/https://hyperallergic.com/511528/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-quentin-tarantino/|url-status=live}}</ref>

Travis Woods also wrote of what the three characters represent and how it is demonstrated in the film. He states that the three leads represent the past, present, and future. Dalton is the past, stuck in a fading world and afraid to let go. Booth is the present, always living in the moment, and Tate the promise of a future on the rise. They also represent three class levels of Hollywood with Booth literally living in the shadows of the movie industry. His home is a trailer in the shadows of the Van Nuys Drive-In Theater. Woods also construes how Booth being the stunt double of Dalton is illustrated throughout. Dalton struggles with an emotional arc and change, while Booth clashes with danger and physical obstacles. Woods points out the actor's job is to provide the audience with the emotional arc, while the stuntman's job is to step in for the physicality and danger, as told to us in the first scene. This is shown when Dalton faces his [[Existential crisis|existential fears]] on the set of ''Lancer'' by taking on a new acting challenge on a Western set and overcoming his fears and inner struggles. Meanwhile, Booth comes in to handle the dangerous stuff on another Western set where he also triumphs. While they both have their victories, Tate has hers as well by not only simply living her life but also by watching herself in a movie with an audience. Woods writes the finale ties it all together; "How a stunt works, and fantasy is made real: the actor performs a scene all the way up to a threat of violence. There's a cut, and the stunt double enters the scene, stands in for the actor and cheats death." And so, Dalton fearlessly confronts the would-be killers outside of his home. After a cut, "Booth enters the scene... cheats death," and handles the physical danger. At the end, Dalton re-enters and gets the glory. A feat that could not have been achieved by either the actor or stuntman alone, but only together. Woods concludes, this also represents the past and present "uniting to allow for a better future". "The past leads to the present, and the present leads to the future, and all three are required for the narrative to continue."<ref>{{cite web|last=Woods|first=Travis|url=https://www.brightwalldarkroom.com/2020/02/03/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-2019/|title='''Still Here''': A Journey Through The Funky Fanfare of ''Once Upon a Time...in Hollywood''|work=Bright Wall/Dark Room|date=2019|access-date=September 25, 2021|url-access=limited|archive-date=March 1, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210301183100/https://www.brightwalldarkroom.com/2020/02/03/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-2019/|url-status=live}}</ref>

David G. Hughes wrote of the symbolized fantasy. He noted that Tate is a "symbol of [[Collective effervescence|effervescent life]], unadulterated joy, and graceful innocence,"<ref name="Hughes"/> while [[Michael Phillips (critic)|Michael Phillips]] of the ''[[Chicago Tribune]]'' saw the character as a [[Goddess]].<ref name="Phillips">{{cite web|last=Phillips|first=Michael|author-link=Michael Phillips (critic)|url=https://www.chicagotribune.com/entertainment/movies/michael-phillips/sc-mov-once-upon-time-hollywood-rev-0722-20190723-s3wu4admbranrkpskl25ktgpq4-story.html|title='Once Upon a Time ... in Hollywood' review:South of a masterwork but still Tarantino's best in years|work=[[Chicago Tribune]]|date=July 26, 2019|access-date=October 28, 2021|archive-date=June 22, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210622054214/https://www.chicagotribune.com/entertainment/movies/michael-phillips/sc-mov-once-upon-time-hollywood-rev-0722-20190723-s3wu4admbranrkpskl25ktgpq4-story.html|url-status=live}}</ref> However Hughes was much more interested in what Booth represents. He wrote how Dalton's stress and psychological issues prevent him from being a symbol of fantasy for the audience. Booth is the film's hero and simultaneously works loyally for Dalton from a place of low social standing. Hughes states this could be "a [[Marxist]] point about invisible labor and the [[Base and superstructure|Substructure]]." However, Hughes feels this does not work to engage the audience. He draws on [[Sigmund Freud]] and his ''Psychopathic Characters on the Stage''. He says what makes Booth interesting and particularly Brad Pitt's portrayal of him is [[sex appeal]]. Being handsome, strong, loyal, and courageous makes him desirable. Hughes states that Booth is [[Laura Mulvey]]'s "perfect,... complete, more powerful ideal ego." Tarantino has Booth fight [[Bruce Lee]] to create the [[myth]] of Booth. Hughes also writes that Booth embodies the [[Buddha]]'s teachings on [[Zen]], but also that he is capable of "barbaric violence". These qualities make him the "fantasy of righteous male power". Hughes compares Booth to [[Charles Manson]], saying both are violent outcasts who sit between the worlds of Western [[Renaissance]] and [[Eastern philosophy]]. However, he says they are the [[antithesis]] of each other. While Booth has a code, Manson only serves himself. Manson "is Hollywood's monster from the [[Id, ego and super-ego|id]] [and Booth] is its [[ego ideal]] and savior."<ref name="Hughes">{{cite web|last=Hughes|first=David G.|url=https://electricghost.co.uk/a-touch-of-zen-violence-on-the-fantasy-of-cliff-booth/|title=A touch of zen and violence: on the playful fantasy of Cliff Booth|work=ElectricGhost|date=September 25, 2019|access-date=September 10, 2021|archive-date=January 16, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210116062506/https://electricghost.co.uk/a-touch-of-zen-violence-on-the-fantasy-of-cliff-booth/|url-status=live}}</ref> A [[Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Los Angeles|Los Angeles Catholic bishop]], [[Robert Barron]], praised the character of Cliff Booth as embodying the four [[cardinal virtues]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.wordonfire.org/resources/video/bishop-barron-on-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood/24840/|title=Bishop Barron on 'Once Upon A Time In Hollywood'|last=Barron|first=Robert|date=August 8, 2019|website=[[Word on Fire]]|access-date=February 27, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200227211227/https://www.wordonfire.org/resources/video/bishop-barron-on-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood/24840/|archive-date=February 27, 2020|url-status=live}}</ref>

Naomi Fry of ''[[The New Yorker]]'' wrote how the film is about the disposability of people in Hollywood. She sees Dalton and Tate as needing to be seen as their livelihoods depend on it and "an actor onscreen as a conduit for [their] own fantasies and those of others." Dalton feels he is no longer able to do this, and is tortured by the feeling. Booth has also been discarded by Hollywood to the point of Dalton having to beg for him to be used. Fry says of Dalton's career that there is "a sense of the ruthlessness of Hollywood, whose denizens are devastated when the industry almost inevitably turns away its gaze." She also notes how Tarantino "pulls a neat trick by casting DiCaprio and Pitt," two of the biggest movie stars as a has-been and a nobody.<ref name="Fry">{{cite magazine|last=Fry|first=Naomi|url=https://www.newyorker.com/culture/culture-desk/the-brutal-logic-of-stardom-in-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood|title=The Brutal Logic of Stardom in 'Once Upon a Time...in Hollywood'|magazine=[[The New Yorker]]|date=August 6, 2019|access-date=October 18, 2021|url-access=limited|archive-date=January 22, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210122135925/https://www.newyorker.com/culture/culture-desk/the-brutal-logic-of-stardom-in-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood|url-status=live}}</ref>

[[Armond White]] and [[Kyle Smith (critic)|Kyle Smith]] of ''[[National Review]]'', in separate reviews, interpreted and praised the film as being politically [[Conservatism in the United States|conservative]], with Smith writing that "It mercilessly sends up leftist values. In its foundations, it's so breathtakingly right-wing it could have been made by [[Mel Gibson]]."<ref>{{cite news|last=Smith|first=Kyle|title=The Shocking Right-Wing Tinge of Once Upon a Time . . . in Hollywood|url=https://www.nationalreview.com/2019/12/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-mocks-leftist-values/|work=National Review|date=13 December 2019|access-date=December 5, 2022|archive-date=December 5, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221205233913/https://www.nationalreview.com/2019/12/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-mocks-leftist-values/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite news|last=White|first=Armond|title=Tarantino's Once Upon a Time … in Hollywood Is His Best Film|url=https://www.nationalreview.com/2019/07/tarantinos-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-is-his-best-film/|work=National Review|date=26 July 2019|access-date=December 5, 2022|archive-date=December 5, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221205053946/https://www.nationalreview.com/2019/07/tarantinos-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-is-his-best-film/|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Finale and the Manson Family ===
The theologian [[David Bentley Hart]] wrote that ''Once Upon a Time'' "exhibit[s] a genuine ethical [[pathos]]" for its portrayal of "cosmic justice". Hart wrote how he was a child when the Tate murders occurred and that the Manson Family were "the first monsters who ever truly terrified me and tormented me with nightmares." He remembers how the children at his school would tell the stories of the Manson Family murders. Hart praised the revisionism when "Tarantino's version of the story unexpectedly veered away into some other, dreamlike, better world, where the monsters inadvertently passed through the wrong door and met the end they deserved." Hart states "the artistic masterstroke" comes in the end when Tate is heard "as a disembodied voice... speaking from that [[Parallel universes in fiction|alternative reality]], that [[World to come|terrestrial paradise]] that evil could not enter."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2019/08/06/opinion/quentin-tarantino.html|title=Quentin Tarantino's Cosmic Justice|last=Hart|first=David Bentley|date=August 6, 2019|website=[[The New York Times]]|access-date=February 27, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191209184142/https://www.nytimes.com/2019/08/06/opinion/quentin-tarantino.html|archive-date=December 9, 2019|url-status=live|url-access=limited}}</ref>

Av Sinensky wrote about the ending of the film when [[Susan Atkins]] concludes that the Manson Family members should kill Dalton because he played a character who killed people on TV, he "taught them to kill." Sinensky notes that Tarantino is putting "the words of his critics into the mouth of a Manson murderer," regarding his use of [[Violence in art|fictional gratuitous violence]].<ref>{{cite web|last=Sinensky|first=Av|url=https://medium.com/@asinensky/every-scene-in-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-ranked-366557728ba4|title=Every Scene In Once Upon A Time In Hollywood...Ranked|work=[[Medium (website)|Medium]]|date=August 14, 2019|access-date=September 18, 2021|archive-date=September 19, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210919022205/https://medium.com/@asinensky/every-scene-in-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-ranked-366557728ba4|url-status=live}}</ref> David G. Hughes opined that Tarantino uses the scene to say that those who crusade against fictional violence are hypocrites and complicit in real violence. Hughes wrote that by switching the real-life violence by Manson Family members with movie violence instead directed at them, "Tarantino is making a firm distinction between cruel real-world violence and ethical, [[Catharsis|cathartic]] fantasy violence."<ref name="Hughes"/>

Priscilla Page identified the mythological narrative of the Manson Family murders as "fram[ing] our understanding of what was happening in America and the world." In the film Spahn Ranch represents the intersection of Hollywood fantasy "and the dark underbelly of Los Angeles."<ref name="Page"/> Michael Phillips likened the Manson girls to "strung out [[Siren (mythology)|Siren]]s,"<ref name="Phillips"/> while Page described the Manson Family as "ghosts haunting Spahn Ranch... Demons to be [[exorcised]]." When Booth matches [[Tex Watson]]'s pointed gun with his pointed finger, justice lies with the fictional violence that purges the mythologized violence. Revenge is furthered as "the film denies Manson a meaningful presence... demythologizes [him], reduces him to a cameo, exposes the Manson Family as inept, and makes Sharon Tate the story's beating heart."<ref name="Page">{{cite web|last=Page|first=Priscilla|url=https://birthmoviesdeath.com/2019/09/30/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-the-final-rodeo|title=Once Upon a Time in Hollywood: The Final Rodeo|work=[[Alamo Drafthouse Cinema#Birth.Movies.Death.|Birth. Movies. Death.]]|date=September 30, 2019|access-date=October 2, 2021|archive-date=March 25, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210325183529/https://birthmoviesdeath.com/2019/09/30/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-the-final-rodeo|url-status=live}}</ref>

Steven Boone referred to Dalton going to Tate's house as "entering the [[Pearly gates|gates]] of [[10050 Cielo Drive|Cielo Drive's]] Hollywood heaven." Something his colleague Simon Abrams also alluded to when he commented, "[[Jay Sebring]] invites [Dalton] in for a drink like a hipper [[St. Peter]]."<ref name="Boone">{{cite web|last1=Abrams|first1=Simon|last2=Boone|first2=Steven|url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/was-once-a-time-hollywood-ending-obvious-1227346/|title=What to Make of the 'Once Upon a Time in Hollywood' Ending|work=[[The Hollywood Reporter]]|date=July 29, 2019|access-date=September 13, 2021|archive-date=September 14, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210914033946/https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/was-once-a-time-hollywood-ending-obvious-1227346/|url-status=live}}</ref> Dan Schindel also saw Dalton's walk up Tate's driveway as "an [[Entering heaven alive|ascent to heaven]]", based on the "rising camera movement".<ref name="Schindel"/> Naomi Fry compared Dalton going through the gates as him entering the [[Garden of Eden]].<ref name="Fry"/>

Writing in the academic journal ''[[Animation (journal)|Animation]]'', [[Jason Barker]] draws from [[Aristotle]]'s [[Poetics (Aristotle)|''Poetics'']] to analyze in detail the film's use of "[[cartoon violence]]", speculating that such violence "is more or less inversely related to the film's dramatic content". Barker concludes that: "Through self-indulgent, inane, insane and tyrannical cartoonism, ''Once Upon a Time. . . in Hollywood'' presents not so much a measure of contemporary violence, as a measure of indifference to violence: dramatic indifference and, perhaps, social indifference to a cartoon violence that is real in more ways than one."<ref>{{cite journal|title=Quentin Tarantino's Cartoon Violence|journal=[[Animation (journal)|Animation]]|publisher=[[SAGE Publishing]]|first=Jason|last=Barker|date=July 15, 2021|volume=16|issue=1–2|pages=51–63|doi=10.1177/17468477211025662|s2cid=235964706|doi-access=free}}</ref>

=== Booth's fantasy ===
Several critics interpreted Cliff Booth as an [[unreliable narrator]] when it came to him remembering his fight with Bruce Lee. "In the span of seconds" the fight "goes from being viewed by dozens of people to absolutely no one." The crowd just disappears which some believe shows the flashback to be a "[[false memory]]". The interpretation is that Booth is only remembering what he wants to and "the purpose of that scene is to show us we can't trust Cliff."<ref>{{cite web|last=Italiano|first=Eric|url=https://brobible.com/culture/article/cliff-booth-bruce-lee-fight-scene-truth-revealed/|title=Did Cliff Booth Actually Kick Bruce Lee's Ass? This Is The Telltale Sign That He's Lying|work=[[Uproxx|BroBible]]|date=February 11, 2020|access-date=October 4, 2021|archive-date=March 1, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210301020832/https://brobible.com/culture/article/cliff-booth-bruce-lee-fight-scene-truth-revealed/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite podcast|last1=Simmons|first1=Bill|last2=Fennessey|first2=Sean|last3=Ryan|first3=Chris|author-link1=Bill Simmons|url=https://m.youtube.com/watch?v=Ske4r2e9TuQ|title='Once Upon a Time...in Hollywood' Live From Sundance|work=[[The Ringer (website)#Podcasts|The Rewatchables]]|date=February 6, 2020|access-date=October 4, 2021|time=19:00-23:00|via=[[YouTube]]|archive-date=October 5, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211005043251/https://www.youtube.com/watch?app=desktop&v=Ske4r2e9TuQ|url-status=live}}</ref>

[[Steven Hyden]] of ''[[Uproxx]]'' interpreted the ending of the film as a vision of Cliff Booth brought on through his consumption of [[LSD]]. Hyden proposes that when Booth smokes the acid-cigarette and says, "And away we go," it marks the beginning of his vision. He then leaves to take his dog Brandy for a walk, walking by the car of killers down the street who Hyden believes Booth sees in the car and recognizes from [[Spahn Ranch]]. This allows Booth's imagination to run wild thanks to the acid. He imagines the killers in the car talking about his and Dalton's show, ''Bounty Law''. He then imagines a scenario that lets him play out his [[Homicidal ideation|violent fantasies]] and allows Dalton to be a hero, using a flamethrower from a film he would never actually still own but which occupies a place in Booth's memory. Hyden writes that the ending is Booth's [[Fantasy (psychology)|hallucinatory fantasy]] that allows him to stay employed by Dalton, while also allowing Dalton to be accepted by the New Hollywood elite, Sharon Tate. Also that in this fantasy Tate and members of the Manson Family are fans of Dalton, just as Booth is.<ref>{{cite web|last=Hyden|first=Steven|url=https://uproxx.com/movies/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-ending-acid-cigarette/|title=A Theory About What's Actually Happening In The Controversial Ending Of 'Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'|work=[[Uproxx]]|date=July 29, 2019|access-date=September 7, 2021|archive-date=January 3, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210103134807/https://uproxx.com/movies/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-ending-acid-cigarette/|url-status=live}}</ref>

Steven Boone of ''[[The Hollywood Reporter]]'' also commented on the ending feeling like Booth's fantasy. About the ending, he wrote "It's as if stuntman Cliff, a serene Hollywood foot soldier... was the editor here."<ref name="Boone"/> Kyle Anderson theorized the ending is not only Booth's fantasy but Dalton's as well. He states that Booth's memory of fighting Lee is "his twisted recollection of an event that probably didn't happen." Anderson notes that "Cliff is a complete [[psychopath]]" whose life has amounted to menial labor, while "Rick [is] a washed-up loser." The ending is not "just a dream of what might have happened," it is Booth's and Dalton's dream. Booth gets to fulfill his hero fantasy and instead of Dalton losing his house and career he gets to be idolized and accepted by the "cool kids".<ref>{{cite web|last=Anderson|first=Kyle|url=https://nerdist.com/article/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-ending/|title=Interpreting the Ending of Once Upon a Time in Hollywood|work=[[Nerdist]]|date=July 30, 2019|access-date=October 16, 2021|archive-date=October 22, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201022090537/https://nerdist.com/article/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-ending/|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Billie Booth ===
Anna Swanson wrote about the death of Billie and how it is used to frame the rest of the film. She writes how Tarantino not showing us what happens is a deliberate decision and also an homage to the death of Marvin ([[Phil LaMarr]]) in ''[[Pulp Fiction]]'' and the fact we do not know why Vincent Vega's ([[John Travolta]]) gun goes off and shoots Marvin. Within the film one can interpret Billie's death as Cliff's speargun accidentally going off in the same vein as Vincent's gun, or as a cold-blooded murder by Cliff and a cover up, or in a number of other scenarios. Swanson argues that which interpretation the individual viewer has will lead them to view the rest of the film through that lens and have a completely different experience than someone who views it alternatively. She notes we do not even know whose perspective the Billie Booth scene is from. It is a flashback within a flashback and so could be Cliff's memory but as it is told by Randy it could be his perspective based on what he heard. It could be what Cliff is imagining Randy is saying to Rick. It could even be an "[[omniscient]] perspective". If one views Cliff as innocent it makes him easier to like, and could be "suggesting an innocent man's life can be ruined by unfortunate circumstances beyond his control." However, if one views Cliff as guilty, "It's a depiction of the extent to which someone can literally get away with murder." In referencing the ending of the film, Swanson asks if Cliff is guilty, "Are we supposed to forgive one death he caused because of the lives he saved?" Swanson concludes that another purpose of the scene is to build up the theme of "Hollywood mythology". Referring to the scene's allusion to [[Natalie Wood]], she writes "the myths last, while the truth is lost in an ocean vaster than the rolling neon streets of the Hollywood of yore."<ref name="Swanson">{{cite web|last=Swanson|first=Anna|url=https://filmschoolrejects.com/cliff-booth-boat-scene-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood/?amp|title=Did Cliff Booth Kill His Wife? An In-Depth Investigation|work=[[Film School Rejects]]|date=December 20, 2019|access-date=October 19, 2021|archive-date=February 5, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200205164648/https://filmschoolrejects.com/cliff-booth-boat-scene-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood/?amp|url-status=live}}</ref>

Lindsey Romain says the scene is "a [[Rorschach Test]] for the audience". She argues that how the viewer interprets the scene changes the interpretation of the ending of the film. If Cliff murdered Billie then he is despicable and the killings he commits at the end are self-serving. However, if he is innocent then he is a hero. Romain writes "either read is accurate, and both feel purposeful." By leaving Billie's death open-ended, Romain believes Tarantino is asking, "Is ''Once Upon a Time… in Hollywood'' a touching [[fable]] about reclaiming relevance, or a horror story about a business that forgives heinous acts the second righteousness is procured?" Romain concludes that "maybe it's both," and "about art... about violence and how we participate in and consume it."<ref>{{cite web|last=Romain|first=Lindsey|url=https://nerdist.com/article/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-boat-scene-cliff/|title=Let's Talk About That Boat Scene in Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood|work=[[Nerdist]]|date=July 29, 2019|access-date=October 19, 2021|archive-date=May 5, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210505223450/https://nerdist.com/article/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-boat-scene-cliff/|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Red Apple ad ===
Writing for ''[[The Washington Post]]'', Sonny Bunch commented on the mid-credits Red Apple cigarettes advertisement scene. He believes it is a commentary of current filmmaking and a "pitch-perfect parody of the films that have dominated box office charts in recent history." Bunch compares the fake ad to the real ones used as mid-credit scenes in the [[DC Extended Universe|DC]], [[Marvel Cinematic Universe|Marvel]], and ''[[Fast & Furious]]'' franchises. The scenes in those films are used to advertise the next film in their franchise. He also notes how those ads tie their franchises' universes together just as Red Apple does with the Tarantino universe.<ref>{{cite news|last=Bunch|first=Sonny|url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2019/08/15/once-upon-time-hollywood-they-made-original-films/|title=Opinion: Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, they made original films|newspaper=[[The Washington Post]]|date=August 15, 2019|access-date=October 24, 2021|url-access=limited|archive-date=January 29, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210129015839/https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2019/08/15/once-upon-time-hollywood-they-made-original-films/|url-status=live}}</ref>

== Historical accuracy and influence ==

In a scene, [[Sharon Tate]] goes into Larry Edmunds Bookshop and purchases a copy of ''[[Tess of the d'Urbervilles]]''.<ref name=geek-easter-eggs/> In real life, Tate gave a copy to [[Roman Polanski]] shortly before her death. In 1978 Polanski directed the film adaptation, ''[[Tess (1979 film)|Tess]]'' (1979), dedicating it to Tate.<ref name=geek-easter-eggs/>

Tate and Polanski's [[Yorkshire Terrier]] in the film is named "Dr. Sapirstein",<ref name="geek-easter-eggs" /> as was Tate's Yorkie in real life, named after the doctor portrayed by [[Ralph Bellamy]] in ''[[Rosemary's Baby (film)|Rosemary's Baby]]''.<ref name="Tate's-dog">{{cite web|last=Romain|first=Lindsey|url=https://nerdist.com/article/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-love-letter-sharon-tate/|title=Once Upon a Time in Hollywood is a Beautiful Love Letter to Sharon Tate|date=July 26, 2019|access-date=June 26, 2021|work=[[Nerdist]]|archive-date=March 1, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210301231714/https://nerdist.com/article/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-love-letter-sharon-tate/|url-status=live}}</ref> The carrier she puts the dog in is the one that Tate actually owned.<ref name="Tate's-dog" />

=== Sharon Tate ===
After being contacted over concerns, Tarantino invited a representative of [[Roman Polanski]], [[Sharon Tate]]'s [[widower]], over to his house to read the script and report back to Polanski, to assure him "he didn't have anything to worry about". Tarantino stated: "When it comes to Polanski, we're talking about a tragedy that would be unfathomable for most human beings," and that he did not contact him while writing it, as he did not want to cause him anxiety. Despite this, Polanski's wife, [[Emmanuelle Seigner]], criticized Tarantino for using Polanski's likeness after the film's premiere.<ref name=Polanski-contacted>{{cite web|last=Sharf|first=Zack|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2019/07/roman-polanski-tarantino-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-talks-1202159276/|title=Roman Polanski Contacted Tarantino With Questions About 'Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'|website=[[Indiewire]]|access-date=August 17, 2019|date=July 19, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190720130648/https://www.indiewire.com/2019/07/roman-polanski-tarantino-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-talks-1202159276/|archive-date=July 20, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref>

Debra Tate, Sharon's sister, initially opposed the film, saying it was exploitative and perpetuated mistruths: "To celebrate the killers and the darkest portion of society as being sexy or acceptable in any way, shape or form is just perpetuating the worst of our society." After Tarantino contacted her and showed her the script, she withdrew her opposition, saying: "This movie is not what people would expect it to be when you combine the Tarantino and Manson names." She felt that Tarantino was a "very stand-up guy"; after visiting the set, she was impressed by Robbie and lent her some of Sharon's jewelry and perfume to wear in the film.<ref name="debra-tate">{{cite news|url=https://www.bustle.com/p/what-does-sharon-tates-family-think-of-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-her-sister-has-spoken-out-18226104|title=What Does Sharon Tate's Family Think Of 'Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'? After the premiere, journalist Farah Nayeri asked Tarantino why Robbie had so few lines. Tarantino responded, "I reject your hypothesis." I wanted you to see Sharon a lot."<ref name=Tarantino-Deadline-Interview/>

=== Manson Family ===
{{See also|Manson Family#Possible murder motives}}
Charles Manson was convicted of the murders of Tate and four others, despite not being present, due mostly to a theory presented by prosecutor [[Vincent Bugliosi]] that Manson was trying to instigate an apocalyptic [[race war]], leaving only Black Muslims<ref name=helter-skelter/>{{rp|246}} and the Family. According to the theory, the Black Muslims<ref name=helter-skelter/>{{rp|246}} would eventually look to Manson to lead them. According to members of the Family – [[Paul Watkins (Manson Family)|Paul Watkins]], [[Manson Family#Ongoing disruptions|Juan Flynn]] and [[Barbara Hoyt]] – Manson referred to the race war as [[Helter Skelter (scenario)|Helter Skelter]], getting the name from the [[Helter Skelter (song)|song of the same name]].<ref name=helter-skelter/>{{rp|244–247, 334, 361–362}}<ref name=watkins>{{cite book|last1=Watkins|first1=Paul|first2=Guillermo|last2=Soledad|author-link=Paul Watkins (Manson Family)|url=https://archive.org/details/my-life-with-charles-manson-by-paul-watkins_202103|title=My Life With Charles Manson|publisher=[[Bantam Books]]|location=New York|year=1979|pages=88,97–98,108–139,145–166|isbn=978-0-553-12788-1|oclc=5165161}}</ref>

Musician and filmmaker [[Boots Riley]] criticized Tarantino's film for not portraying Bugliosi's Helter Skelter narrative, or depicting the Family as [[white supremacists]],<ref>{{cite web|last=Sharf|first=Zack|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2019/08/boots-riley-calls-out-tarantino-manson-family-white-supremacists-1202168736/|title=Boots Riley Calls Out Tarantino's Hollywood for Not Depicting Manson Family as White Supremacists|website=[[IndieWire]]|access-date=August 30, 2019|date=August 26, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190829013429/https://www.indiewire.com/2019/08/boots-riley-calls-out-tarantino-manson-family-white-supremacists-1202168736/|archive-date=August 29, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> as did [[Lorraine Ali]] of the ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'', in which she wrote that portraying the Manson Family as hippies is "a more bankable image than Manson the ignorant white supremacist."<ref name="Herriman">{{cite web|last=Ali|first=Lorraine|url=https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/tv/story/2019-08-19/manson-mindhunter-once-upon-a-time-racist|title=Commentary: Charles Manson was a white supremacist. Why can't pop culture seem to admit it?|work=[[Los Angeles Times]]|date=August 19, 2019|access-date=June 27, 2021|url-access=limited|archive-date=June 9, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210609011723/https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/tv/story/2019-08-19/manson-mindhunter-once-upon-a-time-racist|url-status=live}}</ref>

However, according to members of The Family – [[Susan Atkins]], [[Leslie Van Houten]], [[Patricia Krenwinkel]], [[Catherine Share]], and [[Ruth Ann Moorehouse]] – the Tate murders were not perpetrated to start Helter Skelter, but as copycat murders mirroring that of [[Gary Hinman]], in an attempt to convince police the killer was still at large,<ref name=helter-skelter/>{{rp|426–435}} and get [[Bobby Beausoleil]] released from jail, as he was charged with Hinman's murder. He stated the murders had nothing to do with race.<ref>{{cite news|last=Bardach|first=Ann Louise|url=http://www.bardachreports.com/articles/oa_19811100.htm|title=Jailhouse Interview: Bobby Beausoleil|date=November 1981|access-date=August 26, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181119004534/http://www.bardachreports.com/articles/oa_19811100.htm|archive-date=November 19, 2018|url-status=live}}</ref>

According to [[Jay Sebring]]'s protégé and business partner [[Jim Markham]], who provided original Sebring hair products for ''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'', the murders were instigated by a drug deal gone bad, not a race war. He believes Manson was at Tate's house the day before the murders to sell drugs to Sebring and Voytek Frykowski, which resulted in the two beating Manson up.<ref>{{cite news|last=Siegel|first=Tatiana|url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/manson-family-murder-victims-friend-posits-alternative-motive-1227729|title=Manson Victim's Friend Posits Alternative Motive: 'I Never Bought Into the Race War Theory'|website=[[The Hollywood Reporter]]|access-date=August 29, 2019|date=July 30, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190731034053/https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/manson-family-murder-victims-friend-posits-alternative-motive-1227729|archive-date=July 31, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> In his interview with [[Truman Capote]], Beausoleil said, "They burned people on dope deals. Sharon Tate and that gang."<ref>{{cite book|last=Capote|first=Truman|title=A Capote Reader|publisher=[[Random House]]|year=1987|page=460|isbn=978-0-394-55647-5|url=https://archive.org/details/capotereader00trum|oclc=1334622537}}</ref>

On ''[[The Joe Rogan Experience]]'', Tarantino said he thought Bugliosi's theory was "bullshit". He believes Manson never sent anyone over to Tate's house to murder anyone, and that the murders happened spontaneously.<ref>{{cite podcast|last=Rogan|first=Joe|author-link=Joe Rogan|url=https://open.spotify.com/episode/5cdu4y60lq6QXyUbhMpVWH|work=[[The Joe Rogan Experience]]|title=#1675 - Quentin Tarantino|via=[[Spotify]]|date=June 29, 2021|access-date=June 29, 2021|time=1:34:00-1:38:00|url-access=limited|archive-date=June 29, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210629170221/https://open.spotify.com/episode/5cdu4y60lq6QXyUbhMpVWH|url-status=live}}</ref>

Hollywood' Novelization|magazine=[[Rolling Stone]]|access-date=November 23, 2020|date=November 17, 2020|archive-date=November 19, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201119110225/https://www.rollingstone.com/movies/movie-news/quentin-tarantino-books-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-novelization-1091425/|url-status=live|url-access=limited}}</ref> The [[audiobook]] is narrated by [[Jennifer Jason Leigh]] who previously starred in Tarantino's ''[[The Hateful Eight]]''.<ref name=novel>{{cite web|last=Bui|first=Hoai-Tran|url=https://www.slashfilm.com/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-audiobook/|title='Once Upon a Time in Hollywood' is Getting an Audiobook by Jennifer Jason Leigh|date=May 14, 2021|access-date=May 15, 2021|work=[[/Film]]|archive-date=May 14, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210514235102/https://www.slashfilm.com/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-audiobook/|url-status=live}}</ref> According to Tarantino, her ''Hateful Eight'' character Daisy Domergue was "A Manson girl out west, like [[Susan Atkins]] or something."<ref>{{cite web|last=Truitt|first=Brian|url=https://www.usatoday.com/story/life/movies/2015/12/23/jennifer-jason-leigh-hateful-eight-quentin-tarantino/77769400/|title=Jennifer Jason Leigh gives life to devilish Daisy in 'Hateful Eight'|work=[[USA Today]]|date=December 23, 2015|access-date=May 15, 2021|archive-date=December 28, 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151228212642/http://www.usatoday.com/story/life/movies/2015/12/23/jennifer-jason-leigh-hateful-eight-quentin-tarantino/77769400/|url-status=live}}</ref>

According to Tarantino, the novel is "a complete rethinking of the entire story," and adds details to various sequences and characters, including several chapters dedicated to the backstory of Cliff Booth.<ref>{{cite web|last=Noble|first=Alex|url=https://www.thewrap.com/quentin-tarantino-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-novel-to-explore-cliffs-backstory/|title=Quentin Tarantino: 'Once Upon a Time in Hollywood' Novel Will Explore Backstory of Brad Pitt's Character|date=June 2, 2021|access-date=June 2, 2021|work=[[TheWrap]]|archive-date=June 3, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210603024110/https://www.thewrap.com/quentin-tarantino-once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-novel-to-explore-cliffs-backstory/|url-status=live}}</ref> The novel also departs from the film, the film's finale occurs towards the beginning of the novel, and its aftermath includes Rick Dalton earning newfound fame as a regular on ''[[The Tonight Show Starring Johnny Carson]]''.<ref name=NYT-novel/> It also focuses on [[Charles Manson]]'s pursuit of a music career,<ref name=NYT-novel/> the "inner worlds" of Sharon Tate and Trudi Fraser,<ref name=Guardian-novel>{{cite web|last=Bradshaw|first=Peter|url=https://www.theguardian.com/books/2021/jun/29/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-review-tarantinos-debut-novel-shines|title=Once Upon a Time in Hollywood review - Tarantino's debut novel shines|work=[[The Guardian]]|date=June 28, 2021|access-date=June 28, 2021|archive-date=June 28, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210628232908/https://www.theguardian.com/books/2021/jun/29/once-upon-a-time-in-hollywood-review-tarantinos-debut-novel-shines|url-status=live}}</ref> and has a whole chapter focused on actor [[Aldo Ray]].<ref name=OUATIH-novel/>{{rp|337–349}}

==== ''The Films of Rick Dalton'' ====
In June 2021, Tarantino revealed he wrote and plans to publish a second novel connected to ''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'' about the films of Rick Dalton.<ref name="Goldsmith Q&A"/>{{rp|45:00–47:00}} The book details every film and TV series of Dalton's entire career, some of which are completely fictional but the majority of Dalton's work are real, with Dalton replacing the actors who actually starred in the films.<ref name="Goldsmith Q&A"/>{{rp|46:00–48:00}} In it, Cliff Booth writes a film for Dalton featuring a flamethrower, which they produce and Dalton directs.<ref name="Goldsmith Q&A">{{cite podcast|last=Goldsmith|first=Jeff|title=Once Upon a Time in Hollywood Q&A - Quentin Tarantino|url=https://podcasts.google.com/feed/aHR0cHM6Ly90aGVxYW5kYXdqZWZmZ29sZHNtaXRoLmxpYnN5bi5jb20vcnNz/episode/NmI3ZTRiNGEtOGM4ZC00YWM3LWFmNGUtNDBkY2JlMDA0MWFj?hl=en&ved=2ahUKEwiOn5-AttvxAhUD7J4KHVI1AxwQieUEegQIDRAH&ep=6|work=The Q&A|via=[[Google Podcasts]]|date=July 4, 2021|access-date=July 11, 2021|archive-date=July 11, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210711170215/https://podcasts.google.com/feed/aHR0cHM6Ly90aGVxYW5kYXdqZWZmZ29sZHNtaXRoLmxpYnN5bi5jb20vcnNz/episode/NmI3ZTRiNGEtOGM4ZC00YWM3LWFmNGUtNDBkY2JlMDA0MWFj?hl=en&ved=2ahUKEwiOn5-AttvxAhUD7J4KHVI1AxwQieUEegQIDRAH&ep=6|url-status=live}}</ref>{{rp|47:00–49:00}}

==== ''Bounty Law'' ====
Also in 2019, Tarantino expressed interest in creating a ''Bounty Law'' television series based on five half-hour scripts he wrote in preparation for the film and that he plans on writing three more episodes.<ref name=Tarantino-Deadline-Interview/> Tarantino said he does not know who would play Jake Cahill, the lead but added "if [Leonardo DiCaprio] wants to do it that'd be great."<ref name=Tarantino-Deadline-Interview/> The series would be shot in [[black and white]] and follow the half-hour format of [[Westerns on television|Western television shows]] from the 1950s.<ref name=Tarantino-Deadline-Interview/> Scenes from the episodes already written appear in ''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood''. Tarantino plans on directing all episodes of the series.<ref>{{cite web|last=Chitwood|first=Adam|url=https://collider.com/quentin-tarantino-western-tv-show-bounty-law/|title=Quentin Tarantino Has Written a Western TV Series; Wants to Direct Every Episode|access-date=October 27, 2020|date=July 18, 2020|work=[[Collider (website)|Collider]]|archive-date=October 27, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201027011041/https://collider.com/quentin-tarantino-western-tv-show-bounty-law/|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Stage ===
In June 2021 Tarantino announced his plan to produce a stage adaptation of ''Once Upon a Time in Hollywood''. He already wrote the play, which takes place in [[Italy]]. The entire second act consists of Rick Dalton, Marvin Schwarz, and [[Sergio Corbucci]] having dinner in [[Rome]].<ref>{{cite web|last=Sharf|first=Zack|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2021/06/tarantino-hollywood-stage-play-before-directing-final-movie-1234647616/|title=Tarantino Plans 'Hollywood' Stage Play Before Directing Final Movie, Which Won't Be for 'A while'|work=[[IndieWire]]|date=June 29, 2021|access-date=June 29, 2021|archive-date=June 30, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210630012721/https://www.indiewire.com/2021/06/tarantino-hollywood-stage-play-before-directing-final-movie-1234647616/|url-status=live}}</ref>
