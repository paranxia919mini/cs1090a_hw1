'''''Wolf''''' is a 1994 American [[Romance film|romantic]] [[horror film]] directed by [[Mike Nichols]] and starring [[Jack Nicholson]], [[Michelle Pfeiffer]], [[James Spader]], [[Kate Nelligan]], [[Richard Jenkins]], [[Christopher Plummer]], [[Eileen Atkins]], [[David Hyde Pierce]] and [[Om Puri]]. It was written by [[Jim Harrison]] and [[Wesley Strick]], and an uncredited [[Elaine May]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1994-06-17-ca-5065-story.html|title='Wolf' Man Jack in the Wilds of N.Y. : The Nicholson-Pfeiffer thriller meanders down several paths--a satirical look at publishing, a star vehicle and a meditation on the nature of disease and immortality.|last=Turan|first=Kenneth|work=[[Los Angeles Times]]|date=17 June 1994|access-date=26 March 2018}}</ref><ref name=":0">{{Cite book |last=Harris |first=Mark |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=0pxPEAAAQBAJ&pg=PA473 |title=Mike Nichols: A Life |publisher=Penguin Press |year=2021 |isbn=978-0-39-956224-2 |edition=1st |pages=473–477 |author-link=Mark Harris (journalist)}}</ref> The music was composed by [[Ennio Morricone]], and the cinematography was done by [[Giuseppe Rotunno]].

''Wolf'' was released by [[Columbia Pictures]] on June 17, 1994. The film received positive reviews from critics and grossed $131 million against a $70 million budget.

==Plot==
Will Randall, editor-in-chief of a large New York publishing house, runs over a large [[black wolf]] on a Vermont country road; he is bitten while attempting to help it. A few days later, [[tycoon]] Raymond Alden announces that he's taking over the publishing house. The experienced and ailing Will accepts a demotion while his unscrupulous [[protégé]] Stewart Swinton is given his previous position. Will meets Alden's headstrong yet little-accomplished daughter, Laura. Will soon finds his [[vitality]] increased and his senses sharpened, which leads him to suspect his wife Charlotte and Swinton are having an affair after smelling Swinton's scent on Charlotte's clothing. Will's suspicion is confirmed when he surprises the two in Swinton's apartment. He angrily bites Swinton's hand before leaving Charlotte.

Will begins a relationship with Laura and spends the night at the Aldens' villa after suffering a fainting spell. He awakens in a transformed state and mauls a [[deer]] in the estate's park, later awakening covered in blood. He visits pagan expert Dr. Vijay Alezais, who explains he is [[Lycanthropy|transforming into a wolf]]. He gives Will an [[amulet]] to prevent his transformation's completion. That night, during another transformation, Will breaks into a zoo, steals handcuffs from a policeman while evading arrest, and viciously attacks a group of muggers. He wakes up in his hotel, with no memory of the previous night.

Will organizes a mutiny of writers who threaten to leave the publishing house unless Will remains editor-in-chief. Alden agrees, and Will's first act is to fire Stewart. He urinates on his shoes in a bathroom, claiming he is "marking his territory". While washing his hands, Will finds a mugger's finger in his handkerchief and realizes he wounded someone. He cuffs himself to his hotel room radiator, but Laura arrives and allays his fears. They spend the night together, but Will later leaves the hotel room unnoticed and roams [[Central Park]] transformed. The next morning, Detective Carl Bridger informs Will that Charlotte was found dead in Central Park with canine DNA on her.

That evening, Laura locks Will in a horse stable at his request and makes her way to the police station to give a statement. There she runs into Swinton, who is behaving brutishly and sporting golden eyes. Laura hurries away, making arrangements for her and Will to leave the country. Swinton follows Laura and invades the estate, killing two guards. When Swinton attempts to rape Laura, Will discards the amulet and fights Swinton, ruthlessly mauling him. Laura grabs a gun from a dead guard and finishes off Swinton. Will, still in a half-human state, has a brief moment with Laura and then disappears into the [[New England]] woods. Minutes later, Laura amazes Bridger with her heightened senses as she takes her leave. Will, having transformed into a wolf, howls for Laura in the woods as Laura's eyes begin to change as Will's and Swinton's did.

==Production==
Screenwriter [[Jim Harrison]] left the production because of creative differences with director [[Mike Nichols]], claiming, "I wanted [[Apollonian and Dionysian|Dionysian]], but he wanted Apollonian. He took my wolf and made it into a Chihuahua. I cracked up for 10 minutes and then went out into the country and stood in front of a wolf den and apologized while my dog hid under the truck." Following his experience with the film, Harrison decided to leave Hollywood.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Reynolds |first=Susan Salter |date=November 24, 2002 |title=A writer at life's banquet - Page 2 |url=https://articles.latimes.com/2002/nov/24/entertainment/ca-reynolds24/2 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304233047/http://articles.latimes.com/2002/nov/24/entertainment/ca-reynolds24/2 |archive-date=2016-03-04 |access-date=2020-04-16 |website=[[Los Angeles Times]]}}</ref>

[[Mia Farrow]] was initially signed on for the role of Charlotte Randall,<ref name=":1">{{Cite magazine |last=Thompson |first=Anne |date=February 5, 1993 |title=Jack Nicholson's 'Wolf' |url=https://ew.com/article/1993/02/05/jack-nicholsons-wolf/ |access-date=January 23, 2023 |magazine=[[Entertainment Weekly]] |language=en|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230123121937/https://ew.com/article/1993/02/05/jack-nicholsons-wolf/|archive-date=January 23, 2023}}</ref> but was apparently considered too controversial a choice by the film company due to the then-current [[Woody Allen]] and [[Soon-Yi Previn]] affair.<ref name=":2">{{Cite web |last=Frook |first=John Evan |date=March 23, 1993 |title=Farrow ankles 'Wolf' |url=https://variety.com/1993/film/news/farrow-ankles-wolf-105234/ |access-date=January 23, 2023 |website=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] |language=en-US}}</ref> She decided to take a pay cut to accommodate the studios but had to eventually bow out in the long run due to other scheduling conflicts.<ref name=":2" /> [[Kate Nelligan]] was immediately cast instead.<ref name=":2" /> [[Sharon Stone]] turned down the role of Laura Alden, eventually played by [[Michelle Pfeiffer]].

Filming took place in [[New York City]], [[Long Island]], and [[Los Angeles]].<ref>{{cite web| url=http://www.onthesetofnewyork.com/wolf.html| title=Wolf Film Locations| publisher=onthesetofnewyork.com| access-date=June 5, 2018}}</ref> The exteriors for Raymond Alden ([[Christopher Plummer]])'s country mansion were filmed at [[Old Westbury Gardens]] in [[Nassau County, New York]].<ref>{{cite news| url=http://oldwestburygardens.blogspot.com/2009/02/movies-and-tv-episodes-at-old-westbury.html| title=Movies and TV Shows at Old Westbury Gardens| publisher=oldwestburygardens.blogspot.com| date=February 20, 2009| access-date=June 5, 2018}}</ref> Will Randall ([[Jack Nicholson]])'s publishing offices are in the [[Bradbury Building]] in downtown Los Angeles, a location frequently used in movies.<ref>{{cite news| url=https://www.seeing-stars.com/Locations/1994Locations1.shtml| title=Hollywood on Location 1994 Part 1| publisher=seeing-stars.com| access-date=June 5, 2018}}</ref>

The film was shot from early April to late July in 1993.<ref name=":1" /><ref>{{Cite web |last=Snow |first=Shauna |date=March 24, 1993 |title=Movies |url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1993-03-24-ca-14436-story.html |access-date=January 23, 2023 |website=[[Los Angeles Times]] |language=en-US}}</ref> Its release was delayed for six to eight months, in order to reshoot the poorly received ending.<ref name=":0" /><ref>{{Cite news |last=Weinraub |first=Bernard |date=June 12, 1994 |title=Who's Afraid of the Big Bad Book Editor? |language=en-US |work=[[The New York Times]] |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1994/06/12/movies/who-s-afraid-of-the-big-bad-book-editor.html |access-date=January 23, 2023 |issn=0362-4331}}</ref>

The wolves were professionally trained by employees from [[Thousand Oaks, California]]'s ''Animal Actors of Hollywood'' and [[Palmdale, California]]'s Performing Animal Troupe.

==Reception==

===Box office===
''Wolf'' grossed $65 million domestically and $66 million internationally, for a total of $131 million worldwide.<ref name="Wolf 1994"/>

===Critical response===
''Wolf'' holds a score of 62% on [[Rotten Tomatoes]] based on 63 reviews. The website's consensus reads, "''Wolf'' misses the jugular after showing flashes of killer instinct early on, but engaging stars and deft direction make this a unique horror-romance worth watching".<ref>{{rotten-tomatoes|id=1053942-wolf|title=Wolf}}. {{Retrieved|access-date=2026-04-23}}</ref>

[[Janet Maslin]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' wrote, "So long as it stays confined to the level of metaphor, as it does in the first hour of ''Wolf'', this idea really is irresistible."<ref>{{cite web|url=http://movies.nytimes.com/movie/review?_r=1&res=9D02EFDE143DF934A25755C0A962958260&partner=Rotten%20Tomatoes |first=Janet |last=Maslin |authorlink=Janet Maslin|title=Movie Review - Wolf - Review/Film; Wolf Bites Man, Man Sheds His Civilized Coat |work=[[The New York Times]] |date=June 17, 1994}}</ref> [[Roger Ebert]] of the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'' gave the movie three stars, and wrote, "''Wolf'' is both more and less than a traditional werewolf movie. Less, because it doesn't provide the frankly vulgar thrills and excesses some audience members are going to be hoping for. And more, because Nicholson and his director, Mike Nichols, are halfway serious about exploring what might happen if a New York book editor did become a werewolf".<ref>{{cite web |last=Ebert |first=Roger |authorlink=Roger Ebert| date=June 17, 1994 |title=Wolf |url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/wolf-1994 |access-date=January 23, 2023 |work=[[Chicago Sun-Times]]|via=RogerEbert.com}}</ref> Hal Hinson of ''[[The Washington Post]]'' wrote, "In its own delightfully peculiar way, the film is the only one of its kind ever made – a horror film about office politics{{spaces}}[...] The movie isn't wholly great; it starts to unravel just after the midway point. Still, there are charms enough all the way through to make it the most seductive, most enjoyable film of the summer".<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/longterm/movies/videos/wolfrhinson_b0097e.htm |first=Hal |last=Hinson |title='Wolf' (R) |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |date=June 17, 1994}}</ref> [[Peter Travers]] of ''[[Rolling Stone]]'' called it "a rapturous romantic thriller with a darkly comic subtext about what kills human values".<ref>{{cite magazine|url=https://www.rollingstone.com/reviews/movie/5947251/review/5947252/wolf |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071123204722/http://www.rollingstone.com/reviews/movie/5947251/review/5947252/wolf |url-status=dead |archive-date=November 23, 2007 |first=Peter |last=Travers |authorlink=Peter Travers| title=Wolf : Review |magazine=[[Rolling Stone]] |date=July 14, 1994}}</ref>

[[Desson Thomson|Desson Howe]] of ''[[The Washington Post]]'' wrote that it "works beautifully when it's rooted in reality, when the Werewolf Thing functions as a multiple metaphor for unleashed-[[Id, ego and super-ego#Id|id]] sexuality and the law of the corporate jungle"<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/longterm/movies/videos/wolfrhowe_a0b067.htm |first=Desson |last=Howe |author-link=Desson Thomson |title='Wolf' (R) |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |date=June 17, 1994}}</ref> [[Todd McCarthy]] of ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' wrote, "The studio must convince the horror/special-effects crowd to attend a Jack Nicholson/Michelle Pfeiffer/Mike Nichols picture and persuade the film-makers' fans to see a genre pic... But no matter how snazzy the trappings, when you get down to it, this is still, at heart, a werewolf picture".<ref>{{cite web |last=McCarthy |first=Todd |authorlink=Todd McCarthy |date=June 13, 1994 |title=Wolf Review |url=https://variety.com/1994/film/reviews/wolf-1200437584/ |access-date=January 23, 2023 |work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]}}</ref> ''[[Time Out (company)|Time Out]]'' wrote, "Quite frankly, it's hard to fathom why exactly anyone would have wanted to make this slick, glossy, but utterly redundant werewolf movie... Overall, this is needlessly polished nonsense: not awful; just toothless, gutless and bloodless."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.timeout.com/film/reviews/64730/wolf.html |title=Wolf Review. Movie Reviews - Film |work=[[Time Out (company)|Time Out]] |date=16 January 2012| access-date=19 November 2009}}</ref>

Audiences polled by [[CinemaScore]] gave the film an average grade of "B" on an A+ to F scale.<ref name="CinemaScore">{{cite web |url=https://m.cinemascore.com |title=CinemaScore |work=cinemascore.com}}</ref>

===Awards and nominations===
''Wolf'' won a [[Saturn Award for Best Writing]] for Jim Harrison and [[Wesley Strick]]'s screenplay, and it was nominated for a further 5 [[Saturn Awards]], in the categories of [[Saturn Award for Best Horror Film|Best Horror Film]], [[Saturn Award for Best Actor|Best Actor]] (Jack Nicholson), [[Saturn Award for Best Actress|Best Actress]] (Michelle Pfeiffer), [[Saturn Award for Best Supporting Actor|Best Supporting Actor]] ([[James Spader]]) and [[Saturn Award for Best Make-up|Best Make-up]] ([[Rick Baker (makeup artist)|Rick Baker]]).

[[Ennio Morricone]] was nominated for the [[Grammy Award for Best Score Soundtrack for Visual Media|Grammy Award for Best Instrumental Composition Written for a Motion Picture or for Television]].
