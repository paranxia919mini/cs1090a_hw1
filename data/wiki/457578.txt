'''''Pandora's Box''''' ({{langx|de|'''Die Büchse der Pandora'''}}) is a 1929 German silent [[melodrama]] film directed by [[Georg Wilhelm Pabst]], and starring [[Louise Brooks]], [[Fritz Kortner]], and [[Francis Lederer]]. The film follows Lulu, a seductive young woman whose uninhibited nature brings ruin to herself and those who love her. It is based on [[Frank Wedekind]]'s plays ''[[Earth Spirit (play)|Erdgeist]]'' ("Earth Spirit", 1895) and ''[[Pandora's Box (play)|Die Büchse der Pandora]]'' ("Pandora's Box", 1904).<ref name="comm">{{cite AV media notes | title = Pandora's Box| orig-year = 1929| others = G. W. Pabst| type = Commentary| publisher = [[The Criterion Collection]]| location = New York City, New York| id = CC1656D| year = 2006}}</ref>

Dismissed by critics on its initial release, ''Pandora's Box'' was later rediscovered by film scholars as a classic of [[Weimar Republic|Weimar German]] cinema. This film was shot at [[Staaken Studios]] in [[Berlin]]<ref>{{Cite web |last=Society |first=Louise Brooks |date=2015-12-17 |title=Louise Brooks Society: A Quick Study into the Present‐Day Location of the former Staaken Studios, by Philip Vorwald |url=https://louisebrookssociety.blogspot.com/2015/12/a-quick-study-into-presentday-location.html |access-date=2026-02-09 |website=Louise Brooks Society}}</ref>.

==Plot==
[[File:Die Büchse der Pandora (G. W. Pabst, 1929).webm|thumb|thumbtime=1|''Pandora's Box'' (1929)]]
The film is divided into eight acts plus an overture.

Lulu is the mistress of a respected, middle-aged newspaper publisher, Dr. Ludwig Schön. One day, she is delighted when an old man, her "first patron", Schigolch, shows up at the door to her highly contemporary apartment. However, when Schön also arrives, she makes Schigolch hide on the balcony. Schön breaks the news to Lulu that he is going to marry Charlotte von Zarnikow, the daughter of the  Minister of the Interior. Lulu tries to get him to change his mind, but when he discovers the disreputable-looking Schigolch, he leaves. Schigolch then introduces Lulu to strongman Rodrigo Quast. Quast wants her to join his new variety act.

The next day, Lulu goes to see her best friend Alwa, who happens to be Schön's son. Schön is greatly displeased to see her, but comes up with the idea to have her star in his son's musical production to get her off his hands. However, Schön makes the mistake of bringing Charlotte to see the revue. When Lulu refuses to perform in front of her rival, Schön takes her into a storage room to try to persuade her otherwise, but she seduces him instead. Charlotte finds them embracing.

A defeated Schön resigns himself to marrying Lulu. While the wedding reception is underway, he is disgusted to find Lulu playfully cavorting with Schigolch and Quast in the bedchamber. He gets his pistol and threatens to shoot the interlopers, but Lulu cries out not to, that Schigolch is her father. Schigolch and Quast thus escape. Meanwhile, Alwa confesses his love to Lulu. Schön returns and tells his son to leave. Once they are alone, Schön insists his new wife take the gun and shoot herself. When Lulu refuses, the gun goes off in the ensuing struggle, and Schön is killed.

At her murder trial, Lulu is sentenced to five years for manslaughter. However, Schigolch and Quast trigger a fire alarm and spirit her away in the confusion. When Alwa finds her back in the Schön home, he is initially angry, but stops Lulu when she tries calling the authorities to turn herself in. They decide to flee the country. Countess Augusta Geschwitz, herself infatuated with Lulu, lets the fugitive use her passport. On the train, Lulu is recognized by another passenger, Marquis Casti-Piani. He offers to keep silent in return for money. He also suggests a hiding place, a ship used as an illegal gambling den.

Casti-Piani sells Lulu to an Egyptian for his [[brothel]]. Lulu, Geschwitz, Alwa, Schigolch, and Quast are now passengers on the [[gambling ship]]. Quast blackmails Lulu for financing for his new act. Desperate for money to pay the Egyptian off, Alwa cheats at cards and is caught. Having turned to Geschwitz for help, Lulu now asks Schigolch. He persuades a reluctant Geschwitz to lure Quast to a stateroom, where she kills him. Lulu, Schigolch, and Alwa flee in a rowboat as the police swarm the ship.

They end up in squalor, in a drafty London garret. On [[Christmas Eve]], driven to prostitution, Lulu has the misfortune of picking a remorseful [[Jack the Ripper]]. Though he protests he has no money, she likes him and invites him to her lodging anyway. Jack is touched and secretly throws away his knife. Schigolch drags Alwa away before they are seen. Although he genuinely likes her, Jack spots a knife on a table as they embrace and cannot resist his urge to kill. Unaware of Lulu's fate, Schigolch celebrates with a group of revelers and fulfills his lifelong wish to eat [[Christmas pudding]] while a broken Alwa (who sees Jack leave) follows a passing [[Salvation Army]] parade.

==Themes==
The title is a reference to [[Pandora]] of [[Greek mythology]], who upon opening a box given to her by the [[Mount Olympus|Olympian]] gods released all evils into the world, leaving only hope behind. In the film this connection is made explicitly by the prosecutor in the trial scene.<ref name="neiss">Neiss, Doug (November 8, 2021) [https://brightlightsfilm.com/an-innocent-lulu-how-pabst-rewrote-wedekind-in-pandoras-box/ "An Innocent Lulu: How Pabst Rewrote Wedekind in Pandora’s Box"] ''[[Bright lights Film Journal]]''</ref>

The film is notable for its [[lesbian]] subplot in the attraction of Countess Augusta Geschwitz (in some prints Anna) to Lulu. The character of Geschwitz is defined by her masculine look, as she wears a [[tuxedo]]. Roberts resisted the idea of playing a lesbian.<ref name="comm"/>

==Production==
===Development===
''Pandora's Box'' had previously been adapted for the screen by [[Arzén von Cserépy]] in 1921 in Germany under the same title, with [[Asta Nielsen]] in the role of Lulu.<ref name="comm"/> As there was a musical, [[Pandora's Box (play)|plays]] and other cinema adaptations at the time, the story of ''Pandora's Box'' was well known.<ref name="comm"/> This allowed writer-director [[Georg Wilhelm Pabst|G. W. Pabst]] to take liberties with the story.<ref name="comm"/>

===Casting===
Pabst searched for months for an actress to play the lead character of Lulu.{{sfn|Brooks|2006|p=78}} After seeing American actress Louise Brooks portray a circus performer in the 1928 [[Howard Hawks]] film ''[[A Girl in Every Port (1928 film)|A Girl in Every Port]]'', Pabst tried to get her on loan from [[Paramount Pictures]].{{sfn|Hoberman|2006|p=8}} His offer was not made known to Brooks by the studio until she left Paramount over a salary dispute. Pabst's second choice was [[Marlene Dietrich]]; Dietrich was actually in Pabst's office, waiting to sign a contract to do the film, when Pabst got word of Brooks' availability.{{sfn|Hoberman|2006|p=8}} In an interview many years later, Brooks stated that Pabst was reluctant to hire Dietrich, as he felt she was too old at 27, and not a good fit for the character.{{sfn|Brooks|2006|p=78}} Pabst himself later wrote that Dietrich was too knowing, while Brooks had both innocence and the ability to project sexuality, without coyness or premeditation.<ref name="tcmart">{{cite web|author=Carr|url=https://www.tcm.com/articles/354711/pandoras-box|title=Pandora's Box|publisher=[[Turner Classic Movies]]|url-status=|archive-url=|archive-date=|date=20 October 2010|access-date=4 June 2026|first=Jay}}</ref>

===Filming===
The film was made in [[Berlin]] at the Nero-Film Studio between October 17 and November 23, 1928.<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |title=Pandora's Box (filmography page) |url=https://www.pandorasbox.com/films-of-louise-brooks/pandoras_box/ |access-date=June 12, 2024 |website=Louise Brooks Society}}</ref>{{Quote box|align=right|bgcolor=lavender|width=23%|quote="I revered Pabst for his truthful picture of this world of pleasure, which let me play Lulu naturally. The rest of the cast were tempted to rebellion."|source=–Brooks on the filming experience, 1965{{sfn|Brooks|2006|p=83}}}}
In shooting the film, Pabst drew on Brooks' background as a dancer with the pioneering [[modern dance]] ensemble [[Denishawn]], "choreographing" the movement in each scene and limiting her to a single emotion per shot.<ref name=tcmart /> Pabst was deft in manipulating his actors: he hired [[tango music]]ians to inspire Brooks between takes, coached a reluctant Alice Roberts through the lesbian sequences, and appeased Fritz Kortner, who did not hide his dislike for Brooks.<ref name=tcmart /> During the first week or two of filming, Brooks went out partying every night with her current lover, [[George Preston Marshall]], much to Pabst's displeasure.{{sfn|Hoberman|2006|pages=11–12}} When Marshall left, a relieved Pabst imposed a stricter lifestyle on his star.

Tensions rose between the performers as well, with Fritz Kortner refusing to speak to Brooks on the set.{{sfn|Brooks|2006|p=77}} Per Brooks' own recollection, "Kortner, like everybody else on the production, thought I had cast some blinding spell over Pabst that allowed me to walk through my part... Kortner hated me. After each scene with me, he would pound off the set to his dressing room."{{sfn|Brooks|2006|pages=77–79}} Despite these tensions, Brooks reflected that Pabst used the actors' personal animosities and dispositions toward one another to the benefit of the film's character dynamics, subtly urging them to sublimate their frustrations, and recalled his direction as masterful.{{sfn|Brooks|2006|pages=79–80}}

==Release and reception==
===Response and censorship===
The film debuted on February 9, 1929 at the Gloria–Palast in Berlin. It was released in Germany as a nine reel (10,676 feet) production with a reported running time of 131 minutes.<ref name=":0" />

Upon the film's release in Germany, Pabst was accused of making a "scandalous version" of Wedekind's plays, in which the Lulu character is presented as "a man-eater devouring her sexual victims."<ref name="malcolm">{{cite news|work=[[The Guardian]]|title=GW Pabst: Pandora's Box|date=21 July 1999|last=Malcolm|first=Derek|author-link=Derek Malcolm|url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/1999/jul/22/2|archive-url=https://archive.today/20200521062433/https://www.theguardian.com/film/1999/jul/22/2|archive-date=21 May 2020|url-status=live}}</ref> Critics in [[Berlin]] particularly dismissed the film as a "travesty" of its source material.{{sfn|Hoberman|2006|p=11}} The film's presentation of the Countess as a lesbian was also controversial, and Brooks was castigated as a "non-actress."<ref name="malcolm" /> According to film historians [[Derek Malcolm]] and [[J. Hoberman]], the ire Brooks received for her performance was largely rooted in her being an American.<ref name="malcolm" />{{sfn|Hoberman|2006|p=11}}

The film underwent significant [[film censorship|censorship]] in various countries: In France, the film was significantly re-edited from its original cut, making Alwa's secretary and the countess become Lulu's childhood friends, eliminating the lesbian subplot between the countess and Lulu; furthermore, in the French cut, Lulu is found not guilty at her trial, and there is no [[Jack the Ripper]] character, as the film ends with Lulu joining the [[Salvation Army]].{{sfn|Hoberman|2006|pages=8–9}} This cut was also released in the United Kingdom.<ref name="malcolm" /> Significantly truncated cuts of the film were screened in the United States, ranging various lengths, and concluding the same as the French and British cuts, with Lulu escaping to join the Salvation Army.{{sfn|Hoberman|2006|p=9}}

As well as being censored, the film was banned in Finland (1929), Norway (1929), and Sweden (c. 1929). Despite the film also being banned in The Netherlands in 1930, it was shown in Amsterdam at the De Uitkijk theatre on October 18, 1935. With the rise of the Nazi party, the film was banned in Germany from 1933 to 1945. It was also banned in Portugal (1936–1945).<ref name=":0" />

====U.S. reviews====
In the United States, the film screened in New York City beginning 1 December 1929, also in a truncated version.<ref name="dn">{{cite news|work=[[New York Daily News]]|date=3 December 1929|via=Newspapers.com|page=40|title='Pandora's Box' Silent Film at 55th Street Playhouse|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/23716728/daily_news/|author=Thirer, Irene}}</ref> Film critic Irene Thirer of the ''[[New York Daily News]]'' noted that the film suffered greatly from the edits made to what "must have been ultra-sophisticated silent cinema in its original form."<ref name=dn/> On 8 December 1929, the widely read New York-based [[trade paper]] ''[[The Film Daily]]'' also published a review; that assessment, written by Jack Harrower, was based too on his viewing a heavily censored version of the film, a version identified to have a running time of only 66 minutes.<ref name="TFD">Harrower, Jack (1929). [http://archive.org/details/filmdaily4950newy/page/1472 "''Louise Brooks in'' 'Pandora's Box' (''Silent'')"], review, ''The Film Daily'' (New York, N.Y.), 8 December 1929, p. 8. [[Internet Archive]], San Francisco. Retrieved 15 July 2019.</ref> Harrower describes what he saw as a "[[wikt:Special:Search/hodgepodge|hodgepodge]]" that offers "little entertainment" due to the film being "hacked to pieces by the censors":<ref name="TFD"/>{{blockquote|Louise Brooks, the American actress, has the part of an exotic girl who attracts both men and women. She is a sort of unconscious [[wikt:Special:Search/siren|siren]] who leads men to near-doom although her motives are apparently quite innocent.... A sweet ending has been tacked on to satisfy the censors, and with the rest of the cuts, it is a very incoherent film. It is too sophisticated for any but art theater audiences. G. W. Pabst's direction is clever, but the deletions have killed that, too.<ref name="TFD"/>}}
''Variety'', the American entertainment industry's leading publication in 1929, characterizes ''Pandora's Box'' as incoherent or "rambling" in its 11 December review.<ref name="Variety">"Waly" (1929). [http://www.archive.org/stream/variety97-1929-12#page/n101/mode/2up "Pandora's Box (German made)/(Silent)"], ''Variety'' (New York, N.Y.), 11 December 1929, p. 39. Internet Archive. Retrieved 15 July 2019.</ref> That review, attributed to "Waly", was based on the film's presentation at the [[55th Street Playhouse]] in Manhattan, which apparently screened a longer version of the motion picture than the one seen by Harrower. ''Variety'''s critic estimated the duration of the film he saw at 85 minutes; nevertheless, he also recognized the ruinous effects of extensive cuts to the film. "Management at this house [theater]", notes Waly, "blames the N.Y. censor with ending everything with Pandora and boy friends joining the Salvation Army."<ref name="Variety"/>

''The New York Times'' reviewed the film as well in December 1929. The newspaper's influential critic [[Mordaunt Hall]] also saw the picture at Manhattan's 55th Street Playhouse. Hall in his review judges it to be "a disconnected melodramatic effusion" that is "seldom interesting".<ref name="NYT">{{Cite news |last=Hall |first=Mordaunt |date=1929-12-02 |title=THE SCREEN; Paris in '70 and '71. |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1929/12/02/archives/the-screen-paris-in-70-and-71.html |access-date=2026-06-04 |work=The New York Times |language=en-US |issn=0362-4331}}</ref> He notes that Brooks is "attractive" and "moves her head and eyes at the proper moment", but he adds that he finds her facial expressions in portraying various emotions on screen "often difficult to decide".<ref name="NYT"/> Hall then concludes his remarks by indirectly complimenting the film's [[cinematography]], asserting that the German production was "filmed far better than the story deserves".<ref name="NYT"/>

Despite mixed reviews, the film drew an audience to its American debut. The ''New York Sun'' reported ''Pandora’s Box'' “ . . . has smashed the Fifty-fifth Street Playhouse’s box office records. It will therefore be held for another week”. Following New York, ''Pandora's Box'' was shown at the Little Theater in Baltimore (January 1930), Acme Theater in New York City (May 1930), Little theater in Newark, New Jersey (May 1931), and 5th Ave. Theater in New York City (December 1933). The film's last known public showing in the United States prior to its later revival was at the Sunday playhouse at [[Taliesin (studio)|Taliesin]], the Wisconsin estate of [[Frank Lloyd Wright]], in May, 1934.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Gladysz |first=Thomas |date=April 23, 2023 |title="Sin Lust Evil" in America: Louise Brooks and the Exhibition History of Pandora's Box (1929) |url=https://filmint.nu/louise-brooks-and-the-exhibition-history-of-pandoras-box-1929-thomas-gladysz/ |journal=Film International}}</ref>

===Rediscovery and restoration===
Though it was largely dismissed by critics upon initial release, ''Pandora's Box'' was rediscovered by film scholars in the 1950s, and began to accrue acclaim, eventually earning the reputation of being an unsung classic.<ref name="comm"/><ref name=pop>{{Cite web|url=https://www.popmatters.com/bfi-silent-film-pandoras-box-2515450655.html|website=[[PopMatters]]|title=The BFI Re-Opens Silent Film 'Pandora's Box'|last=Gladysz|first=Thomas|date=7 December 2017|url-status=live|archive-url=https://archive.today/20200521063449/https://www.popmatters.com/bfi-silent-film-pandoras-box-2515450655.html|archive-date=21 May 2020}}</ref> It is now considered one of the classics of [[Weimar Germany]]'s cinema, along with ''[[The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari]]'', ''[[Metropolis (1927 film)|Metropolis]]'', ''[[The Last Laugh (1924 film)|The Last Laugh]]'', and ''[[The Blue Angel]]''.<ref name="comm"/> Brooks' talent as an actress, which had been criticized upon the film's initial release (specifically by German critics), was also subject to international reappraisal and acclaim from the likes of European critics such as [[Lotte H. Eisner]], [[Henri Langlois]], and [[David Thomson (film critic)|David Thomson]].{{sfn|Tynan|2006|pages=22–23}}

Film critic [[Roger Ebert]] reviewed the film in 1998 with great praise, and remarked of Brooks' presence that "she regards us from the screen as if the screen were not there; she casts away the artifice of film and invites us to play with her." He included the film on his list of [[The Great Movies]].<ref>{{cite web|author=Ebert, Roger|work=[[Chicago Sun-Times]]|date=26 April 1998|title=Pandora's Box|url=http://rogerebert.suntimes.com/apps/pbcs.dll/article?AID=/19980426/REVIEWS08/401010349/1023|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20051226122023/http://rogerebert.suntimes.com/apps/pbcs.dll/article?AID=/19980426/REVIEWS08/401010349/1023|archive-date=26 December 2005}}</ref> On the [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], 93% of 44 critics' reviews are positive. The website's consensus reads: "Marked by GW Pabst's innovative, atmospheric direction and a surprisingly modern storyline, Pandora's Box ultimately owes its power to Louise Brooks' monumental, iconic performance."<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/pandoras_box/ |title=Pandora's Box |access-date=21 May 2020 |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] }}</ref>

When a new 35mm print was shown at Film Forum in New York City in 2006, ''Pandora’s Box'' was reported to be the second highest grossing independent film in the United States.<ref name=":0" />

In 2009, [[Hugh Hefner]] funded a restoration of the film.{{sfn|Hutchinson|2020|p=102}} This restoration was screened at the 2012 [[San Francisco Silent Film Festival]].<ref name=sfsff>{{cite web|url=https://silentfilm.org/the-preservationist-and-the-playboy-restoring-pandoras-box/|publisher=[[San Francisco Silent Film Festival]]|year=2012|title=The Preservationist and the Playboy: Restoring Pandora's Box|archive-url=https://archive.today/20200521061224/https://silentfilm.org/the-preservationist-and-the-playboy-restoring-pandoras-box/|archive-date=21 May 2020|url-status=live}}</ref> In their program, the festival noted: "As a heavily censored film that deals with the psychological effects of sexual repression, ''Pandora’s Box'' meets two of Hefner’s charitable objectives: artistic expression and a pristine new film, both finally unfurled after decades of frustration."<ref name=sfsff/> In 2023, the Hefner funded restoration was released on Blu-ray by Eureka, a UK label.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Gladysz |first=Thomas |date=January 11, 2024 |title=The Troubled History of Silent Film 'Pandora's Box' |url=https://www.popmatters.com/pandoras-box-troubled-history-feature |journal=Pop Matters}}</ref>

In a list of the 100 most important German films, compiled in 1994 by the {{interlanguage link|Association of German Cinémathèques|de|Deutscher Kinematheksverbund}}, ''Pandora's Box'' was placed at #33.<ref>{{cite journal |title=The 100 Most Important German Films |url=http://www.fiafnet.org/pdf/uk/fiaf54.pdf |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150605042344/http://www.fiafnet.org/pdf/uk/fiaf54.pdf |archive-date=5 June 2015 |journal=Journal of Film Preservation |date=April 1997 |issue=54 |pages=41-43 |url-status=dead}}</ref> Quentin Tarantino listed the movie among his 10 greatest films of all time.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Porteous |first=Samuel |date=2023-02-16 |title=The Story Behind 'Pandora's Box,' Quentin Tarantino's Favorite Silent Film |url=https://bookandfilmglobe.com/film/the-story-behind-pandoras-box-quentin-tarantinos-favorite-silent-film/ |access-date=2026-06-04 |website=Book and Film Globe |language=en-US}}</ref> In 2022, the film was ranked joint 243rd in the [[The Sight and Sound Greatest Films of All Time 2022|Sight & Sound Greatest Films of All Time]] poll, tied for that ranking with 21 other films.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/856e9118-c9db-56c4-8da6-3c4a8ba66aed/pandoras-box |title=Pandora's Box (1928) |publisher=British Film Institute}}</ref>

===Home media===
In the [[Cinema of the United Kingdom|United Kingdom]], ''Pandora's Box'' was released on DVD on 24 June 2002, by Second Sight Films.<ref name="secondsight">{{cite web|url=http://www.secondsightfilms.co.uk/pandora.htm |title=Second Sight - Classic Film on TV and DVD |access-date=28 August 2008 |publisher=Second Sight Films |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080513002208/http://www.secondsightfilms.co.uk/pandora.htm |archive-date=13 May 2008 |url-status=dead }}</ref><ref name="allmoviedvd2">{{cite web |url=https://www.allmovie.com/cg/avg.dll?p=avg&sql=16:1104162 |title=Pandora's Box (Overview) |access-date=28 August 2008 |publisher=[[Allmovie]] |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20060426195934/http://www.allmovie.com/cg/avg.dll?p=avg |archive-date=26 April 2006 }}</ref>

In North America, ''Pandora's Box'' was released on a two-disc DVD set on 28 November 2006, by [[The Criterion Collection]].<ref name="allmoviedvd">{{cite web|url=https://www.allmovie.com/movie/pandoras-box-v37169/releases |title=Die Buchse der Pandora [2 Discs] |access-date=28 August 2008 |publisher=[[Allmovie]] }}</ref> Four soundtracks were commissioned for the film's [[DVD]] release: an approximation of the score cinema audiences might have heard with a live orchestra, a [[Weimar Republic]]-era [[cabaret]] score, a modern orchestral interpretation, and an improvisational piano score.<ref name="allmoviedvd" /> On October 15, 2024, many years after this DVD edition went [[out of print]], Criterion re-released the film in the [[Blu-ray]] format, using a new [[2K resolution|2K]] scan and restoration of the film.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Cole |first=Ed Gonzalez, Jake |date=2024-10-10 |title='Pandora's Box' Blu-ray Review: The Criterion Collection |url=https://www.slantmagazine.com/dvd/pandoras-box-blu-ray-review-g-w-pabst/ |access-date=2024-10-15 |website=Slant Magazine |language=en-US}}</ref>
