'''''Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore''''' is a 1974 American [[romantic comedy]] [[Drama (film and television)|drama film]]<ref name=CommonSense>{{Cite web|url=https://www.commonsensemedia.org/movie-reviews/alice-doesnt-live-here-anymore|title = Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore - Movie Review |website=[[Common Sense Media]] |date = July 23, 2019}}</ref> directed by [[Martin Scorsese]] and written by [[Robert Getchell]].<ref name="Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore">{{cite web|url= https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/16175/alice-doesnt-live-here-any-more#credits |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20201011163344/https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/16175/alice-doesnt-live-here-any-more#credits |url-status= dead |archive-date= October 11, 2020 |title=Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore|work=[[Turner Classic Movies]]|access-date=May 25, 2016}}</ref> It stars [[Ellen Burstyn]] as a [[widow]] who travels with her preteen son across the [[Southwestern United States]] in search of a better life. [[Kris Kristofferson]], [[Billy "Green" Bush]], [[Diane Ladd]], [[Valerie Curtin]], [[Lelia Goldoni]], [[Vic Tayback]], [[Jodie Foster]], [[Alfred Lutter]], and [[Harvey Keitel]] appear in supporting roles.<ref name="Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore"/>

The film was released theatrically on December 9, 1974, by [[Warner Bros. Pictures|Warner Bros.]], and it later premiered at the [[1975 Cannes Film Festival|28th Cannes Film Festival]], where it competed for the [[Palme d'Or]]. It was a critical and commercial success, grossing $21 million on a $1.8 million budget. At the [[47th Academy Awards]], Burstyn won [[Academy Award for Best Actress|Best Actress]], and Ladd and Getchell were nominated for [[Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress|Best Supporting Actress]] and [[Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay|Best Original Screenplay]].

The film was adapted into a popular [[television series]], ''[[Alice (American TV series)|Alice]]'', that aired on [[CBS]] from 1976 to 1985.

-->In [[Socorro, New Mexico]], [[Alice Hyatt]]'s husband, Donald, a [[Coca-Cola]] delivery driver, is killed on the job in a traffic accident. A former singer, Alice sells most of her belongings, intending to take her son, Tommy, to her childhood home of [[Monterey, California]], where she hopes to pursue the singing career she abandoned when she married Donald.

Their financial situation forces them to take temporary lodgings in [[Phoenix, Arizona]], where she finds work as a lounge singer. She meets Ben, a younger, seemingly unmarried man who charms her into a sexual relationship that comes to a sudden end when his wife, Rita, confronts Alice. Ben breaks into Alice's apartment while Rita is there and physically assaults Rita in front of Alice. He also threatens Alice and smashes up her apartment. Fearing for their safety, Alice and Tommy quickly leave town.

Having spent most of what little money they had on their escape, Alice takes a job as a waitress in [[Tucson, Arizona]], at a local diner owned by Mel. There, she bonds with her fellow servers—independent, no-nonsense, outspoken Flo and quiet, timid, incompetent Vera—and meets divorced local rancher David. He quickly becomes enamored of Alice, who is wary of pursuing another relationship but begins to warm to him as he establishes a paternal relationship with Tommy.

Their relationship is threatened when David uses physical force to discipline Tommy. Although Alice still dreams of going to Monterey, they reconcile. David offers to sell his ranch and move to Monterey, but in the end, Alice decides to stay in Tucson with him.

==Production==
[[Ellen Burstyn]] was still in the midst of filming ''[[The Exorcist]]'' when [[Warner Bros. Pictures|Warner Bros.]] executives expressed interest in working with her on another project. Burstyn recalled: "It was early in the woman's movement, and we were all just waking up and having a look at the pattern of our lives and wanting it to be different&nbsp;... I wanted to make a different kind of film. A film from a woman's point of view, but a woman that I recognized, that I knew. And not just myself, but my friends, what we were all going through at the time. So my agent found ''Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore''&nbsp;... When I read it I liked it a lot. I sent it to Warner Bros. and they agreed to do it. Then they asked who I wanted to direct it. I said that I didn't know, but I wanted somebody new and young and exciting. I called [[Francis Ford Coppola|Francis Coppola]] and asked who was young and exciting and he said 'Go look at a movie called ''[[Mean Streets]]'' and see what you think.' It hadn't been released yet, so I booked a screening to look at it and I felt that it was exactly what&nbsp;... ''Alice'' needed, because [it] was a wonderful script and well written, but for my taste it was a little slick. You know – in a good way, in a kind of [[Doris Day]]–[[Rock Hudson]] kind of way. I wanted something a bit more gritty."<ref name=TCM>{{cite web|url=https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/16175/alice-doesnt-live-here-any-more#articles-reviews?articleId=236442 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201011163344/https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/16175/alice-doesnt-live-here-any-more#articles-reviews?articleId=236442 |url-status=dead |archive-date=October 11, 2020 |first=Lorraine |last=LoBianco |title=Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore |date=March 24, 2009 |website=Turner Classic Movies}}</ref>

Burstyn described her collaboration with Scorsese, making his first Hollywood studio production,<ref>{{cite news|url=https://movies.nytimes.com/movie/1479/Alice-Doesn-t-Live-Here-Anymore/overview |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071013025029/http://movies.nytimes.com/movie/1479/Alice-Doesn-t-Live-Here-Anymore/overview |url-status=dead |archive-date=2007-10-13 |title=''All Movie Guide'' overview |department=Movies & TV Dept. |work=[[The New York Times]] |first=Lucia |last=Bozzola |date=2007 |access-date=2011-05-06}}</ref> as "one of the best experiences I've ever had". He agreed with her that the film should have a message. "It's a picture about emotions and feelings and relationships and people in chaos," he said. "We felt like charting all that and showing the differences and showing people making terrible mistakes ruining their lives and then realizing it and trying to push back when everything is crumbling – without getting into [[soap opera]]. We opened ourselves up to a lot of experimentation."<ref name=TCM />

The part of Alice was offered to [[Shirley MacLaine]],<ref>{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=dvGADAAAQBAJ&q=shirley%20maclaine%20alice%20doesnt%20live%20here%20anymore&pg=PA98|last=Grist|first=Leighton|title=The Films of Martin Scorsese, 1963-77: Authorship and Context|year=2000|publisher=Springer|isbn=9780230286146}}page 98</ref> who turned it down.<ref>{{cite news|last=Foerster|first=Jonathan|title=Shirley MacLaine isn't getting old, she's just advanced|date=9 February 2011|newspaper=[[Naples Daily News]]|url=http://archive.naplesnews.com/entertainment/shirley-maclaine-isnt-getting-old-shes-just-advanced-ep-392244535-334584291.html/|access-date=27 June 2017}}</ref> In a 2005 interview, she said she regretted that decision.<ref>{{cite news|last=Modderno|first=Craig|title=Shirley MacLaine's Words of Wisdom|date=16 October 2005|newspaper=[[The New York Times]]|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2005/10/16/movies/shirley-maclaines-words-of-wisdom.html|access-date=27 June 2017}}</ref>

Scorsese's casting director auditioned 300 boys for the role of Tommy before they discovered [[Alfred Lutter]]. "I met the kid in my hotel room and he was kind of quiet and shy," Scorsese said. But when he paired him with Burstyn and suggested she deviate from the script, he held his own. "Usually, when we were improvising with the kids, they would either freeze and look down or go right back to the script. But this kid, you couldn't shut him up."<ref name=TCM />

The film was shot on location predominantly in and around Tucson, but some scenes were shot in Amado, Arizona, and Phoenix. A [[Mel's Diner]] still exists in Phoenix.<ref name=TCM />

The soundtrack includes "[[All the Way from Memphis]]" by [[Mott the Hoople]]; "[[Leon Russell (album)|Roll Away the Stone]]" by [[Leon Russell]]; "[[Daniel (Elton John song)|Daniel]]" by [[Elton John]]; [[Jeepster (song)|"Jeepster"]] by [[T.Rex (band)|T-Rex]]; and "[[I Will Always Love You]]" by [[Dolly Parton]]. During her lounge act, Alice sings "[[Where or When]]" by [[Richard Rodgers]] and [[Lorenz Hart]]; "[[When Your Lover Has Gone]]" by [[Einar Aaron Swan]]; [[Gone with the Wind (song)|"Gone with the Wind"]] by [[Allie Wrubel]] and [[Herb Magidson]]; and "[[I've Got a Crush on You]]" by [[George Gershwin|George]] and [[Ira Gershwin]].<ref>{{cite web |title=Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore Soundtrack |url=https://ringostrack.com/en/movie/alice-doesnt-live-here-anymore/8998 |website=RingosTrack.com |access-date=27 July 2023}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |last1=Feil |first1=Chris |title=Soundtracking: Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore |url=http://thefilmexperience.net/blog/2019/10/23/soundtracking-alice-doesnt-live-here-anymore.html |website=The Film Experience |access-date=27 July 2023 |date=October 23, 2019}}</ref> In a film clip from ''[[Coney Island (1943 film)|Coney Island]]'', [[Betty Grable]] is heard singing "Cuddle Up a Little Closer, Lovey Mine" by [[Otto A. Harbach]] and Karl Hoschna; and in a film clip from ''[[Hello Frisco, Hello]]'', [[Alice Faye]] performs "[[You'll Never Know]]" by [[Harry Warren]] and [[Mack Gordon]].

The opening of the film was shot in one day using a set that cost $85,000 to build.{{sfn|Wilson|2011|p=44}}

The first cut of the film was three hours and sixteen minutes long.{{sfn|Wilson|2011|p=44}}

==Reception==
[[Vincent Canby]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' called the film a "fine, moving, frequently hilarious tale", and wrote that "the center of the movie and giving it a visible sensibility is Miss Burstyn, one of the few actresses at work today&nbsp;... who is able to seem appealing, tough, intelligent, funny, and bereft, all at approximately the same moment&nbsp;... Two other performances must be noted, those of Diane Ladd and Valerie Curtin&nbsp;... Their marvelous contributions in small roles are a measure of the film's quality and of Mr. Scorsese's fully realized talents as one of the best of the new American filmmakers."<ref>{{cite news |last=Canby |first=Vincent |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1975/02/02/archives/flim-view-terrific-toughtakling-alice.html |title=Terrific, Tough-Talking 'Alice' |work=[[The New York Times]] |page=13X |date=February 2, 1975 |access-date=June 3, 2016}}</ref>

[[Roger Ebert]] of the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'' called the film "one of the most perceptive, funny, occasionally painful portraits of an American woman I've seen." He wrote: "The movie has been both attacked and defended on feminist grounds, but I think it belongs somewhere outside ideology, maybe in the area of contemporary myth and romance."<ref>{{cite news |last=Ebert |first=Roger |url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/alice-doesnt-live-here-anymore-1974 |title=Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore |work=[[Chicago Sun-Times]] |via=[[RogerEbert.com]] |date=1974-12-01 |access-date=2020-07-27}}</ref> Ebert placed the film third of his list of the best films of 1975 (even though it was released in December 1974).<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.innermind.com/misc/s_e_top.htm |title=Siskel & Ebert Top Ten Lists (1968-1998) |website=Inner Mind |date=2010-02-03 |access-date=2011-05-06}}</ref>

[[Judith Crist]] praised Burstyn for "making us care about her in all her incredibilities, stripping the character to its essential warmth as a woman, concerns as a mother, dependencies as a wife, and yearnings as an individual." But she criticized Scorsese's direction, writing that he was "putting on a camera show of his own, the handheld pursuit of the image lending an exhausting freneticism to what is melodrama enough on its own."<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Crist |first=Judith |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=6OgCAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA64 |title=A Star Outshining the Galaxy |magazine=[[New York (magazine)|New York]] |page=64 |date=January 27, 1975 |access-date=October 22, 2022 |via=[[Google Books]]}}</ref>

[[Pauline Kael]] of ''[[The New Yorker]]'' wrote, "''Alice'' is thoroughly enjoyable: funny, absorbing, intelligent even when you don't believe in what's going on—when the issues it raises get all fouled up."<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Kael |first=Pauline |url=https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1975/01/13/woman-on-the-road |title=The Current Cinema |magazine=[[The New Yorker]] |page=74 |date=January 13, 1975 |access-date=October 22, 2022 |url-access=subscription}}</ref> ''[[TV Guide]]'' rated the film three out of four stars, calling it "effective but uneven" with performances that "cannot conceal the storyline's shortcomings."<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.tvguide.com/movies/alice-doesnt-live-here-anymore/2000112760/ |title=Alice Doesn't Live Here Anywhere |website=[[TV Guide]] |access-date=2011-05-06}}</ref> Arthur D. Murphy of ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' called the film "a distended bore", adding that it "takes a group of well-cast film players and largely wastes them on a smaller-than-life film—one of those 'little people' dramas that makes one despise little people."<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Murphy |first=Arthur D. |url=https://variety.com/1973/film/reviews/alice-doesn-t-live-here-anymore-1200423290/ |title=Film Reviews: Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore |magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] |date=December 11, 1974 |access-date=May 6, 2011}}</ref>

[[Gene Siskel]] of the ''[[Chicago Tribune]]'' gave the film two stars out of four, writing, "[t]he characters aren't real, the situations in which they are placed aren't real, and, as a result, one cares little how the alleged relationships develop."<ref>{{cite news |last=Siskel |first=Gene |url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/88414671/gene-siskel-movie-reviewalice/ |title='Alice Doesn't Live Here...' |work=[[Chicago Tribune]] |at=Section 3, p. 5 |date=March 4, 1975 |access-date=October 22, 2022 |via=[[Newspapers.com]]}} {{Open access}}</ref> Charles Champlin of the ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' called Burstyn's performance "highly charged and sympathetic", and Ladd "wonderful". But he felt the film was "seemingly uncertain whether to be a stylized and updating revision of the romantic comedy modes of the late '30s or a rough-and-tumble piece of social realism flavored with bitter comedy."<ref>{{cite news |last=Champlin |first=Charles |url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/111770481/charles-champlins-review-of-alice/ |title=Widow Starts Over in 'Alice' |work=[[Los Angeles Times]] |at=Part IV, pp. 16, [https://www.newspapers.com/clip/111770520/charles-champlins-review-of-alice/ 20] |date=December 9, 1974 |access-date=October 22, 2022 |via=Newspapers.com}} {{Open access}}</ref> Similarly, [[Molly Haskell]] of ''[[The Village Voice]]'' felt the film was inconsistent in its attempt to "make a 'woman's picture' that will satisfy contemporary audiences' hunger for a heroine of some stature and significance, while at the same time allowing Scorsese to pay ironic tribute to the tear-jerkers and spunky showbiz sagas of the past and such demigoddesses as [[Alice Faye]] and [[Betty Grable]]." Overall, she felt, "the fault is largely that too many cooks have been allowed to contribute their ingredients (they're called 'life moments' and the result is inorganic soup), without a guiding intelligence."<ref>{{cite news |last=Haskell |first=Molly |title=Alice in Actors Studio Land |url=https://news.google.com/newspapers?id=AeQPAAAAIBAJ&sjid=qosDAAAAIBAJ&pg=6334%2C733735 |work=The Village Voice |page=71 |date=January 13, 1975 |access-date=October 22, 2022 |via=[[Google News Archive]]}}</ref>

On [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film has an approval rating of 92%, based on 83 reviews, with an average rating of 7.5/10. The website's consensus states: "''Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore'' finds Martin Scorsese wielding a somewhat gentler palette than usual, with generally absorbing results."<ref>{{rotten-tomatoes|alice_doesnt_live_here_anymore|type=m|access-date=December 18, 2023}}</ref> On Metacritic, the film has a weighted average score of 78 out of 100, based on 11 critics, indicating "generally favorable" reviews.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/alice-doesnt-live-here-anymore |title=Alice Doesn't Live Here Anymore Reviews |website=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=October 22, 2022}}</ref>

==Television adaptation==
{{Main|Alice (American TV series)}}

The film inspired the sitcom ''[[Alice (American TV series)|Alice]]'', which was broadcast on CBS from 1976 to 1985. The only member of the film cast to reprise his role was Vic Tayback as Mel (though his diner was moved to Phoenix). Lutter portrayed Tommy in the pilot episode but was replaced by [[Philip McKeon]] for the series. Ladd joined the show later in its run, but in a different role than she played in the film (Ladd replaced the Flo character in the series as a character named Belle). [[Linda Lavin]] took the role of Alice in the series after Burstyn said she wouldn't do television. [[Beth Howland]] played Vera in the series. The character of Flo was later spun off into yet another series, ''[[Flo (TV series)|Flo]]''.

==Home media==
[[Warner Home Video]] released the film on Region 1 DVD on August 17, 2004. It is in [[anamorphic widescreen]] with audio tracks in English and French and subtitles in English, French, and Spanish. Bonus features include commentary by Scorsese, Burstyn, and Kristofferson, and ''Second Chances'', a background look at the making of the film. [[The Criterion Collection]] released the film on Blu-ray and 4K Ultra HD on July 14, 2026.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.criterion.com/films/29004-alice-doesn-t-live-here-anymore|title=Alice Doesn’t Live Here Anymore|website=Criterion.com|access-date=July 8, 2026}}</ref>
