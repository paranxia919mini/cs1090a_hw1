'''''Prom Night''''' is a 1980 Canadian [[mystery film|mystery]] [[slasher film]]<ref name=canby/>{{sfn|Nowell|2010|page=202}} directed by [[Paul Lynch (director)|Paul Lynch]], written by [[William Gray (screenwriter)|William Gray]], and starring [[Leslie Nielsen]] and [[Jamie Lee Curtis]]. The film follows four high school seniors who are targeted at their [[prom]] by a masked killer, seeking vengeance for the accidental death of Robin Hammond, a young girl, from six years earlier. The film also stars Casey Stevens, [[Anne-Marie Martin|Eddie Benton]], [[Mary Beth Rubens]], Michael Tough, [[Robert A. Silverman]], and [[Antoinette Bower]].

Lynch began developing ''Prom Night'' after a meeting with [[Irwin Yablans]], producer of ''[[Halloween (1978 film)|Halloween]]'' (1978), who suggested he make a horror film similarly centered around a holiday. Instead, Gray wrote a screenplay adapted from a revenge-themed story by [[Robert Guza Jr.]]—an acquaintance of Lynch—using a school prom as a unifying setting. ''Prom Night'' was filmed in [[Toronto]] in late 1979.

[[Embassy Pictures|Avco Embassy Pictures]] released ''Prom Night'' on July 18, 1980, in the United States, where it was a significant financial success, breaking weekend [[box office]] records in various cities and grossing over $14 million. It was concurrently distributed by [[Astral Media|Astral Films]] in Canada, where it was the country's highest-earning horror film that year. Critical reaction to the film was mixed, with some dismissing its depictions of violence against young women, while others alternately praised it for its more subtle onscreen violence. ''Prom Night'' received some critical accolades, garnering [[Genie Awards|Genie Award]] nominations for [[Canadian Screen Award for Best Editing|editing]] and also for the [[Genie Award for Best Performance by a Foreign Actress|leading performance]] of Curtis at the [[2nd Genie Awards]]. An alternative cut of the film was popularly aired on American and Canadian TV networks in 1981, and a soundtrack album was released in Japan the same year by [[RCA Records]].

In the intervening years, ''Prom Night'' has accrued a substantial [[cult film|cult following]],{{sfn|Wise|2001|p=174}} and has been cited by some film scholars as one of the most influential slasher films of the period.{{sfn|Nowell|2010|p=10}} The film has had an extensive home-video release history from numerous distributors across various formats, with its first [[DVD]] edition issued by [[Anchor Bay Entertainment]] in 1998. A remastered [[Blu-ray]] edition of the film was released by [[Synapse Films]] in 2014.

==Plot==
In 1974, 11-year-olds Wendy Richards, Jude Cunningham, Kelly Lynch, and Nick McBride play [[hide-and-seek]] in an abandoned convent. When 10-year-old Robin Hammond tries to join them, the group scares her, leading to Robin accidentally falling to her death through a window. The children make a pact not to tell anyone what happened in fear of getting in trouble. As the four flee the convent, the shadow of an unseen person who witnessed Robin's death appears.

Six years later, Robin's elder sister, Kim, and [[Twin#Dizygotic (fraternal) twins|fraternal twin]] brother, Alex, are preparing for the school prom, which falls on the anniversary of Robin's death. Kelly, Jude, and Wendy receive anonymous [[obscene phone call]]s, while Nick ignores his ringing phone.

Kim and Nick are now dating. Jude is asked to the prom by goofy jokester Seymour "Slick" Crane. Kelly is going with Drew Shinnick, her boyfriend. Wendy—Nick's ex-girlfriend—asks school bully Lou Farmer to the prom with the purpose of embarrassing Nick and Kim. In the locker room, Kim and Kelly are startled when an unseen assailant shatters a mirror. Later, Wendy, Jude, and Kelly each find their yearbook photos stabbed with a piece of glass. Meanwhile, Kim and Alex's father, school principal Mr. Hammond, learns that Leonard Murch, a [[sex offender]] informally blamed for Robin's death, has escaped from a psychiatric facility. Lt. McBride, Nick's father, investigates.

At the prom, a figure wearing a [[balaclava (clothing)|ski mask]] targets Kelly first, slitting her throat with a mirror shard in the girls' locker room after she rejects Drew's sexual advances. Later, Jude and Slick have sex in his van outside school grounds. The masked killer appears, stabbing Jude in the throat with the mirror shard before causing Slick to drive off a cliff to his death. Meanwhile, while staking out the prom, McBride is informed that the sex offender blamed for Robin's death has been captured.

Now wielding an ax, the killer chases Wendy through an empty wing of the school before cornering her in a utility closet and hacking her to death. Mr. Sykes, the school janitor, witnesses Wendy's murder and attempts to warn the principal, but his claims are not taken seriously due to his [[Alcohol intoxication|drunkenness]]. Meanwhile, Kim and Nick prepare to be crowned prom king and queen, but Lou and his lackeys tie up Nick, and Lou takes his crown. Mistaking him for Nick, the killer decapitates him. Lou's severed head rolls onto the stage, sending the prom-goers fleeing in horror.

Kim frees Nick, and they are confronted by the killer. In the ensuing brawl, Kim strikes the killer in the head with the ax. The two briefly lock eyes before the assailant runs outside, clutching his head wound. Kim chases after him and begs approaching police officers not to shoot, before the killer collapses on the ground. He is revealed to be Alex, who, in his last moments, exclaims tearfully, "They killed her!" Alex dies in Kim's arms, crying out Robin's name.

==Production==
===Development===
Director [[Paul Lynch (director)|Paul Lynch]] developed ''Prom Night'' after a meeting with producer [[Irwin Yablans]], who had previously produced ''[[Halloween (1978 film)|Halloween]]'' (1978).{{sfn|Rockoff|2011|p=85}} Lynch had wanted to work on a horror film, and had initially pitched ''Don't Go See the Doctor'', a film about a physician who murders his patients. {{sfn|Tadros|1983|p=22}} Yablans instead suggested that Lynch utilize a holiday as a basis for the film, which resulted in Lynch deciding to build the premise around the event of the high school [[prom]].{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=3:40}} Writer [[Robert Guza Jr.]], a [[University of Southern California|USC]] film student of whom Lynch was an acquaintance, had written a story about a group of teenagers whose involvement in a tragic event as children came back to haunt them.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=4:25}} Lynch described Guza's story as "[[Stephen King]]-like," with an opening sequence in which several children accidentally kill a young classmate during a game of hide-and-seek.{{sfn|Lynch|Gray|Jankiewicz|2014|loc=0:43}}

[[William Gray (screenwriter)|William Gray]], a former ''[[Toronto Sun]]'' rock music critic who had recently co-written the supernatural horror film ''[[The Changeling (1980 film)|The Changeling]]'' (1980), was hired to adapt Guza's story into a feature-length screenplay.{{sfn|Nowell|2010|page=29}}<ref name=insurance>{{cite news|date=December 27, 1980|work=[[Times & Transcript|The Moncton Times and Transcript]]|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-moncton-transcript/192560719/|title=From insurance to scripts|page=26|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> Gray utilized Guza's central premise, expanding it into a revenge-themed slasher film.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=4:40}} Commenting on his writing, Gray said: "To do horror, you have to be a craftsman, not an artist. I'm not an artist. I'm the kind of guy who writes what people suggest. I'm a hired writer."<ref name=insurance/> While Gray is the sole credited screenwriter, the subplot involving Leonard Murch, a [[child sexual abuse|child sex offender]] blamed for Robin's death, was written by an uncredited John Hunter.{{sfn|Lynch|Gray|Jankiewicz|2014|loc=18:15–19:00}}

While attending a [[Telefilm Canada]] event in Los Angeles,{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=5:08}} Lynch met producer [[Peter Simpson (film producer)|Peter Simpson]], to whom he pitched the idea.{{sfn|Tadros|1983|p=83}} "I said, 'I'm working on this thing that is called ''Prom Night''," Lynch recalled. "[Peter] said, 'Look, let's get together and talk.' This was on a Thursday. On Monday, he read the [[Film treatment|treatment]], came back and said, 'We'll make a deal to develop a script and do ''Prom Night''.' And that's how ''Prom Night'' came about."{{sfn|Tadros|1983|p=83}} 

===Casting===
[[File:Jamie Lee Curtis in Prom Night (1980).jpg|thumb|left|upright=.7|[[Jamie Lee Curtis]] as Kim Hammond in ''Prom Night'']]
In the documentary ''[[Going to Pieces: The Rise and Fall of the Slasher Film|Going to Pieces]]'' (2006), Lynch stated he was having difficulty securing financing for the film until [[Jamie Lee Curtis]] signed onto the project.{{sfn|McQueen|2006|loc=20:47}} She received a salary of $30,000 ({{Inflation|US|30000|1980|fmt=eq}}) for her appearance in the film.<ref>{{cite news|work=[[Nanaimo Daily News]]|title=Lucrative productions|author=Adilman, Sid|date=July 20, 1980|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22378742/nanaimo_daily_news/|page=2|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> [[Eve Plumb]] (from TV's ''[[The Brady Bunch]]'') originally auditioned for the role of Kim Hammond<ref>{{Cite interview|url=http://www.terrortrap.com/interviews/petersimpson/|title=Curtains Unveiled: An Interview with Peter Simpson|last=Simpson|first=Peter |interviewer=Knowles, Jason |interviewer2=Hunter, Dan|date= August 2004|work=The Terror Trap|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190510152144/http://terrortrap.com/interviews/petersimpson/|archive-date=May 10, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> and was slated to play the role,{{sfn|Lynch|Gray|Jankiewicz|2014|loc=6:18}} but was ultimately passed over after Curtis' manager contacted Lynch about her starring in the film.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=6:24}} Lynch was enthusiastic about casting Curtis, as she had established herself in the horror genre portraying [[Laurie Strode]] in ''Halloween'' (1978), and {{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=6:50}} after auditioning for Simpson—which included a dance audition for the film's dance numbers—Curtis was officially cast in the role.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=7:15}} Lynch recalled, "For whatever reason, Peter [Simpson] made her jump through hoops, but she wanted it, and she got it."{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=7:15}} According to Curtis, ''Prom Night'' was the first project for which she "made any money."<ref name=jamie>{{cite AV media|publisher=[[Shout! Studios|Shout! Factory]]|title=My Time with Terror: An Interview with Jamie Lee Curtis|year=2013|medium=[[Blu-ray]] featurette|work=[[The Fog]]|oclc=857246789}}</ref>

[[Leslie Nielsen]], an established film and TV actor, was cast as Kim Hammond's father; this role marks one of the last serious films for Nielsen, as he spent the remainder of his career performing in comedies.{{sfn|Rockoff|2011|p=85}} Lynch sought Nielsen for the role as he was one of Canada's "most-experienced actors."{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=15:00}} Opposite Nielsen, portraying his character's wife, is [[Antoinette Bower]], whom Lynch had seen on numerous Canadian TV programs.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=19:50}} Lynch cast Bower in the role as he felt she offered an "off-putting" sensibility that would make her a viable suspect in the film's story.{{sfn|Lynch|Gray|Jankiewicz|2014|loc=9:31}}

The majority of the film's supporting actors and actresses were stage performers and recent theater graduates from the [[University of Toronto]].{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=7:30–7:45}} Michael Tough, a 17-year-old actor who was cast as Kim's younger brother, Alex, recalled that he and Curtis spent an afternoon together shopping to become familiar with each other and establish a sibling-like dynamic.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=13:56}} [[Mary Beth Rubens]], who appears as Kim's ill-fated friend, Kelly Lynch, had recently graduated from the Montreal Theatre School when she was cast in the role.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=7:40}} Joy Thompson was cast as Jude Cunningham, another of Kim's friends, stalked by the killer.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=8:08}}

Canadian actor [[Robert A. Silverman]], who was cast in the role of the mysterious school janitor Mr. Sykes, was still recovering from injuries sustained when he was struck by an automobile three years prior to making the film.{{sfn|Rockoff|2011|pages=85–86}} Lynch felt that Silverman's "off-kilter" performance, owing to his physical ailments at the time, contributed to the character's mysteriousness.{{sfn|Rockoff|2011|p=86}}

===Filming===
[[File:Don Mills Collegiate Institute.JPG|thumb|right|upright=1|The [[Don Mills Collegiate Institute]] served as the school location, as seen in 2016]]
''Prom Night'' was filmed over 24 days in [[Toronto]], with [[principal photography]] beginning August 13, 1979,<ref name=afi/> and completing September 13.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=9:02–9:14}} The majority of the crew were recent graduates from film schools in Canada.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=9:44}} [[Don Mills Collegiate Institute]] served as the main school location,<ref name=Blackadar>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-toronto-star/190150239/|work=[[Toronto Star]]|date=August 23, 1979|p=C3|title=It's just Jamie screaming|last=Blackadar|first=Bruce|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref><ref name=Irish>{{cite news|work=[[North York Mirror]]|date=August 29, 1979|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-north-york-mirror/190150400/|last=Irish|first=Paul|title=Hollywood comes to Don Mills|p=3|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> and numerous students from the institute were hired to appear as [[extra (acting)|extra]]s.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-toronto-star/190150600/|work=[[Toronto Star]]|date=August 23, 1979|last=Fillion|first=John|title='Murder' pays in Don Mills|via=Newspapers.com|p=A18}}</ref>

The Langstaff Jail Farm in Richmond Hill{{sfn|Easterbrook|MacLean|1996|page=270}} was used for the abandoned convent featured prominently at the beginning of the film, which was demolished shortly after.{{sfn|Lynch|Gray|Jankiewicz|2014|loc=1:29}} Art director Reuben Freed had wanted to take advantage of Toronto's [[Victorian architecture]], and felt that the Langstaff building had an appropriate [[Gothic Revival architecture|Gothic]] appearance.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=10:33}} Commenting on the production design, Freed said: {{quote|Making the environments, creating the sense of terror, creating from the fabric of what was around me... This was Toronto, this Victorian city, [and] there were a lot of interesting buildings to work with, and I quickly saw what was available.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=10:35–10:44}}}}

Because of the way the film is structured, most of the cast spend little time together, as each appears in their own subplot leading up to (and during) the prom.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=8:10–8:25}} The initial shoot took place in the school, as the crew had to complete before school was back in session in late August, while the remainder of the shoot was spent filming the sequences at the various characters' homes.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=11:40–11:52}} The filming of the dance numbers, which were choreographed by Lynch's sister, was completed without the use of a [[Steadicam]].{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=12:40}} Lynch stated that he was "amazed they turned out as well as they did", as the crew had little experience with filming such sequences.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=12:40–13:04}}

===Special effects===
In terms of on-screen violence, Lynch had intentionally devised the film to have a minimal amount of gore, as he did not want it to be overtly gratuitous; however, the distributor, [[Embassy Pictures|Avco Embassy Pictures]], mandated that they include some graphic violence, resulting in the on-screen beheading of Lou Farmer in the finale.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=25:24–25:35}} In order to make the sequence appear realistic, the special effects team decided to build a prop floor through which the actor, David Mucci, could place his head, creating the illusion of his severed head lying on the floor.{{sfn|Rockoff|2011|p=85}} For the filming of the decapitation, a dummy head was crafted via prosthetics, and moulded around a prop [[skull]]. {{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=26:00}}

==Music==
{{Main|Prom Night (soundtrack)}}
The ''Prom Night'' score and soundtrack were composed by [[Paul Zaza]] and Carl Zittrer, with additional writing by Bill Crutchfield and James Powell.{{sfn|Osborne|2010|p=471}} Director Lynch sought Zittrer after hearing his compositions in ''[[Black Christmas (1974 film)|Black Christmas]]'' (1974){{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=30:30}} and ''[[Murder by Decree]]'' (1979).{{sfn|Hubai|2019|page=2}} The film's score features a use of "texture-heavy, orchestral underscores" which Zaza and Zittrer recorded using 35mm [[magnetic tape|magnetic]] music tape, manually smoothing out the tape's edges with [[sandpaper]] and razors to create fade outs.{{sfn|Hubai|2019|pages=3–4}}

In addition to the score, ''Prom Night'' features multiple original [[disco]] songs by Zaza and Zittrer, which are featured prominently in the film's prom scenes.{{sfn|Hubai|2019|page=4}} Originally, the film was shot with the actors dancing to then-popular disco tracks, but, according to Zaza, the publishing rights to the songs were far outside the film's budget.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=31:10–31:44}} Under orders from producer Peter Simpson, Zaza wrote a series of original disco songs over a five-day period, which closely copied the original tracks that were intended to be used in the film,{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=32:07}} notably mimicking the tempo.{{sfn|Hubai|2019|page=4}} According to Zaza: "Simply put, our songs needed to "sound like" something you've already heard... We believed that if we could emulate the style of these hits by using similar drum patters, orchestrations, vocal arrangements, and mixing and production techniques, we could create a disco "environment" for the film that would work well with the plot."{{sfn|Hubai|2019|page=5}} The similarities between the songs prompted a $10 million [[lawsuit]] against the film's producers, which was eventually settled for $50,000.{{sfn|Felsher|2014|loc=32:45}}

An official release of the ''Prom Night'' soundtrack was issued in [[Japan]] in 1981 by [[RCA Records]], featuring both the musical score and the original disco songs from the film.{{sfn|Hubai|2019|page=6}}<ref>{{cite av media|title=Prom Night (Original Soundtrack From Motion Picture)|publisher=[[RCA Records]]|year=1981|id=RPL-8089}}</ref> A standalone 7-inch [[Single (music)|single]] was also released, featuring two tracks written for but not featured in the film: "All Is Gone" and "Forever", both performed by Blue Bazar.<ref>{{cite av media|title=Prom Night (Original Soundtrack From Motion Picture)|publisher=[[RCA Records]]|year=1981|id=RPS-43}}</ref> Two different reissues of the soundtrack were released in 2019 on vinyl, one by 1984 Publishing,<ref>{{cite web|work=[[Bloody Disgusting]]|title=[Exclusive] The Full ‘Prom Night’ Soundtrack is Coming to Vinyl and We’ve Got the Release Art!|date=April 30, 2019|last=Squires|first=John|url=https://bloody-disgusting.com/music/3558631/exclusive-full-prom-night-soundtrack-coming-vinyl-weve-got-release-art/|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201125072407/https://bloody-disgusting.com/music/3558631/exclusive-full-prom-night-soundtrack-coming-vinyl-weve-got-release-art/|archive-date=November 25, 2020}}</ref> and another by Perseverance Records.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/291812/perseverance-records-releasing-prom-night-soundtrack-on-cd-for-first-time-ever/|work=[[Dread Central]]|title=Perseverance Records Releasing PROM NIGHT Soundtrack on CD for First Time Ever|date=March 30, 2019|last=Millican|first=Josh|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250915033932/https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/291812/perseverance-records-releasing-prom-night-soundtrack-on-cd-for-first-time-ever/|archive-date=September 15, 2025}}</ref>

===Distribution and marketing===

[[Paramount Pictures]] expressed interest in distributing ''Prom Night'',{{sfn|Lynch|Gray|Jankiewicz|2014|loc=27:44}} but producer Peter Simpson declined when the studio offered to give the film only a [[limited theatrical release|limited release]].{{sfn|Nowell|2010|p=173}} Instead, Paramount released another independent slasher film, ''[[Friday the 13th (1980 film)|Friday the 13th]]'', which premiered two months before ''Prom Night''.{{sfn|Nowell|2010|p=173}} [[Embassy Pictures|Avco Embassy Pictures]] ultimately purchased U.S. distribution rights to ''Prom Night'' at the [[Marché du Film|Cannes Film Market]] in May 1980, and created 250 prints of the film for theatrical exhibition.{{sfn|Rockoff|2011|p=86}}  

Avco Embassy Pictures heavily marketed ''Prom Night'' to female audiences by emphasizing its female characters and prom setting, highlighting "dance, romance, and female beauty rituals... while taking steps to avoid the alienation of the male youths that were considered to dominate horror film attendance.{{sfn|Nowell|2010|pages=179–180}} Film scholar Richard Nowell describes the film's [[Trailer (promotion)|theatrical trailer]] as an "exact replica" of that for ''[[Carrie (1976 film)|Carrie]]'' (1976).{{sfn|Nowell|2010|page=173}} The trailer opens with footage of prom-goers dancing to disco music, followed by a title logo reminiscent of that for the disco film ''[[Saturday Night Fever]]'' (1977).{{sfn|Nowell|2010|page=180}} The trailer's closing title features an alternate red logo with blood dripping from each letter.{{sfn|Nowell|2010|page=180}} Nowell notes that the opening logo deliberately targets a female audience, while the closing logo "signal[s] the presence of horror-related content" typically marketed toward male youth.{{sfn|Nowell|2010|pages=173, 180}}

Television spots for the film similarly emphasized its female characters, opening with a montage of young women preparing for the prom with a male narrator stating: "These are the girls of Hamilton High, and tonight they will be more beautiful than ever before in their lives."{{sfn|Nowell|2010|page=181}} The second half of the television spots contrasts the glamorous imagery with nine shots which "established the film to be an intriguing whodunit by showing a mysterious ax-wielding figure lurking in the shadows," reinforcing "the coexistence of romance with horror, thrills, and intrigue."{{sfn|Nowell|2010|pages=181–182}} 

The film's advertising campaign was maligned by American critics [[Gene Siskel]] and [[Roger Ebert]] in a September 18, 1980 episode of ''[[Sneak Previews]]'', cited among a glut of other violent slasher films released the same year that thematized "Women in Danger."<ref name=Brutal>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/chicago-tribune/190071321/|via=Newspapers.com|work=[[Chicago Tribune]]|date=September 21, 1980|series=Arts & Books|p=6|title=Brutal attacks on women: Films take a turn for the worse|author-link=Gene Siskel|first=Gene|last=Siskel}}</ref> Siskel and Ebert used the episode to criticize the advertising campaigns for several films, including ''[[Don't Answer the Phone!]]'', ''[[Hell Night]]'' and ''Prom Night'': "[These] ads have been saturating television for the past two years," said Ebert, "and the summer and fall of 1980 are the worst yet."{{sfn|Martin|2016|pages=17–18}}

After its first week of release in the United States, Avco Embassy Pictures announced a promotional tour for the film, which would have star Curtis appearing at premieres in various cities, such as New York City, [[Washington, D.C.|D.C.]], [[Baltimore]], [[Cincinnati]], [[Denver]], and [[Los Angeles]].<ref name=afi/> Additionally, 10,000 promotional "scream pillows" were to be distributed at screenings to help quiet the outbursts of theater patrons.<ref name=afi/> Curtis appeared on a July 18, 1980 episode of the talk show ''Fantasy Film Festival'' to promote the film, where she was interviewed by [[Mick Garris]].<ref>{{cite web|work=Fantasy Film Festival|date=July 18, 1980|title=Jamie Lee Curtis|url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6KPyGZufAZ0|via=[[YouTube]]|publisher=Mick Garris Interviews|url-status=live|archive-url=https://ghostarchive.org/varchive/6KPyGZufAZ0|archive-date=March 5, 2026}}</ref>

===Television broadcast===
In late 1980, Canada's [[CTV Television Network|CTV Network]] paid a record $200,000 for three TV airings of ''Prom Night''.<ref name=afi/> The film was also acquired for TV broadcast in the United States on [[NBC]], which purchased two [[prime time]] showings of the film for $3.25 million.<ref name=afi/> In order to slightly lengthen the film after nudity was excised for TV airings, an alternate cut of ''Prom Night'' was crafted by editor Michael MacLaverty.<ref name=tvcut>{{cite AV media|title=Additional Footage Added for Television Broadcast|work=Prom Night|medium=[[Blu-ray]] featurette|publisher=[[Synapse Films]]|year=2014|oclc=894921318}}</ref> The TV version of the film features some additional scenes that were excised from the original theatrical cut, as well as extended sequences that had been truncated.<ref name=tvcut/> Among these are a scene in which Mr. and Mrs. Hammond meet with Dr. Fairchild (David Gardner), a psychologist evaluating Mrs. Hammond; three brief scenes featuring Adele (Liz Stalker-Mason), a temporary school secretary who is encountered by Mr. Hammond and Kim, respectively; a sequence in which Jude, Vicki (Pita Oliver) and Kim pass notes during biology class; and Kim returning home and speaking briefly with her mother.<ref name=tvcut/>

The TV cut of ''Prom Night'' premiered in Canada on CTV Network at 9:00 p.m. on February 21, 1981,<ref name=Foul/> with a U.S. premiere occurring the following night on NBC.<ref name=afi/><ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-times/190074497/|work=[[The Times (Shreveport, Louisiana)|The Times]]|date=February 22, 1981|last=Prime|first=John Andrew|title=Networks present week of movies|p=1-G|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> The film's Canadian telecast premiere was marked by a timing error in which the film started at the midway point.<ref name=Foul>{{cite news|last=Adilman|first=Sid|date=February 23, 1981|title=Foul Play suspected on TV's Prom Night|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-toronto-star/192560279/|via=Newspapers.com|work=[[Toronto Star]]|page=D1}}</ref> After 20 minutes had elapsed, the telecast was abruptly halted and momentarily replaced with an episode of the series ''[[Foul Play (TV series)|Foul Play]]''.<ref name=Foul/> At 10:00 p.m., one hour after the premiere had been scheduled to start, the film was aired from the beginning, resulting in the network's late-night news beginning an hour later than usual.<ref name=Foul/>

==Reception==
===Box office===
With a [[limited theatrical release|limited release]] in the United States on July 18, 1980, ''Prom Night'' was a financial success as a [[sleeper hit]].{{sfn|Nowell|2010|p=184}} During its opening week in select cities (including [[Chicago]], [[Milwaukee]], [[Louisville, Kentucky|Louisville]], and several cities in Texas), it grossed $1,189,636 at the U.S. box office, and was subsequently slated for a staggered platform release.<ref name=afi/>

During its opening week runs in New York and Los Angeles, the film earned a combined $1.3 million, and marked Avco Embassy's highest-grossing Los Angeles opening to date, as well as breaking weekend records in [[Philadelphia]] and [[New England]].<ref name=afi/> The film continued to prove financially successful through [[Labor Day]], earning $2.1 million over the holiday weekend.<ref name=afi/>

By October 1980, the film had grossed $12 million.<ref name=afi/> It was reported later that month that the film's North American box office revenue had exceeded $14 million, $636,000 of which was grossed in Canada.<ref>{{cite news|last=Adilman|first=Sid|date=October 18, 1980|title=Bonanzas at the box office|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/edmonton-journal/192631113/|work=[[Edmonton Journal]]|page=I-5|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> By the end of its theatrical run, ''Prom Night'' had grossed a total of $14.8 million at the U.S. box office with approximately 5,500,460 admissions.<ref name="bo">{{cite web|url=https://boxofficemojo.com/movies/?id=promnight.htm|work=[[Box Office Mojo]]|title=Prom Night (1980)|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221124045106/https://www.boxofficemojo.com/release/rl912950785/|archive-date=November 24, 2022|url-status=live}}</ref> Canadian journalist Ron Base described the film as "the ''[[Meatballs (film)|Meatballs]]'' of 1980", referring to that film's similar commercial success in the United States.<ref>{{cite news|work=[[Times & Transcript|The Moncton Times and Transcript]]|date=December 20, 1980|last=Base|first=Ron|page=16|title=Canadians still prefer Hollywood|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-times-transcript/192631250/}}</ref>

Numerous critics compared ''Prom Night'' to Jamie Lee Curtis's previous film, ''Halloween'' (1978), as well as [[Brian De Palma]]'s ''[[Carrie (1976 film)|Carrie]]'' (1976), a horror film also revolving around a high school prom. {{efn-lr|Attributed to multiple critical reviews.<ref name=canby/><ref name=Siskel/><ref name=Lamb/><ref name=Mathews/><ref name=Variety/><ref name=Mahar/><ref name=NewsPress/>}} [[Gene Siskel]] of the ''[[Chicago Tribune]]'' described it as a "watered-down cross" of both films, though he noted it was "not as violent as one might expect, based on those frightening ads of a masked man holding a [[phallus|phallic]] knife... You would think that ''Prom Night'' was another one of those hideous attacks-on-promiscuous-women pictures. It's not. Gender makes no difference in this routine revenge film."<ref name=Siskel>{{cite news|work=[[Chicago Tribune]]|date=July 21, 1980|title=Prom Night|first=Gene|last=Siskel|page=6|series=Tempo|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/chicago-tribune-gene-siskel-movie-review/128760545/|via=Newspapers.com|author-link=Gene Siskel}}</ref> Jamie Lamb of the ''[[Vancouver Sun]]'' similarly compared it to both ''Halloween'' and ''Carrie'', adding that it "isn't entertaining, nor is it particularly violent. It seems a hodge-podge of characters and tricks from other movies intended for a young audience, which is now heading back to school."<ref name=Lamb>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-vancouver-sun/190157689/|work=[[Vancouver Sun]]|last=Lamb|first=Jamie|date=September 3, 1980|title=Prom Night a bore despite terror tactics|p=D1|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> Jack Mathews of the ''[[Detroit Free Press]]'' also compared it to the two aforementioned films before noting it as being "not as effective as either,"<ref name=Mathews>{{cite news|last=Mathews|first=Jack|work=[[Detroit Free Press]]|title=You won't want a date for 'Prom Night'|date=August 22, 1980|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22377933/detroit_free_press/|page=6B}}</ref> while ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' stated that it "[borrows] shamelessly from ''Carrie'' and any number of gruesome [[exploitation film|exploitationers]]," but conceded that it "manages to score a few horrific points amid a number of sagging moments."<ref name=Variety>{{cite web|url=https://variety.com/1979/film/reviews/prom-night-1200424678/|date=December 31, 1979|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231003154246/https://variety.com/1979/film/reviews/prom-night-1200424678/|archive-date=October 3, 2023|url-status=live|work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|author=''Variety'' Staff|title=Prom Night}}</ref> ''[[The Oregonian]]''{{'}}s Ted Mahar wrote that the film "is about 25 minutes worth of story assembled from specific films and tortured out to feature length."<ref name=Mahar>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-oregonian/192558514/|work=[[The Oregonian]]|last=Mahar|first=Ted|date=August 30, 1980|p=D2|title=Dopey comedy, rip-off terror film offer nothing really new to viewer|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref>

[[Vincent Canby]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' gave the film a middling review, writing that it "is a comparatively genteel hybrid, part shock melodrama, like ''Halloween,'' and part mystery, though it's less a whodunit than a who's-doing-it."<ref name=canby/> Canby praised the film's restrained violence, writing that director Lynch "chooses to underplay the bloody spectacle. This isn't to say that there aren't some sticky moments, including one not especially convincing beheading, but that more often than not the camera cuts away, or the screen goes discreetly gray, before the audience is drenched in gore. This may or may not be the reason that the audience with which I saw the film yesterday booed at the end."<ref name=canby>{{cite web|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1980/08/16/arts/prom-night-chiller-from-canada-masks-gore.html|work=[[The New York Times]]|title='Prom Night,' Chiller from Canada, Masks Gore|date=August 16, 1980|first=Vincent|last=Canby|author-link=Vincent Canby|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191212061629/https://www.nytimes.com/1980/08/16/arts/prom-night-chiller-from-canada-masks-gore.html|archive-date=December 12, 2019}}</ref> [[Roger Ebert]], who viewed the film on a double bill with ''[[I Spit On Your Grave]]'', recounted: "''Prom Night'' is merely an execrable movie...&nbsp;If you have an appetite for violence and the macabre, at least try to satisfy it in a movie done with artistry and craftsmanship—Brian De Palma's ''[[Dressed to Kill (1980 film)|Dressed to Kill]]'', for example. ''Prom Night'' should be cut up to make [[bookmark]]s."{{sfn|Ebert|2000|pages=286–287}}

[[Kevin Thomas (film critic)|Kevin Thomas]] of the ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' praised the film as "an efficient rather than stylish Canadian-made horror picture that mercifully lets you complete its grislier moments in your imagination. Even so, its various jolts should be sufficient to satisfy scare-show fans."<ref>{{cite news|work=[[Los Angeles Times]]|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22378220/the_los_angeles_times/|page=4|title=The Gory Horror of 'Prom Night'|date=August 18, 1980|via=Newspapers.com|author=Thomas, Kevin|author-link=Kevin Thomas (film critic)}}</ref> Donna Chernin, writing for ''[[The Plain Dealer]]'', also awarded the film a favorable review, noting that while it "hinges on a few preposterous plot points, it does feature a surprise ending that caught me off guard. The movie is often genuinely scary."<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-plain-dealer/190158526/|work=[[The Plain Dealer]]|last=Chernin|first=Donna|date=July 24, 1980|title='Prom Night': Dance macabre|p=8-D|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> ''[[The Atlanta Journal-Constitution|The Atlanta Constitution]]'' also reviewed the film positively, calling it a "surprisingly good scare film. At least the murderer actually has a motive, for a change. If nothing else, it proves there's still a line between a respectable horror film and gross exploitation."<ref>{{cite news|page=26|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22377755/the_atlanta_constitution/|date=September 27, 1980|series=Film|title=Prom Night|work=[[The Atlanta Constitution]]|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> ''[[The News-Press]]'' wrote: "If ''Carrie'' and ''Friday the 13th'' weren't enough for you, this will satisfy your appetite for youth, gore and chills."<ref name=NewsPress>{{cite news|work=[[The News-Press]]|location=Fort Myers, Florida|date=September 26, 1980|series=At the Movies|title=Prom Night|via=Newspapers.com|page=3D|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22377707/newspress/}}</ref>  

Les Wedman, writing for ''[[The Province]]'', was critical of the film's lack of distinguishing Canadian characteristics, despite it being filmed in Canada with a largely Canadian cast and crew.<ref name=Wedman>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-province-prom-canada-article/76678545/|work=[[The Province]]|date=September 5, 1980|last=Wedman|first=Les|title=You wouldn't know it was a Canadian movie|via=Newspapers.com|p=A10}}</ref> He did, however, concede that director Lynch "sets up some scary-enough sequences, complete with great buckets of blood."<ref name=Wedman/> The ''[[The Gazette (Montreal)|Montreal Gazette]]''{{'}}s Bruce Bailey praised Curtis's performance but criticized the film's lengthy exposition, noting that "it takes rather long to get down to the business of delivering a few shocks."<ref>{{cite news|work=[[The Gazette (Montreal)|Montreal Gazette]]|title='Prom' short on needed shocks|author=Bailey, Bruce|date=November 24, 1980|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22378153/the_gazette/|page=47|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref>

==== Modern appraisal ====
{{Rotten Tomatoes prose|45|4.6|22|consensus=Horror aficionados might have a ball with ''Prom Night'', but a lack of mystery and inability to capitalize on the dance hall setting makes for a generic night of mayhem.|access-date=January 30, 2026|ref=y}} ''[[AllMovie]]''{{'}}s review by Donald Guarisco was generally negative, though he wrote that it "utilizes a surprising amount of skill both behind and in front of the camera as it goes through its paces".<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://allmovie.com/movie/prom-night-v39485/review |title=Prom Night - Review  |last=Guarisco |first=Donald |work=[[AllMovie]] |url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140224220733/https://allmovie.com/movie/prom-night-v39485/review|archive-date=February 24, 2014}}</ref>

In a 1998 ''[[Video Watchdog]]'' review of the film for its first DVD release, [[Tim Lucas]] gave the film an unfavorable review, writing: "It is hard to imagine anyone taking much of a shine to this Canadian slasher opus, aside from those who saw the film during its original run and have inexplicably fond memories of it," deeming it a "dull, by-the-numbers hybrid of ''Halloween'' and ''Carrie''."<ref>{{cite magazine|work=[[Video Watchdog]]|last=Lucas|first=Tim|issue=43|p=62|title=Discs: Prom Night|year=1998|issn=1070-9991|author-link=Tim Lucas}}</ref> In a review published by ''[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out]]'', the film was called "a sincere ''Halloween'' rip-off which takes time out to milk ''Carrie'', ''Saturday Night Fever'', and all those B-feature 'lust and rivalry' high school sagas", but praised Jamie Lee Curtis's performance, writing: "Curtis is superb as Miss Naturally Popular and Prom Queen-to-be, isolated in empty high school corridors."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://timeout.com/london/film/prom-night|work=[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out]]|access-date=December 29, 2016|title=Prom Night, directed by Paul Lynch|date=September 10, 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141218193944/https://timeout.com/london/film/prom-night|archive-date=December 18, 2014}}</ref> ''[[TV Guide]]'' gave the film one out of four stars, writing: "Curtis disco-dancing and wonderful moments such as when the severed head of a victim rolls across the dance floor. ''Prom Night'' is better than most slasher movies, mainly because it's funnier."<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.tvguide.com/movies/prom-night/review/115066|work=[[TV Guide]]|title=Prom Night|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150602033044/https://www.tvguide.com/movies/prom-night/115066/|archive-date=June 2, 2015|url-status=dead}}</ref>

Scholar Richard Nowell considers ''Prom Night'' one of the most influential slasher films of the 1980s, noting that it "rearranged ''Halloween''{{'}}s story-structure into a [[whodunit]]."{{sfn|Nowell|2010|pages=10, 161}} Filmmaker [[Joe Dante]] said that after ''Prom Night'' was a big hit, it made the slasher film a viable genre.<ref>{{cite AV media |last = Dante |first= Joe |date = 2020|title = [[In Search of Darkness: Part II]]|time = 15 minutes 41 seconds|author-link=Joe Dante|type = film |quote = Once a picture like Prom Night came out and made a lot of money, the genre of the slasher film [...] became a viable genre unto itself.}}</ref> [[John Kenneth Muir]], in his book ''Horror Films of the 1980s'' (2010), describes the film as a "better-than-average slasher" and praises Curtis's performance.{{sfn|Muir|2010|pages=125–126}} He also classifies its twist ending as "a shocker second only to [that of] ''[[Sleepaway Camp]]''," adding that its final moments generate both "pathos and remorse."{{sfn|Muir|2010|p=126}} In a 2024 retrospective for ''[[Collider (website)|Collider]]'', Shawn Van Horn describes the film's conclusion as distinctly melancholic among that of other slasher films of the period:

{{quote|''Prom Night''{{'}}s ending is absolutely heartbreaking. Slashers like to have that sense of resolution, where the [[final girl]] overcomes the big bad. She is now empowered and evil is defeated. Not here though. Yes, the killer is dead, but we find no joy or relief in his demise. Alex is a boy who misses his little sister so much that it mentally breaks him. He saw killing those who harmed her as the only way out of that trauma, but it didn't work. His death is harder to take than any of his victims. As for Kim, she hasn't overcome anything. Her nightmare is just beginning.<ref>{{cite web|work=[[Collider (website)|Collider]]|last=Van Horn|first=Shawn|title=This Underrated Jamie Lee Curtis Slasher Has an Ending Unlike Any Other|date=January 7, 2024|url=https://collider.com/prom-night-1980-ending-explained/|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250720015438/https://collider.com/prom-night-1980-ending-explained/|archive-date=July 20, 2025}}</ref>}}

==Sequels==
{{Main|Prom Night (film series)}}
''Prom Night'' was followed by [[Prom Night (film series)|three sequels-in-name-only]], each of which has little in common with the 1980 film, aside from all being set at a location named Hamilton High School. In 2008, a [[Prom Night (2008 film)|remake]] was released, which also bears little resemblance to the 1980 film.
