'''''Willow''''' is a 1988 American [[high fantasy]] [[adventure film]] directed by [[Ron Howard]] and produced by Nigel Wooll. The film was executive produced by [[George Lucas]] and written by [[Bob Dolman]] from a story by Lucas. The film stars [[Warwick Davis]] as the title character, [[Val Kilmer]], [[Joanne Whalley]], [[Billy Barty]] and [[Jean Marsh]]. Davis portrays aspiring magician Willow Ufgood who teams up with disaffected warrior Madmartigan (Kilmer) to protect young baby princess [[Elora Danan]] from evil queen Bavmorda (Marsh).

Lucas conceived the idea for the film in 1972, approaching Howard to direct during the [[post-production]] phase of ''[[Cocoon (film)|Cocoon]]'' in 1985. Bob Dolman was brought in to write the screenplay, coming up with seven drafts that Lucas was actively involved in developing.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Gajewski |first=Ryan |date=2022-11-30 |title='Willow' Film Writer Says TV Reboot's Process Had "More of a Corporate Feeling" Than Movie |url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/hollywood-flashback-george-lucas-willow-disney-plus-1235271476/ |access-date=2023-10-17 |website=The Hollywood Reporter |language=en-US |archive-date=December 4, 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221204183301/https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/hollywood-flashback-george-lucas-willow-disney-plus-1235271476/ |url-status=live }}</ref> It was finished in late 1986. It was then set up at [[Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer]] and [[principal photography]] began in April 1987, finishing the following October. The majority of filming took place in [[Dinorwic quarry]] in Wales with some at [[Elstree Studios (Shenley Road)|Elstree Studios]] in [[Hertfordshire]], as well as a small section in New Zealand. [[Industrial Light & Magic]] created the visual effects and animation sequences, which led to a revolutionary breakthrough with digital [[morphing]] technology.

The film was released by [[Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer|MGM/UA Distribution Co.]] on May 20, 1988 to mixed reviews from critics with praise for the visual effects, humour, character designs and Warwick Davis's performances but criticism for its direction, pacing, tone and story. It grossed $137.6 million worldwide against a $35 million budget. While not the blockbuster some expected, it turned a profit based on international box office returns and strong home video and television returns. It received two [[Academy Awards|Academy Award]] nominations. A [[Willow (TV series)|television series]] that served as a [[sequel]] to the film was released on [[Disney+]] in 2022.

==Plot==
<!--- Please keep between 400-700 words-->
The evil [[Magician (fantasy)|sorceress]] Queen Bavmorda of Nockmaar hears of a prophecy that a child with a special [[Runes|rune]] birthmark will bring about her downfall. She imprisons all pregnant women in her realm. The child is born, but her mother persuades the [[midwife]] Ethna to smuggle the baby out of the castle. Bavmorda sends her Nockmaar Hounds after the midwife. With the hounds closing in on her, Ethna sets the baby adrift on a tangle of dried grass in a river before succumbing to the hounds. Meanwhile, Bavmorda sends her daughter Sorsha and an army led by General Kael to hunt down the baby.

Downriver, a village of Nelwyn (a race of [[Dwarf (folklore)|dwarves]]) prepares for a festival. The baby is found by the children of farmer and aspiring sorcerer Willow Ufgood, and his family takes her in. At the festival, a Nockmaar hound arrives and attacks cradles. After Nelwyn warriors kill it, Willow presents the baby to the village leader High Aldwin as a possible reason for the hound's appearance. High Aldwin orders the baby must return to a Daikini (the Nelwyn's name for "[[Human|tall people]]") family, so Willow and other volunteers set out with the baby to find one.

At a [[intersection (road)|crossroads]], they find Madmartigan, a [[mercenary]] trapped in a [[Gibbeting|crow's cage]], who offers to take the baby in exchange for his freedom. Most of Willow's party think they should give him the baby, but Willow and his friend Meegosh refuse, causing the others to leave for home. After meeting Madmartigan's old comrade Airk leading an army to attack Nockmaar forces, Willow agrees to Madmartigan's terms.

On the way home, Willow and Meegosh discover that some [[brownie (folklore)|brownies]] have stolen the baby, and pursue them. They are captured by the brownies, but Fairy Queen Cherlindrea frees them and explains the baby is [[Elora Danan]], the foretold Princess of Tir Asleen. She gives Willow a magic wand and sends him to find the enchantress Fin Raziel.

Willow sends Meegosh home and continues the journey in the company of two brownies, Franjean and Rool. At a tavern, he re-encounters Madmartigan, who is disguising himself as a woman to hide from his lover's husband Llug. Sorsha and Kael's soldiers arrive, but Madmartigan is revealed as a man to Llug, who starts a brawl which helps Willow and Madmartigan escape with Elora.

Madmartigan allows Willow to follow him to the lake where Raziel lives. They are captured soon thereafter, along with Raziel, who had been turned into a [[Common brushtail possum|possum]] by Bavmorda. Willow tries to restore her, but turns her into a [[rook (bird)|rook]] instead. Franjean accidentally doses Madmartigan with love dust, causing him to declare his undying love to a skeptical Sorsha. Fleeing, Willow's party finds Airk and the remnants of his army. When the Nockmaar army pursues, Madmartigan takes Sorsha hostage, and they flee once more, but Sorsha manages to escape.

Willow's party arrives at Tir Asleen, only to find it cursed and overrun with [[troll]]s. Kael's army arrives, and Madmartigan and Willow attempt to fend them off. Willow turns Raziel into a goat and accidentally turns a troll into an Eborsisk, a giant two-headed monster, and in the ensuing chaos, Kael captures Elora. Sorsha, realizing she loves Madmartigan, defects to his side. Airk's army arrives, but Kael escapes with Elora and makes his way to Nockmaar Castle, where Bavmorda prepares a ritual to banish Elora into oblivion.

Willow's party and Airk's army travel to Nockmaar Castle, but upon their arrival, Bavmorda casts a spell turning them all into pigs. Willow, having used the wand to protect himself, finally restores Raziel to her human form. She reverses Bavmorda's spell, and Willow's army manages to trick their way into the castle. Kael slays Airk, and Madmartigan avenges him, while Willow, Sorsha, and Raziel confront Bavmorda in the ritual chamber. Bavmorda incapacitates Raziel and Sorsha. Willow uses [[sleight of hand|sleight-of-hand]] to trick Bavmorda into thinking he made Elora vanish. Bavmorda, unnerved, moves to attack him, but accidentally completes the ritual while standing next to the altar, banishing herself.

During celebrations at the restored Tir Asleen, Willow is gifted a spellbook by Raziel. Leaving Elora in the care of Madmartigan and Sorsha, Willow returns home to his village and family in triumph.

==Production==
===Development===
[[George Lucas]] conceived the idea for the film (originally titled ''[[Munchkin]]s'') in 1972. Similarly to his intent in ''[[Star Wars (film)|Star Wars]]'', he created "a number of well-known mythological situations for a young audience".<ref name=little>{{cite book |last=Hearn |first=Marcus |date=2005 |title=The Cinema of George Lucas |publisher=[[Harry N. Abrams, Inc.]] |location=New York City |isbn=0-8109-4968-7 |page=153}}</ref><ref name=funky/> During the production of ''[[Return of the Jedi]]'' in 1982, Lucas approached [[Warwick Davis]], who was portraying [[Wicket W. Warrick|Wicket]] the [[Ewok]], about playing Willow Ufgood. Five years passed before he was actually cast in the role. Lucas "thought it would be great to use a little person in a lead role. A lot of my movies are about a little guy against the system, and this was just a more literal interpretation of that idea."<ref name=little/>

Lucas explained that he had to wait until the mid-1980s to make the film because visual effects technology was finally advanced enough to execute his vision.<ref name=funky>{{cite news |last=Harmetz |first=Aljean |date=May 21, 1987 |title='Star Wars' Is 10, And Lucas Reflects |work=[[The New York Times]] |url=https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/library/film/052187wars-ten.html |access-date=October 19, 2015}}</ref> Meanwhile, actor-turned-director [[Ron Howard]] was looking to do a [[fantasy film]]. He was at [[Industrial Light & Magic]] during the [[post-production]] phase of ''[[Cocoon (film)|Cocoon]]'', when he was first approached by Lucas to direct ''Willow''. He had previously starred in Lucas's ''[[American Graffiti]]'',<ref>{{cite book |last=Howard |first=Ron |author-link=Ron Howard |date=2005 |title=The Cinema of George Lucas |chapter=Forward |publisher=[[Harry N. Abrams, Inc.]] |location=New York City |isbn=0-8109-4968-7}}</ref> and Lucas felt that he and Howard shared a symbiotic relationship similar to the one he enjoyed with [[Steven Spielberg]]. Howard nominated [[Bob Dolman]] to write the screenplay based on Lucas's story. Dolman had worked with him on a 1983 [[television pilot]] called ''Little Shots'' that had not resulted in a series, and Lucas admired Dolman's work on the [[Situation comedy|sitcom]] ''[[WKRP in Cincinnati]]''.<ref name=Howard/>

Dolman joined Howard and Lucas at [[Skywalker Ranch]] for a series of lengthy story conferences, and wrote seven drafts of his script between the spring and fall of 1986.<ref name=Howard>Hearn, p.154-155</ref> [[Pre-production]] began in late 1986. Various major film studios turned down the chance to distribute and cofinance it with [[Lucasfilm]] because they believed the fantasy genre was unsuccessful. This was largely due to films such as ''[[Krull (film)|Krull]]'', ''[[Legend (1985 film)|Legend]]'', ''[[Dragonslayer (1981 film)|Dragonslayer]]'' and ''[[Labyrinth (1986 film)|Labyrinth]]''.<ref>{{cite news |last=Harmetz |first=Aljean |date=June 9, 1988 |title=A Pained Lucas Ponders Attacks on 'Willow' |work=[[The New York Times]] |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1988/06/09/movies/a-pained-lucas-ponders-attacks-on-willow.html?pagewanted=all |access-date=October 19, 2015 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160307232652/http://www.nytimes.com/1988/06/09/movies/a-pained-lucas-ponders-attacks-on-willow.html?pagewanted=all |archive-date=March 7, 2016 |url-status=live |df=mdy-all }}</ref> Lucas took it to [[Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer]] (MGM), which was headed by [[Alan Ladd Jr.]]<ref>{{Cite news |last=Gajewski |first=Ryan |date=December 4, 2022 |title='Willow' Film Writer Says TV Reboot's Process Had "More of a Corporate Feeling" Than Movie |work=The Hollywood Reporter |url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/hollywood-flashback-george-lucas-willow-disney-plus-1235271476/ |access-date=November 30, 2022 |archive-date=November 30, 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221130193424/https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/hollywood-flashback-george-lucas-willow-disney-plus-1235271476/ |url-status=live }}</ref> Ladd and Lucas shared a relationship as far back as the mid-1970s, when Ladd, running [[20th Century Studios|20th Century Fox]], [[greenlit]] Lucas's idea for ''Star Wars''.<ref name=digital/> However, in 1986, MGM was facing financial troubles, and major investment in a fantasy film was perceived as a risk. Ladd advanced half of the $35 million budget in return for theatrical and television rights, leaving Lucasfilm with home video and [[pay television]] rights to offer in exchange for the other half.<ref name=digital/> [[Sony Pictures Home Entertainment|RCA/Columbia Pictures Home Video]] paid $15 million to Lucas in exchange for the video rights.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=KuuuBgAAQBAJ&q=willow+rca%2Fcolumbia&pg=PA141|title=Hollywood in the Information Age: Beyond the Silver Screen|last=Wasko|first=Janet|date=2013-06-26|publisher=John Wiley & Sons|isbn=9780745678337|language=en|page=141}}</ref>

Lucas named the character of General Kael ([[Pat Roach]]) after film critic [[Pauline Kael]],<ref name="The New York Times">{{cite news |last=Van Gelder |first=Lawrence |date=September 4, 2001 |title=Pauline Kael, Provocative and Widely Imitated New Yorker Film Critic, Dies at 82 |newspaper=[[The New York Times]] |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2001/09/04/movies/pauline-kael-provocative-and-widely-imitated-new-yorker-film-critic-dies-at-82.html |access-date=July 13, 2008}}</ref> a fact that was not lost on Kael in her printed review of the film. She referred to General Kael as an "homage a moi". Similarly, the two-headed dragon was called an "Eborsisk" after film critics [[Gene Siskel]] and [[Roger Ebert]].<ref name="opie"/>

===Filming===
[[Principal photography]] began on April 2, 1987, and ended the following October. Interior footage took place at [[Elstree Studios (Shenley Road)|Elstree Studios]] in [[Hertfordshire]], England, while [[location shooting]] took place in [[Dinorwic quarry]], Wales and New Zealand.<ref name=digital/> Lucas initially visualized shooting the film similar to ''Return of the Jedi'', with studio scenes at Elstree and locations in [[Northern California]], but the idea eventually faded. However, some exteriors were done around Skywalker Ranch and on location at [[Burney Falls]], near [[Mount Shasta]].<ref name=castle/> The Chinese government refused Lucas the chance for a brief location shoot. He then sent a group of photographers to South China to photograph specific scenery, which was then used for background [[chroma key|blue screen]] footage. [[Tongariro National Park]] in New Zealand was chosen to house Bavmorda's castle.<ref name=castle>{{cite book |last=Baxter |first=John |date=October 1999 |title=Mythmaker: The Life and Work of George Lucas |publisher=[[Avon (publishers)|Avon]] |location=New York City |isbn=0-380-97833-4 |pages=[https://archive.org/details/mythmakerlifewor00baxt/page/365 365–366] |url=https://archive.org/details/mythmakerlifewor00baxt/page/365 }}</ref>

Some of the waterfalls scenes for the movie were shot at Burney Falls in Northern California, although [[Powerscourt Waterfall]] in Ireland was also used for other scenes.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://screenrant.com/willow-movie-where-filmed-locations/ |title=Where Was Willow Filmed: All Locations |website=[[Screen Rant]] |date=October 20, 2020 |access-date=March 9, 2021 |archive-date=March 16, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210316101806/https://screenrant.com/willow-movie-where-filmed-locations/ |url-status=live |last=Armitage|first=Helen}}</ref>

===Visual effects and design===
[[File:Willow SFX.png|alt=A little man in a hooded cloak with his back to the camera holds a lightening wand toward a two-legged animal that appears to be part goat and part ostrich.|thumb|250px|right|Willow tries to restore Fin Raziel to human form.]]
Lucasfilm's Industrial Light & Magic (ILM) created the visual effects and animation sequences. The script called for Willow to restore Fin Raziel ([[Patricia Hayes]]) from a goat to her human form. Willow recites what he thinks is the appropriate spell, but turns the goat into an ostrich, a peacock, a tortoise and, finally, a tiger, before returning her to normal. ILM VFX supervisor [[Dennis Muren]] considered using CGI [[stop-motion]] animation for the scene.<ref>Baxter, p.367</ref> He also explained that another traditional and practical way in the late 1980s to execute this sequence would have been through the use of an [[optical printer|optical dissolve]] with [[cutaway (filmmaking)|cutaway]]s at various stages.<ref name=digital/>

Muren found both stop-motion and optical effects to be too technically challenging and decided that the transformation scene would be a perfect opportunity for ILM to create advances with digital [[morphing]] technology. He proposed filming each animal, and the actress [[Double (occupation)|doubling]] for Hayes, and then feeding the images into a computer program developed by Doug Smythe.<ref name=digital/> The program would then create a smooth transition from one stage to another before outputting the result back onto film. Smythe began development of the necessary software in September 1987. By March 1988, Muren and fellow designer David Allen achieved what would represent a breakthrough for [[computer-generated imagery]] (CGI).<ref name=digital>Hearn, p.156-157</ref> The techniques developed for the sequence were later utilized by ILM for ''[[Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade]]'', ''[[Terminator 2: Judgment Day]]'' and ''[[Star Trek VI: The Undiscovered Country]]''.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.vfxvoice.com/over-30-years-willow-has-morphed-into-an-effects-classic/|title=Over 30 Years, WILLOW has Morphed into an Effects Classic|last=Failes|first=Ian|date=April 3, 2018|website=VFX Voice|access-date=April 4, 2018|archive-date=April 4, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180404073635/http://vfxvoice.com/over-30-years-willow-has-morphed-into-an-effects-classic/|url-status=live}}</ref>

The head of ILM's animation department, [[Wes Takahashi]], supervised the film's animation sequences.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.agni-animation.com/fullerton/halloffame/Wes_Ford_Takahashi.html|title=Subject: Wes Ford Takahashi|publisher=Animators' Hall of Fame|access-date=June 14, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160812020742/http://www.agni-animation.com/fullerton/halloffame/Wes_Ford_Takahashi.html|archive-date=August 12, 2016|url-status=usurped|df=mdy-all}}</ref>

The [[film score]] was composed and conducted by [[James Horner]] and performed by the [[London Symphony Orchestra]].<ref name="allmusic">{{cite web |last=Hobart |first=Tavia |title=Willow [Original Score] |work=[[AllMusic]] |url={{AllMusic|class=album|id=r84663|pure_url=yes}} |access-date=January 20, 2009}}</ref> According to Horner, "I am a musicologist, a doctor of music. Therefore I listened to, studied and analysed a lot of music. I also enjoy metaphors, the art of quoting and of cycles. The harmonic draft of the ''Willow'' score, and most particularly its spiritual side, came from such a cycle, from such mythology and music history that I was taught, and that I myself convey with my own emotions and compositions."<ref name=JHFM>{{cite web |last=Martin |first=Jean-Baptiste |date=June 3, 2013 |title=Willow: Between Quotes |website=jameshorner-filmmusic.com |url=https://jameshorner-filmmusic.com/willow-between-quotes/ |access-date=January 4, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140105004153/http://jameshorner-filmmusic.com/willow-between-quotes/ |archive-date=January 5, 2014 |url-status=live |df=mdy-all }}</ref>

Eclectic influences on the score include [[Leoš Janáček]]'s ''[[Glagolitic Mass]]'', [[Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart]]'s "[[Requiem (Mozart)|Requiem]]", "[[Cantata Profana|The Nine Splendid Stags]]" from [[Béla Bartók]], [[Edvard Grieg]]'s "Arabian Dance" for the theater play ''[[Peer Gynt]]'', and compositions by [[Sergei Prokofiev]].<ref name=JHFM/>

"Willow's Theme" purposefully (see Horner's quote above) contains a reworking/alteration of part of the theme of the first movement ("Lebhaft") of [[Robert Schumann]]'s [[Symphony No. 3 (Schumann)|Symphony No. 3]] referencing it, while "[[Elora Danan]]'s Theme" shows a reference to the Bulgarian [[Folk music|folk]] song "Mir Stanke Le" (Мир Станке ле), also known as the "Harvest Song from Thrace".

==Release==
===Box office===
The film was shown and promoted at the [[1988 Cannes Film Festival]].<ref name="festival-cannes.com">{{cite web |title=Festival de Cannes: Willow |work=festival-cannes.com |url=https://www.festival-cannes.com/en/archives/ficheFilm/id/296/year/1988.html |access-date=July 31, 2009 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120807020756/http://www.festival-cannes.com/en/archives/ficheFilm/id/296/year/1988.html |archive-date=August 7, 2012 |url-status=dead |df=mdy-all }}</ref><ref name=alien/> It was released on May 20, 1988, in 1,209 theaters, earning $8,300,169 in its opening weekend, [[List of 1988 box office number-one films in the United States|placing number one at the weekend box office]]. Lucas had hoped it would earn as much money as ''[[E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial]]'',<ref name=alien>Baxter, p.372</ref> but the film faced early competition with ''[[Crocodile Dundee II]]'', ''[[Big (film)|Big]]'' and ''[[Rambo III]]''.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1988/06/09/movies/crocodile-dundee-ii-top-film-at-box-office.html|title='Crocodile Dundee II' Top Film at Box Office|date=June 9, 1988|work=[[The New York Times]]|access-date=October 19, 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160307105708/http://www.nytimes.com/1988/06/09/movies/crocodile-dundee-ii-top-film-at-box-office.html|archive-date=March 7, 2016|url-status=live|agency=Associated Press|df=mdy-all}}</ref> Grossing $57.3 million at the box office in the United States and Canada,<ref>{{cite web |title=Willow |work=[[Box Office Mojo]] |url=https://www.boxofficemojo.com/release/rl2910553601/weekend/ |access-date=December 23, 2008 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090207210315/http://boxofficemojo.com/movies/?id=willow.htm |archive-date=February 7, 2009 |url-status=live |df=mdy-all }}</ref> it was not the [[blockbuster (entertainment)|blockbuster]] hit insiders had anticipated.<ref>Wasko, Janet. Hollywood in the information age: beyond the silver screen, page 198. Polity Press/Blackwell Publishers, UK (1994). {{ISBN|0-292-79093-7}}.</ref> The film opened in Japan in July and grossed $16.7 million in its first seven weeks, MGM's highest-grossing film in Japan at the time and went on to gross $20.3 million there.<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|title='Willow' Top Grosser For MGM in Japan|page=5|date=October 5, 1988}}</ref><ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|title=Film Briefs: Lucasfilm's 'Willow' not weeping overseas|page=9|date=December 28, 1988}}</ref> It performed well in other international markets, grossing $80.3 million<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=August 9, 1989|pages=30–31|title=MGM/UA International had a record breaking year (advertisement)}}</ref><ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=August 9, 1989|last=Groves|first=Don|page=11|title=UIP Up, Up and Away For Year; Rentals Take Off}}</ref> for a worldwide total of $137.6 million. Strong home video and television sales added to its profits.<ref>Maltby, Richard. Hollywood cinema: second edition, page 198. Blackwell Publishing, UK (1994). {{ISBN|0-631-21614-6}}.</ref>

===Critical reception===
''Willow'' was released to mixed reviews from critics.<ref name=alien/> {{Rotten Tomatoes prose|score=53|count=64|average=6.1|consensus=State-of-the-art special effects and an appealing performance from Warwick Davis can't quite save ''Willow'' from its slow pace and generic story.|ref=yes |access-date=June 13, 2026}} On [[Metacritic]], the film has a score of 47 out of 100 based on 12 critics, indicating "mixed or average reviews".<ref>{{Citation|title=Willow (1998) Reviews|url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/willow|publisher=[[Metacritic]]|access-date=December 4, 2022|archive-date=December 3, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221203210858/https://www.metacritic.com/movie/willow|url-status=live}}</ref> Audiences polled by [[CinemaScore]] gave the film an average grade of "A-" on an A+ to F scale.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Home|url=https://www.cinemascore.com/|access-date=December 4, 2022|website=[[CinemaScore]]|language=en-US|archive-date=April 13, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220413083139/https://www.cinemascore.com/|url-status=live}}</ref>

[[Janet Maslin]] from ''[[The New York Times]]'' praised Lucas's storytelling, but was critical of Ron Howard's direction. "Howard appears to have had his hands full in simply harnessing the special effects," Maslin said.<ref>{{cite news |last=Maslin |first=Janet |author-link=Janet Maslin |date=May 20, 1988 |title='Willow,' a George Lucas Production |work=[[The New York Times]] |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1988/05/20/movies/review-film-willow-a-george-lucas-production.html |access-date=October 19, 2015 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160127034009/http://www.nytimes.com/1988/05/20/movies/review-film-willow-a-george-lucas-production.html |archive-date=January 27, 2016 |url-status=live |df=mdy-all }}</ref>

[[Siskel and Ebert|Siskel & Ebert]] gave the film two thumbs down on their television show ''[[At the Movies (1986 TV program)|At the Movies]]''. Ebert's print review in the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'' was a mixed 2.5 stars out of a possible 4. He wrote that while the special effects were good, the core story was unengaging and the characters flat: "There can be no true suspense in a movie where even the characters seem to be inspired by other movies."<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/willow-1988#google_vignette|title=Willow movie review & film summary (1988)|website=Chicago Sun-Times|date=May 20, 1988|last=Ebert|first=Roger|via=RogerEbert.com}}</ref>

[[Desson Thomson]] writing in ''[[The Washington Post]]'', explained "[[Rob Reiner]]'s similar fairytale adventure ''[[The Princess Bride]]'' (which the [[Cinematography|cinematographer]] [[Adrian Biddle]] also shot) managed to evoke volumes more without razzle-dazzle. It's a sad thing to be faulting Lucas, maker of the ''[[Star Wars]]'' trilogy and ''[[Raiders of the Lost Ark]]'', for forgetting the tricks of entertainment."<ref>{{cite news |last=Desson |first=Howe |author-link=Desson Thomson |date=May 20, 1988 |title=Willow |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/longterm/movies/videos/willowpghowe_a0c895.htm |access-date=October 19, 2015 |archive-date=March 5, 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160305051001/http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/style/longterm/movies/videos/willowpghowe_a0c895.htm |url-status=live }}</ref> Mike Clark in ''[[USA Today]]'' wrote that "the rainstorm wrap-up, in which Good edges Evil is like ''[[Led Zeppelin]] Meets [[The Wild Bunch]]''. The film is probably too much for young children and possibly too much of the same for cynics. But any 6–13-year-old who sees this may be bitten by the ’movie bug’ for life."<ref name=digital/>

===Home media===
The film was first released on [[VHS]], [[Betamax]], [[Video 8]] and [[LaserDisc]] on November 22, 1988 by RCA/Columbia Pictures Home Video. The film also had multiple re-releases on VHS in the 1990s under Columbia TriStar Home Video as well as a widescreen LaserDisc in 1995. [[20th Century Home Entertainment|20th Century Fox Home Entertainment]] re-released the film on VHS and on [[DVD]] for the first time as a "[[special edition]]" on November 27, 2001, both of which were [[THX]] certified.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.awn.com/news/more-late-november-dvd-releases|title=More Late November DVD Releases|website=Animation World Network|date=November 5, 2001|last=Godfrey|first=Leigh}}</ref> The release included an [[audio commentary]] by Warwick Davis and two "making of" featurettes. In the commentary, Davis confirms that there were a number of "[[Deleted scene|lost scenes]]" previously rumored to have been deleted from it including a battle in the valley, Willow battling a boy who transforms into a fish-like monster in a lake while retrieving Fin Raziel, and an extended sorceress duel at the climax.<ref>{{cite web |title=Willow (Special Edition) (1988) |work=[[Amazon.com]] |date=November 27, 2001 |url=https://www.amazon.com/Willow-Special-Val-Kilmer/dp/B00003CXDD |access-date=December 23, 2008 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20081220230718/http://www.amazon.com/Willow-Special-Val-Kilmer/dp/B00003CXDD |archive-date=December 20, 2008 |url-status=live |df=mdy-all }}</ref> George Lucas and 20th Century Fox Home Entertainment released the film on [[Blu-ray]] disc on March 12, 2013, with an all-new digital transfer overseen by Lucasfilm.<ref>{{cite web |last=Neuwirth  |first=Aaron |title=WILLOW Comes To Blu-ray For The First Time In March |date=December 14, 2012 |url=https://whysoblu.com/willow-amazon-blu-ray-pre-order/ |access-date=December 17, 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121217050811/https://whysoblu.com/willow-amazon-blu-ray-pre-order/ |archive-date=December 17, 2012 |url-status=live |df=mdy-all |website=Why So Blu?}}</ref> Following Disney's acquisition of Lucasfilm (and 21st Century Fox's assets), the film was re-released with the Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer logo at the beginning by [[Walt Disney Studios Home Entertainment]] on Blu-ray, DVD and [[Digital distribution|Digital]] (for the first time) on January 29, 2019, and was later made available to stream on [[Disney+]] when the service launched on November 12, 2019. The 4K UHD Blu-ray of the film was released by Sony Pictures Home Entertainment on December 10, 2024.<ref>{{cite web|title=Disney Finally Bringing Willow to 4K For the Very First Time|url=https://comicbook.com/movies/news/willow-4k-release-date-disney-movie-george-lucas/|publisher=Comicbook|access-date=November 13, 2024|archive-date=November 13, 2024|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241113185756/https://comicbook.com/movies/news/willow-4k-release-date-disney-movie-george-lucas/|url-status=live|date=November 13, 2024|last=Ridgely|first=Charlie}}</ref>

===Novels===

Lucas outlined the ''Chronicles of the Shadow War'' trilogy to follow the film and hired comic book writer/novelist [[Chris Claremont]] to adapt them into a series of books. They take place about fifteen years after the original film and feature the teenage Elora Danan as a central character.
