{{nihongo|'''''Kuroneko'''''|藪の中の黒猫|Yabu no Naka no Kuroneko|lit. "A Black Cat in a Bamboo Grove"; or simply '''''The Black Cat'''''|lead=yes}}<ref name="Galbraith 1994">[[Stuart Galbraith IV|Galbraith IV, Stuart]] (1994). ''Japanese Fantasy, Science Fiction and Horror Films''. [[McFarland & Company]].</ref> is a 1968 Japanese ''[[jidaigeki]]'' [[horror film]] directed by [[Kaneto Shindō]], and an adaptation of a supernatural folktale. Set during a civil war in [[feudal Japan]],{{efn|''Kuroneko'' takes place during either the [[Heian period]] (794–1185){{sfn|Curran|Sato-Rossberg|Tanabe|2015|p=23}} or the [[Sengoku period]] (1467–1615).<ref name="McDonagh 2011">{{cite web|url=https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/2023-kuroneko-the-mark-of-the-cat|title=Kuroneko: The Mark of the Cat|last=McDonagh|first=Maitland|author-link=Maitland McDonagh|date=17 October 2011|publisher=[[The Criterion Collection]]|access-date=13 July 2021}}</ref>}} the film's plot concerns the vengeful spirits, or ''[[onryō]]'', of a woman and her daughter-in-law, who are raped and murdered by a band of [[samurai]]. It stars [[Nakamura Kichiemon II|Kichiemon Nakamura]], [[Nobuko Otowa]], and [[Kiwako Taichi]].

''Kuroneko'' was shot in black-and-white and in [[TohoScope]] format, and distributed by [[Toho]]. It was not dubbed in English, but was released with subtitles in the United States in 1968.<ref name="Galbraith 1994"/>

==Plot==
Yone and her daughter-in-law Shige, who live in a house in a bamboo grove, are raped and murdered by a troop of [[samurai]], and their house is burned down. A [[black cat]] appears, licking at the bodies.

The women return as [[ghost]]s with the appearance of fine ladies, who wait at [[Rajōmon]]. They find the samurai troop and bring them to an illusory mansion in the bamboo grove where the burnt-out house was. They seduce and then kill the samurai like cats, tearing their throats with their teeth.

Meanwhile, in [[Tōhoku|northern Japan]] a battle is taking place with the [[Emishi]]. A young man, Gintoki, fortuitously kills the enemy general, Kumasunehiko. He brings the severed head to show the governor, [[Minamoto no Raikō]]. He says that he fought the general under the name Gintoki. He is made a samurai in acknowledgement of his achievement. When he goes looking for his mother and wife, he finds their house burned down and the women missing.

Raikō tells Gintoki to find and destroy the ghosts who are killing the samurai. Gintoki encounters the two women and realizes that they are Yone, his mother, and Shige, his wife. They have made a pact with the underworld to return and kill samurai in revenge for their deaths. Because Gintoki has become a samurai, by their pact they must kill him, but Shige breaks her pledge to spend seven nights of love with Gintoki. Then, because she has broken the pact, Shige is condemned to the underworld. Reporting on his progress, a mournful Gintoki tells Raikō that he has destroyed one of the ghosts.

Gintoki encounters his spectral mother again at Rajōmon trying to seduce samurai. After seeing her reflection as a ghost in a pool of water, he attacks her with his sword, cutting off her arm, which takes on the appearance of a cat's limb. Gintoki brings the limb to Raikō, claiming it is evidence that he has killed the second ghost. Raikō is pleased and says Gintoki will be remembered as a hero, but first orders him to complete seven days of ritual purification. During the purification, Gintoki is visited by Yone, who claims to be a seer sent by the emperor to ward off evil spirits. She tricks Gintoki into giving her the limb, and then flies through the ceiling and disappears into the sky. Distraught and disheveled, Gintoki staggers through the woods to the cottage where he met the ghosts, and there he collapses. The walls disappear around him, revealing the charred remains of his family home where Shige and Yone were murdered. Snow falls and covers his body as a cat is heard meowing in the distance.

==Release==
''Kuroneko'' was released theatrically in Japan on February 24, 1968, where it was distributed by [[Toho]].{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=247}} It was released in the United States by Toho International with English subtitles in July 1968.{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=247}}

It was placed in competition at the [[1968 Cannes Film Festival]],<ref name="McDonagh 2011" /><ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.festival-cannes.com/en/archives/ficheFilm/id/2694/year/1968.html|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120308223958/http://www.festival-cannes.com/en/archives/ficheFilm/id/2694/year/1968.html|archive-date=8 March 2012|title=Festival de Cannes: Black Cat from the Grove|work=Festival de Cannes|access-date=5 April 2009}}</ref> but the festival was cancelled due to the events of [[May 1968 in France]].

It was released on DVD and Blu-Ray by [[The Criterion Collection]] on Oct. 18, 2011.

==Reception==
===Contemporary reviews===
{{expand section|date=October 2016}}
[[Tom Milne]] of the ''[[Monthly Film Bulletin]]'' found the film "Much less extravagant than Shindo's earlier excursion into ghostly horrors with ''Onibaba''", and that it was "more of a mood piece."<ref name="mfb">{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Monthly Film Bulletin]]|title=Yabu no Naka no Kueroneko (Kuroneko)|pages=78–79|volume=36|issue=423|publisher=[[British Film Institute]]|date=April 1969|last=Milne|first=Tom}}</ref> The review concluded that the film "has a sufficiently ingenious story to remain enjoyable throughout, and it sporadically discovers moments of genuinely bizarre invention".<ref name="mfb" />

===Retrospective assessments===
[[Manohla Dargis]], in a review of the film for ''[[The New York Times]]'' in 2010, described it as "a ghost story that's more eerie than unnerving, and often hauntingly lovely".<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2010/10/22/movies/22kuro.html|title=Tragic Love Tale of Spirits and Samurai|last=Dargis|first=Manohla|author-link=Manohla Dargis|date=21 October 2010|work=[[The New York Times]]|access-date=27 December 2012}}</ref> The following year, Maitland McDonagh called the film "darkly seductive" and "sleek, hair-raisingly graceful, and ready to take its place alongside the other landmarks of Japanese horror history".<ref name="McDonagh 2011" />

On the review aggregator website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film holds an approval rating of 95% based on {{nowrap|22 reviews}}, with a rating average of 8.1/10.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/kuroneko/|title=Kuroneko (1968)|website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]|access-date=13 July 2021}}</ref>

==Themes==
[[Yūsuke Suzumura]] of [[Hosei University]] has speculated that the film's title was deliberately intended to allude to the [[Ryūnosuke Akutagawa]] story ''[[In a Grove]]'' (''Yabu no naka'' in Japanese), as well as [[Akira Kurosawa]]'s [[Rashomon (film)|film version of the story]].<ref name=Suzumura>{{cite web |url=http://hijas.hosei.ac.jp/Default.aspx?tabid=913 |title=''Eiga no naka no Nihon'' |last1=Suzumura |first1=Yūsuke |work="Nihon-Ishiki" no Hensen: Kodai kara Kinsei e |publisher=Hosei University |access-date=28 December 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150610200831/http://hijas.hosei.ac.jp/Default.aspx?tabid=913 |archive-date=10 June 2015 |url-status=dead }}</ref> Although the Japanese title literally means "a black cat in a bamboo grove", the phrase ''yabu no naka'' in Japanese is also used idiomatically to refer to a mystery that is difficult to unravel. Suzumura also identified the legends of [[Minamoto no Raikō]] as an influence on the film: since Raikō himself appears in the film, it is likely that the film's protagonist's name {{nihongo|''Gintoki''|銀時||"silver-time"}} refers to the name of Raikō's legendary follower {{nihongo|[[Sakata no Kintoki|''Kintoki'']]|金時||"gold-time"}}.<ref name=Suzumura />
[[File:Kabuki-Play-Yoshitsune-Senbon-Zakura-1847.png|thumb|A depiction of the ''[[chūnori]]'' (riding in mid-air) technique found in [[kabuki]] theatre]]
In an essay about ''Kuroneko'', film critic [[Maitland McDonagh]] highlighted the roles cats play in Japanese folklore—particularly the ''[[bakeneko]]'', a ''[[yōkai]]'' (or supernatural entity) thought to have the ability to take the form of a human victim, often eating the victim in the process.<ref name="McDonagh 2011" /> ''Kuroneko'' is one of a number of Japanese [[Kaibyō#In film|"monster cat" horror films]] (''kaibyō eiga'' or ''bake neko mono''), a subgenre derived primarily from the repertoire of kabuki theatre.{{sfn|Curran|Sato-Rossberg|Tanabe|2015|p=30}}

Other theatrical elements observed in ''Kuroneko'' include the film's implementation of [[Spotlight (theatre lighting)|spotlights]];{{sfn|Curran|Sato-Rossberg|Tanabe|2015|p=33}} the use of smoke to create a ghostly atmosphere, which is characteristic of kabuki theatre;{{sfn|Curran|Sato-Rossberg|Tanabe|2015|p=34}} the dance movements of the mother's spirit, based on dances in [[Noh]] theatre;{{sfn|Curran|Sato-Rossberg|Tanabe|2015|p=34}} and the resemblance of the spirits' jumping and flying movements to ''[[chūnori]]'', a visual trick used in kabuki theatre in which actors are made to "fly" in mid-air through the use of wires.{{sfn|Curran|Sato-Rossberg|Tanabe|2015|p=34}} Additionally, lead actor Kichiemon Nakamura was a kabuki performer, and Hideo Kanze, who played the Mikado in ''Kuroneko'', specialized in Noh theatre.{{sfn|Curran|Sato-Rossberg|Tanabe|2015|p=34}}

==Legacy==
''Kuroneko'' was screened at a 2012 retrospective on Shindō and [[Kōzaburō Yoshimura]] in London, organised by the [[British Film Institute]] and the [[Japan Foundation]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.jpf.org.uk/attachments/399340778195988Two%20Masters%20of%20Japanese%20Cinema%20Press%20Release.pdf |title=Two Masters of Japanese Cinema: Kaneto Shindo & Kozaburo Yoshimura at BFI Southbank in June and July 2012 |website=Japan Foundation |access-date=18 July 2023}}</ref>

==Awards==
In Japan, the film won two awards from the [[Mainichi Film Concours]].{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=247}} [[Nobuko Otowa]] won the award for Best Actress for her work in ''Kuroneko'' and ''[[Operation Negligee]]'', and [[Kiyomi Kuroda]] won the award for Best Cinematography for this and ''Operation Negligee''.{{sfn|Galbraith IV|2008|p=247}}
