'''''The Store''''' is a 1983 documentary film directed by [[Frederick Wiseman]], his fifteenth feature-length documentary,<ref name="upi"/> and his first in color.<ref name="mamber"/>

The film explores the [[Dallas]] [[Neiman Marcus]] flagship store's wealthy customers,<ref name="rolfes">{{cite web |last1=Rolfes |first1=Ellen |title="Sell, sell, sell": How Neiman Marcus' flagship ==Reception==
A review on notcoming.com noted that "Wiseman alternates longer scenes with more atmospheric footage, and he often uses brief shots of elevators, stairs or escalators as transitional devices".<ref name="notcoming">{{cite web |title=The Store |url=http://www.notcoming.com/reviews/thestore |website=notcoming.com |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260306024538/http://www.notcoming.com/reviews/thestore |access-date=April 21, 2026 |archive-date=March 6, 2026 |language=en}}</ref> [[Stephen Mamber]] of UCLA wrote that "Wiseman keeps returning to a sable coat salesman so often it feels like the entire film has been a document of his effort".<ref name="mamber"/>

Film scholar [[Barry Keith Grant]] argued that ''The Store'' marked a shift in Wiseman's filmmaking, moving beyond the liberal humanism of his earlier films toward a more politically conscious, deconstructionist approach.{{sfn|Grant|2023|p=166}} Grant paired ''The Store'' with ''[[Model (film)|Model]]'' (1981) as films examining advertising and conspicuous consumption, arguing that the two together with ''[[Meat (1976 film)|Meat]]'' (1976) form a trilogy exploring what [[Vance Packard]] had called "the packaged soul".{{sfn|Grant|2023|p=184}} Grant described the films as Wiseman's "most overtly Brechtian", citing the director's use of mirrors and on-screen production apparatus to foreground the constructed nature of consumer imagery.{{sfn|Grant|2023|pp=188, 191–192}} Grant reported that some reviewers had been disappointed that ''Model'' and ''The Store'' revealed "nothing new about American culture", a view he rejected.{{sfn|Grant|2023|p=194}}
