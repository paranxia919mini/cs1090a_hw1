'''''The Devils''''' is a 1971  [[historical drama|historical]] [[psychological horror]]-[[psychological drama|drama]] film written, produced and directed by [[Ken Russell]], and starring [[Vanessa Redgrave]] and [[Oliver Reed]].<ref name="BFIsearch">{{Cite web |title=Collections Search – The Devils |url=https://collections-search.bfi.org.uk/web/Details/ChoiceFilmWorks/150016683 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250807043847/https://collections-search.bfi.org.uk/web/Details/ChoiceFilmWorks/150016683|archive-date=7 August 2025|url-status=live |website=[[British Film Institute]]}}</ref> A dramatised historical account of the fall of [[Urbain Grandier]], a 17th-century [[Catholic Church|Roman Catholic]] priest accused of [[witchcraft]] after the [[Loudun possessions|possessions in Loudun, France]], the plot also focuses on [[Jeanne des Anges|Sister Jeanne des Anges]], a [[sexual repression|sexually repressed]] nun who incites the accusations.

''The Devils'' is in part adapted from the 1952 non-fiction book ''[[The Devils of Loudun]]'' by [[Aldous Huxley]], as well as [[John Whiting]]'s subsequent 1960 play ''[[The Devils (play)|The Devils]]''. [[United Artists]] initially pitched the idea to Russell, but bowed out after reading his finished screenplay, as they felt it was too controversial in nature. [[Warner Bros. Pictures|Warner Bros.]] agreed to produce and distribute, and filming largely took place at [[Pinewood Studios]] in late 1970.

The film's graphic portrayal of violent and sexual content, often combined with religion, ignited harsh reaction from censors, and it originally received an [[X rating]] in both the United Kingdom and the United States. It was banned in several countries, and heavily edited for exhibition in others. Although critics largely dismissed ''The Devils'' for its explicit content, it won awards for Best Director at the [[33rd Venice International Film Festival]], as well as from the U.S. [[National Board of Review]]. Although a [[director's cut]] of the film was given its first theatrical release in the UK in 2002, it has not been made officially available on home media, and releases of edited versions on [[DVD]] and [[streaming service]]s remain scant. Following a debut at the [[2026 Cannes Film Festival]] on 14 May 2026, [[Warner Bros. Clockwork]] is scheduled to theatrically release a 4K restoration of the director's cut in North America for one week beginning 16 October 2026, followed by a release in the UK and Ireland through [[British Film Institute|BFI Distribution]] on 30 October; the restored version received an official [[NC-17]] rating from the [[Motion Picture Association]] for "sexual content/graphic nudity".<ref>{{cite web |title=Introducing our first NC-17 rated feature: KEN RUSSELL’S THE DEVILS |url=https://x.com/clockwork_wb/status/2086845079881412769 |website=[[X (social network)|X]] |publisher=[[Warner Bros. Clockwork]] |date=10 August 2026 |access-date=10 August 2026}}</ref>

Film scholarship primarily focuses on themes of sexual repression and [[political corruption]]. ''The Devils'' has been recognized as one of the most controversial films of all time by numerous publications and critics, and remained [[List of banned films|banned]] in Finland until 2001.

==Plot==
{{hatnote|This plot summary refers to the uncut version of the film. Some scenes in the description are omitted from other versions.}}

In 1634 France, following the aftermath of the [[Huguenot rebellions]], the [[Catholic Church in France|French Catholic]] statesman [[Cardinal Richelieu]] influences [[Louis XIII|King Louis XIII]] in an attempt to gain further power. Richelieu seeks to consolidate his authority by systematically divesting France's [[feudal lord]]s of their power and centralising the country's government. He convinces Louis that the fortifications of cities throughout France should be demolished to prevent [[Protestants]] from rising up. Louis agrees, but forbids Richelieu from carrying out demolitions in the fortified town of [[Loudun]], having made a promise to its Governor not to damage the town.

Meanwhile, in Loudun – where Catholics and [[Huguenots]] live in harmony – the Governor has died, leaving control of the city to [[Urbain Grandier]], a dissolute, proud and popular priest. He is having an affair with a relative of [[Canon (title)|Canon]] Jean Mignon, another priest in the town; Grandier is, however, unaware that the neurotic, hunchbacked Sister [[Jeanne des Anges]], the [[abbess]] of the local [[Ursulines|Ursuline]] convent, is sexually obsessed with him. Jeanne asks for Grandier to become the [[convent]]'s new [[confessor]]. Grandier secretly marries another woman, Madeleine De Brou, but news of this reaches Jeanne, who becomes jealous. When Madeleine returns a book by Ursuline foundress [[Angela Merici]] that Jeanne had earlier lent her, the abbess attacks her and accuses her of being a "[[fornicator]]" and "[[sacrilegious]]".

[[Baron]] Jean de Laubardemont arrives with orders to demolish the city's walls, overriding Grandier's orders to stop. Grandier summons the town's soldiers and forces Laubardemont to back down pending the arrival of an order for the demolition from King Louis. Grandier departs Loudun to visit the King. In the meantime, Canon Mignon tells Jeanne that he is to be their new confessor. She informs him of Grandier's marriage and affairs, and also inadvertently accuses Grandier of [[witchcraft]] and of [[Demonic possession|possessing]] her, which Mignon relays to Laubardemont. In the process, the information is pared down to just the claim that Grandier has bewitched the convent and [[Deal with the devil|dealt with the Devil]]. With Grandier away from Loudun, Laubardemont and Mignon decide to find evidence against him.

Laubardemont summons [[inquisitor]] Father Pierre Barre, a "professional [[Witch hunt|witch-hunter]]", whose interrogations involve depraved acts of "[[exorcism]]", including the forced administration of [[enema]]s to his victims. Jeanne claims that Grandier has bewitched her, and the other nuns do the same. A public exorcism erupts in a church, in which the nuns remove their clothes and enter a state of [[Mass psychogenic illness|"religious" frenzy]]. [[Henri, Prince of Condé (1588–1646)|Duke Henri de Condé]] (actually King Louis in disguise) arrives, claiming to be carrying a holy [[relic]] which can exorcise the "devils" possessing the nuns. Barre then proceeds to use the relic in "exorcising" the nuns, who then act as though they have been cured – until Condé/Louis reveals the case allegedly containing the relic to be empty. The possessions and the exorcisms then continue unabated, descending into a massive [[orgy]] in which the nuns remove the [[crucifix]] from above the [[high altar]] and masturbate with it. Mignon is both shocked and aroused by the sacrilegious chaos.

During the chaos, Grandier and Madeleine return. Grandier denies bewitching the nuns and condemns Jeanne, but he and Madeleine are arrested nonetheless. The nuns are returned to the convent, where Jeanne attempts to [[Suicide by hanging|hang herself]], but is cut down before she dies. After being given a [[show trial]], Grandier is shaven and tortured. The judges sentence Grandier to death by [[burning at the stake]]. Laubardemont has also obtained permission to destroy the city's fortifications. Despite pressure to confess to the charges, Grandier refuses, and is taken to be publicly burnt at the stake. His executioner promises to strangle him rather than let him suffer death by fire, but Barre starts the fire himself, and Mignon, visibly panic-stricken about the possibility of Grandier's innocence, pulls the noose tight before it can be used to strangle Grandier. As Grandier burns, Laubardemont orders for explosive charges to be set off and the city walls are blown up, causing the revelling townspeople to flee.

After the execution, Barre leaves Loudun to continue his witch-hunting activities elsewhere in the Vienne region of [[Nouvelle-Aquitaine]]. Laubardemont informs Jeanne that Mignon has been put away in an asylum for claiming that Grandier was innocent, and that "with no signed confession to prove otherwise, everyone has the same opinion". He gives her Grandier's charred [[femur]] and leaves. Jeanne kisses and masturbates with the bone. Madeleine, having been released, staggers over the rubble of Loudun's walls and walks out of the ruined city.

==Themes==
{{quote box|align=left|width=18em|quote="The most passionate and articulate of [[political film]]s, ''The Devils'' is intellectual and feverish, absolutely inspired in its design, appropriately terrifying, and yes, outrageous – because it is outraged. This may be the cinema's most damning indictment of humankind, and it is refreshed every time someone fails to see beyond the shame it depicts to its spine of dignity."|source=–[[Tim Lucas]] on the film's political messaging<ref name="BFILucas">{{cite web|url=https://www2.bfi.org.uk/films-tv-people/sightandsoundpoll2012/voter/51|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160228053408/http://www.bfi.org.uk/films-tv-people/sightandsoundpoll2012/voter/51|archive-date=28 February 2016|publisher=[[British Film Institute]]|access-date=23 November 2022|title=Tim Lucas}}</ref>}}
Addressing the film's initial reaction among critics, social groups and Warner Bros. executives, film historian [[Tim Lucas]] describes ''The Devils'' as "not merely an indictment of 17th Century conspiracies, but an indictment of political agendas which have been with us throughout the course of human history. When government is at its most immoral, history shows that it tends to ally itself with the Church, and to deflect public attention from its own corruption by demonizing convenient [[scapegoat]]s—artists, philosophers, [[progressives]]... in a word, ''[[Liberalism|liberals]]''." Comparing Grandier's demonization to various issues of contention in the United States during various decades of the 20th century, from [[Prohibition in the United States|alcohol in the 1920s]] to [[tobacco smoking]] in the 1990s, he notes that although public opinion on such issues evolves over time, the film articulates that "in the crossfire of ideals and oratory, lives are sometimes destroyed."{{sfn|Lucas|1996|p=38}}

Meanwhile, film scholar Thomas Atkins attests that, while ''The Devils'' contains overt themes regarding religion and political influence, the film is more concerned with "sex and sexual aberrations".{{sfn|Atkins|1976|p=55}} He interprets the film as being centrally thematically interested in [[sexual repression]] and its cumulative effects on the human psyche.{{sfn|Atkins|1976|p=59}} Commenting on the Sister Jeanne character, Atkins writes: "There are any number of examples of tormented visualization involving the Mother Superior... What more stunning visual metaphor for the psychological suffocation of the Mother Superior than to stuff her deformed body into a tiny lookout space from which she watches her fantasy lover? The mere confinement of mass in congested space creates an understanding of the annihilating pleasures of her sexual drive."{{sfn|Atkins|1976|p=58}} Atkins likens Sister Jeanne's erotic fantasy sequences to "eroticism from a deranged consciousness".{{sfn|Atkins|1976|p=64}} Filmmaker [[Alex Cox]] concurs with this point, stating that ''The Devils'' "could have been a turgid tale of human rights versus intolerance", but instead became something "much more: an intense, at times surreal, at times hideously realistic story of sexual obsession and oppression".<ref name="Forbidden">{{cite AV media |people=[[Alex Cox]] |date=5 February 2012 |title=Forbidden – James Ferman's introduction to 'The Devils' (BBC, 1995) |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NLrMjbPTYiY |access-date=23 November 2022 |time=4:09 |location=[[YouTube]] |publisher=Paul A J Lewis}}</ref>

The color [[white]] is featured significantly, specifically in the design of the cityscape, which largely consists of overtly white stone structures. Atkins insists that through this use of blank white, Russell establishes a "leitmotif of whiteness which resists and dissolves natural relationships";{{sfn|Atkins|1976|p=58}} Adam Scovell further notes that the "horrifying white-clean aesthetic" of Russell and Jarman's vision of Loudun appears to be inspired by Sister Jeanne's exorcism being compared to a "rape in a [[public lavatory]]" in the Huxley novel.<ref name="BBCstill"/>

==Production==
===Development===
[[File:Ken Russell in 1971.jpg|thumb|upright|left|Writer-director Ken Russell in 1971]]
In November 1965, following the [[Broadway theatre|Broadway]] opening of [[John Whiting]]'s 1961 play ''[[The Devils (play)|The Devils]]''—which was based on [[Aldous Huxley]]'s non-fiction book ''[[The Devils of Loudun]]'' (1952), about the alleged 17th-century [[Loudun possessions|possessions in Loudun, France]]—producer [[Alexander H. Cohen]] announced plans to adapt the play for film, revealing that he had made a formal offer to Huxley's estate for the rights. After Cohen's deal failed to materialize, [[United Artists]] signed a deal with producer [[Robert H. Solo]] to make the adaptation in August 1969.<ref name=afi/> Following the success of  ''[[Women in Love (film)|Women in Love]]'' (1969) in the United States, United Artists offered ''The Devils'' to director [[Ken Russell]]. According to journalist [[John Baxter (author)|John Baxter]] and Russell's widow Lisi Tribble, he was familiar with the story by the time United Artists proposed the project to him, having watched a production of Whiting's play in London and subsequently being inspired to research its historical basis.<ref name="BBCstill"/>{{sfn|Baxter|1973|pages=182–183}} Russell said that "when I first read the story, I was knocked out by it—it was just so shocking—and I wanted others to be knocked out by it, too. I felt I had to make it."{{sfn|Kermode|1996|p=53}} Russell later said at the time he made the film: "I was a devout Catholic and very secure in my faith. I knew I wasn't making a pornographic film... although I am not a political creature, I always viewed ''The Devils'' as my one political film. To me it was about [[brainwashing]], about the state taking over."{{sfn|Kermode|1996|p=53}}

Though Russell admired Whiting's play, his screenplay mainly drew from Huxley's book as he found the play "too sentimental".<ref name=afi/> Some extraneous elements incorporated into the screenplay were not found in either source, including details about the plague, which were supplied by Russell's brother-in-law, a scholar of French history.<ref name=afi/> Upon studying Grandier, Russell felt that he "represented the paradox of the Catholic church... Grandier is a priest but he is also a man, and that puts him into some absurd situations."{{sfn|Baxter|1973|p=204}} In Russell's original screenplay, the role of Sister Jeanne of the Angels, the disabled Mother Superior, was significantly larger, and continued after Grandier's execution.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=59}} However, in order to shorten the lengthy screenplay, Russell was forced to cut down the role.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=59}}

United Artists announced the film in August 1969, with Solo to produce under a three-picture deal with the studio, and Russell to direct.<ref name=afi/> Filming was set to start in May 1970.<ref>{{cite news|title=Huxley Drama to Be Film|newspaper=[[The New York Times]]|date=19 August 1969|page=18}}</ref> However, after United Artists executives read the screenplay, they "refused to touch it", abandoning plans to fund the production.{{sfn|Kermode|1996|p=53}} At the time, production designer [[Derek Jarman]] had already constructed some of the sets for the film.{{sfn|Baxter|1973|p=201}} It was subsequently acquired by [[Warner Bros. Pictures|Warner Bros.]],{{sfn|Kermode|1996|p=53}} who signed on in March 1970 to distribute the film.<ref name=afi/>

[[Oliver Reed]], who had worked with Russell previously on ''Women in Love'', was cast as [[Urbain Grandier]], the philandering doomed priest.<ref name=stars>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/38473791/the_los_angeles_times/|title=Stars Signed for 'The Devils'|newspaper=[[Los Angeles Times]]|page=15|date=19 June 1970|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> [[Richard Johnson (actor)|Richard Johnson]], who had portrayed Grandier in a stage production of ''The Devils'', had originally been attached to the project in 1969, but eventually dropped out of the production.<ref name=afi/> Reed agreed to do the film for a percentage of the profits.{{sfn|Reed|1979|p=135}} [[Gemma Jones]] was cast as Madeleine de Brou, Grandier's wife.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=58}} [[Jeanne des Anges|Sister Jeanne des Anges]] was originally to be played by [[Glenda Jackson]], who had starred opposite Reed in Russell's ''Women in Love'', as well as in Russell's ''[[The Music Lovers]]'' (1971).<ref name=afi/>{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=59}} However, Jackson turned down the role saying: "I don't want to play any more neurotic sex starved parts."<ref>{{cite news|title=A Queen without vanity|last=Toynbee|first=Polly|author-link=Polly Toynbee|newspaper=[[The Observer]]|page=3|date=28 February 1971}}</ref> Russell later claimed that he felt Jackson had actually turned down the role because it had been truncated from his original screenplay.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=59}} Jackson was replaced by [[Vanessa Redgrave]].{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=60}}

[[Max Adrian]] was cast as inquisitor Ibert (in his second-to-last film performance),{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=65}} while [[Dudley Sutton]], who had become a cult figure for his performance in ''[[The Leather Boys]]'' (1964), agreed to appear in the film as Baron de Laubardemont.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=64}} Sutton recalled that all of the "respectable actors turned it down. They thought it was blasphemous, which it is not."{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=64}} In the role of Father Mignon, a priest who attempts to usurp Grandier's power, Russell cast [[Murray Melvin]], despite the fact that he was decades younger than the character, who was intended to be in his eighties.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=66}} [[Michael Gothard]], an English character actor, was cast as self-professed witch hunter Father Barre.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=63}} Russell cast [[Christopher Logue]], an occasional actor who was mainly known as a poet and literary scholar, as the vengeful [[Cardinal Richelieu]].{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=67}}

As Philippe, the young woman Grandier impregnates, Russell cast television actress [[Georgina Hale]].{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=67}} Judith Paris, originally a dancer, was cast as Sister Agnes, Richelieu's niece who enters the convent under the guise of becoming a nun in order to gather information on Sister Jeanne.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=63}} In the credits, her role is mislabeled as "Sister Judith".{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=63}}

===Filming===
Filming began 17 August 1970<ref name=stars/> in London at [[Pinewood Studios]].<ref name=afi/> ''The Devils'' marked Jarman's debut in the film industry; having previously worked in theatre, he was introduced to Russell by his friend, [[Hornsey College of Art]] teacher Janet Detuer, and agreed to work on the film after seeing  ''Women in Love''. His set for Loudun, which took three months to design,{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=75}} was the largest set constructed on Pinewood's backlot since ''[[Cleopatra (1963 film)|Cleopatra]]'' (1963), and was influenced by the works of [[Neoclassical architecture|neoclassical]] artists such as [[Claude Nicolas Ledoux]], [[Étienne-Louis Boullée]] and [[Giovanni Battista Piranesi]].{{sfn|Ashby|2012|pp=15–16}} Russell and Jarman were further influenced by the blank white sets of [[Carl Theodor Dreyer]]'s ''[[The Passion of Joan of Arc]]'' (1928) and the cityscape in [[Fritz Lang]]'s ''[[Metropolis (1927 film)|Metropolis]]'' (1927).{{sfn|Ashby|2012|pp=15–16}}{{sfn|Baxter|1973|p=207}} The interiors of many buildings, specifically the convent, were crafted with [[plaster]] and made to appear as masonry; the plaster designs were then nailed to plywood framework.{{sfn|Baxter|1973|p=206}} According to Christopher Hobbs, the film's prop designer, Jarman "was very inexperienced, and found the work exhaustive and difficult", but Russell was very supportive of his ideas and designs.{{sfn|Ashby|2012|p=15}}

Director Russell hired a large cast of [[extra (acting)|extras]], whom he later referred to as "a bad bunch" who were demanding, and alleged that one of the female extras appearing as a civilian was sexually assaulted by another male extra.{{sfn|Baxter|1973|pages=208–209}} Jones recalled that Reed, who at the time had a reputation for being disruptive and confrontational, was extremely kind to her on set, and "behaved impeccably".{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=58}} Sutton recalled of Redgrave that she was "always an adventurous type of person" on the set, in terms of exploring her character and interacting with the other performers.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=61}} Russell echoed this sentiment, referring to Redgrave as "one of the least bothersome actresses I could ever wish for; she just threw herself into it."{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=88}}

Additional photography occurred at [[Bamburgh Castle]] in [[Northumberland]], England.<ref name=afi/> Jones recalled filming at the castle as challenging due to cold weather and having the flu during this period of the shoot, to which Russell was "frightfully unsympathetic".{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=88}} Throughout the shoot, Russell and Reed clashed frequently, and by the time principal photography had finished, the two were hardly on speaking terms.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=118}}

===Music===
The film's score was composed for a small ensemble by [[Peter Maxwell Davies]].{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=81}} Russell was reportedly intrigued by Davies' composition ''[[Eight Songs for a Mad King]]'', which had provoked strong reactions when it was premiered in 1969. Davies reportedly took the job because he was interested in the late medieval and Renaissance historical period depicted in the film.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|pages=81–2}} He went on to write a score for Russell's next film, ''[[The Boy Friend (1971 film)|The Boy Friend]]''. Australian promoter [[James Murdoch (music advocate)|James Murdoch]], who was also Davies' agent, was involved in organising the music for both these films.

The score was recorded by Davies' regular collaborators the [[Fires of London]] who took on extra players as the score required more instruments than their basic line-up.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=83}} Davies' music is complemented by period music (including a couple of numbers from [[Terpsichore (Praetorius)|''Terpsichore'']]), performed by the [[Early Music Consort|Early Music Consort of London]] under the direction of [[David Munrow]].<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.maxopus.com/work_detail.aspx?key=255 | title=The Devils: composer's note | website=maxopus.com | access-date=21 March 2016 | archive-date=3 April 2016 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160403112725/http://www.maxopus.com/work_detail.aspx?key=255}}</ref>
In 1974, the Fires of London and the Early Music Consort of London gave a performance of a concert suite of the music at the [[BBC Proms|Proms]].<ref name="BBC">{{Cite web |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/events/enrzc8|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171221133349/https://www.bbc.co.uk/events/enrzc8|archive-date=21 December 2017|publisher=[[BBC]]|title=Prom 40}}</ref> ''Terpsichore'', which was not well known when the film was made,<ref name="Holman">[[Peter Holman]]: ''Terpsichore at 400: Michael Praetorius as a Collector of Dances.'' The Viola da Gamba Society Journal, Volume Six, 2012. S.&nbsp;34-51. [https://www.vdgs.org.uk/files/VdGSJournal/Vol-06.pdf Online] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160406214936/http://www.vdgs.org.uk/files/VdGSJournal/Vol-06.pdf |date=2016-04-06 }}</ref> has become a popular choice for concert hall performances, and Davies' contributions to the soundtrack have also been revived in the concert hall.<ref name="Telegraph">{{Cite news |url=https://www.telegraph.co.uk/culture/music/classicalconcertreviews/9721818/H7steria-BBC-Concert-Orchestra-Queen-Elizabeth-Hall-review.html |title=H7steria, BBC Concert Orchestra, Queen Elizabeth Hall, review |last=Hewett |first=Ivan |author-link=Ivan Hewett |date=4 December 2012 |newspaper=[[The Daily Telegraph]]|url-access=subscription|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171227191238/http://www.telegraph.co.uk/culture/music/classicalconcertreviews/9721818/H7steria-BBC-Concert-Orchestra-Queen-Elizabeth-Hall-review.html|archive-date=27 December 2017|url-status=live}}</ref>

==Release and reception==
===Box office===
''The Devils'' was one of the most popular films in 1972 at the British box office,{{sfn|Harper|2011|p=269}} earning [[theatrical rental]]s of approximately $8–9 million in Europe. It was also popular in Italy. With an additional $2 million in the United States and Canada from a limited release, its worldwide rentals total roughly $11 million.<ref name=rental/>

===Critical reception===
''The Devils'' received significant negative critical reaction upon its release due to its "outrageous", "overheated", and "pornographic" nature.{{sfn|Hanke|1984|p=118}} The film was publicly condemned by the [[Holy See|Vatican]], who, though acknowledging that it contained some artistic merit, asked that its screenings at the [[Venice International Film Festival]] be cancelled.<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[The Des Moines Register]]|date=31 August 1975|title=Vatican Assails Film on Priests, Orgies in Convent|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/38473359/the_des_moines_register/|via=Newspapers.com|agency=[[Associated Press]]}}</ref> [[Judith Crist]] called the film a "grand fiesta for sadists and perverts",{{sfn|Hanke|1984|p=118}} while [[Roger Ebert]] of the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'' gave the film a rare zero-star rating, sarcastically mocking it as overly self-important.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/the-devils|title=The Devils|author-link=Roger Ebert|first=Roger|last=Ebert|newspaper=[[Chicago Sun-Times]]|year=1971|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191004173549/https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/the-devils|archive-date=4 October 2019}}</ref> [[Pauline Kael]] wrote in ''[[The New Yorker]]'' that Russell "doesn't report hysteria, he markets it."{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=157}} [[Vincent Canby]], writing for ''[[The New York Times]]'', noted that the film contains "silly, melodramatic effects", and felt that the performances were hindered by the nature of the screenplay, writing: "Oliver Reed suggests some recognizable humanity as poor Father Grandier, but everyone else is ridiculous. Vanessa Redgrave, who can be, I think, a fine actress, plays Sister Jeanne with a plastic hump, a Hansel-and-Gretel giggle, and so much sibilance that when she says 'Satan is ever ready to seduce us with sensual delights', you might think that [[Groucho Marx]] had let the air out of her tires."<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[The New York Times]]|date=17 July 1971|last=Canby|first=Vincent|author-link=Vincent Canby|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1971/07/17/archives/the-devils-an-adaptation-with-fantasywork-by-ken-russell-opens-at.html|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191105003211/https://www.nytimes.com/1971/07/17/archives/the-devils-an-adaptation-with-fantasywork-by-ken-russell-opens-at.html|archive-date=5 November 2019|title='The Devils,' an Adaptation With Fantasy: Work by Ken Russell Opens at Fine Arts Miss Redgrave in Role of Sister Jeanne}}</ref> Kael also wrote that while the "critics were turned off by the madness" that the audiences "were turned on by it."<ref>{{Cite book |last=Kael |first=Pauline |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=tkShTL84MrcC&q=the+age+of+movies+selected+writing+of+pauline+kael |title=The Age of Movies: Selected Writings of Pauline Kael: A Library of America Special Publication |date=2011-10-27 |publisher=Library of America |isbn=978-1-59853-171-8 |pages=298 |language=en}}</ref>

[[Charles Champlin]] of the ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' lambasted the film, writing that its message is "not anti-clerical—there's hardly enough clericalism to be anti anymore—it is anti-humanity. A rage against cruelty has become a celebration of it... you weep not for the evils and the ignorance of the past, but for the cleverness and sickness of the day."<ref>{{cite news|last=Champlin|first=Charles|author-link=Charles Champlin|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/38424773/the_los_angeles_times/|newspaper=[[Los Angeles Times]]|page=57|title=The Games 'Devils' Plays|date=16 July 1971|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> Ann Guarino of the [[New York Daily News|New York ''Daily News'']] noted that the film "could not be more anti-Catholic in tone or more sensationalized in treatment", but conceded that the performances in the film were competent.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/38424811/daily_news/|newspaper=[[New York Daily News]]|last=Guarino|first=Ann|date=17 July 1971|title=Ken Russell's 'Devils' Is Anti-Religious Film|via=Newspapers.com|page=47}}</ref> The ''[[Ottawa Citizen]]''{{'}}s Gordon Stoneham similarly felt the film had been over-sensationalized, noting that Russell focuses so much on the "baroque effects, and concentrates so much on the [[Grand Guignol]] aspects of the affair, the narrative is never firmly in focus."<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[Ottawa Citizen]]|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/38459063/the_ottawa_citizen/|last=Stoneham|first=Gordon|date=13 October 1971|page=91|via=Newspapers.com|title=The Devils bizarre film but fascinating}}</ref>

Bridget Byrne of the ''[[Los Angeles Herald Examiner]]'' alternately praised the film as "brilliant, audacious, and grotesque", likening it to a fairytale, but added that audiences "have to grasp its philosophy, work out the undercurrents of seriousness, close the structural gaps for [themselves], even as [they] are transported by a literal orgy of splendor."<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[Los Angeles Times]]|date=21 July 1971|title=Russell's 'Devils': Brilliant, Audacious, and Grotesque|page=15|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/38424747/the_los_angeles_times/|last=Byrne|first=Bridget}}</ref> Writing for the [[Hackensack, New Jersey]] ''Record'', John Crittenden praised the film's visuals as "genius", but criticised Reed's performance while asserting that Redgrave was underused.<ref>{{cite news|date=19 July 1971|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/38424724/the_record/|last=Crittenden|first=John|page=17D|newspaper=The Record|location=Hackensack, New Jersey|title=A Devil's Brew of Garish Sex|via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> Stephen Farber of ''The New York Times'' noted the film as an ambitious work, conceding that the "ideas in Russell's film may seem overly schematic, but his terrifying, fantastical nightmare images have astonishing psychological power."<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[The New York Times]]|date=15 August 1971|title='The Devils' Finds An Advocate|last=Farber|first=Stephen|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1971/08/15/archives/the-devils-finds-an-advocate-the-devils-finds-an-advocate.html|page=1}}</ref>

On the [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], ''The Devils'' holds an approval rating of 55%, based on 101 reviews. The website's critics consensus reads, "Grimly stylish, Ken Russell's baroque opus is both provocative and persuasive in its contention that the greatest blasphemy is the leveraging of faith for power."<ref>{{cite web|title=The Devils|url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/the_devils|publisher=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]|access-date=24 August 2026}}</ref> On [[Metacritic]], it has a weighted average score of 49 out of 100, based on 11 critics, indicating "mixed or average" reviews.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-devils/critic-reviews|title=The Devils|publisher=[[Metacritic]]|access-date=26 December 2019}}</ref>

===Censorship history===
The explicit sexual and violent content, paired with its commentary on religious institutions, resulted in significant [[censorship]].<ref name=afi/><ref name="hollywood">{{cite web|url=https://hollywood-elsewhere.com/2010/03/a_history_of_ce.php|website=Hollywood Elsewhere|title=A History of Censorship|last=Wells|first=Jeffrey|date=29 March 2010|access-date=8 April 2010|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100625024205/http://hollywood-elsewhere.com/2010/03/a_history_of_ce.php|archive-date=25 June 2010}}</ref> Commenting on the controversial nature, Reed stated: "We never set out to make a pretty Christian film. [[Charlton Heston]] made enough of those... The film is about twisted people."<ref name="reed">{{cite news|title=Oliver Burns—at the Stake and at Film Critics|last=Kramer|first=Carol|author-link=Carol Kramer|newspaper=[[Chicago Tribune]]|date=22 August 1971|page=E3|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/38473878/chicago_tribune/|via=Newspaprs.com}}</ref> The [[British Board of Film Classification|British Board of Film Censors]] found the combination of religious themes and violent sexual imagery a serious challenge, particularly as the Board was being lobbied by socially conservative pressure groups such as the [[Nationwide Festival of Light|Festival of Light]] at the time of distribution.{{sfn|Robertson|1993|pages=139–146}} In order to gain a release and earn a British [[X certificate#United Kingdom|'X' certificate (suitable for those aged 18 and over)]], Russell made minor cuts{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=131}} to the more explicit nudity (mainly in the cathedral and convent sequences), details from the first exorcism (mainly that which indicated an anal insertion), some shots of the crushing of Grandier's legs, and a pantomime sequence during the climactic burning.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|pages=169–70}} Russell later said:

{{blockquote|Trevelyan's objections to my original cut were that the scenes of torture—such as the skewering of Grandier's tongue with needles, or the breaking of his knees—simply went on too long. To him, these scenes needed only a few frames to tell the audience what was happening; after that, the sounds and their imaginations would fill in the rest. With the exception of one sequence ("The Rape of Christ") which was removed in its entirety, all Trevelyan did was to tone down or reduce the scenes as I had cut them.{{sfn|Kermode|1996|p=54}}}}

{{quote box|align=right|width=22em|quote="''The Devils'' is a harsh film—but it's a harsh subject. I wish the people who were horrified and appalled by it would have read the book, because the bare facts are far more horrible than anything in the film."|source=–Russell on the film's controversial content{{sfn|Baxter|1973|p=202}}}}
The most significant cuts were made by Warner Bros., prior to submission to the BBFC. Two notable scenes were removed in their entirety: One was a two-and-a-half-minute sequence featuring naked nuns sexually defiling a statue of Christ, which includes Father Mignon looking down on the scene and [[Masturbation#Male masturbation|masturbating]].{{sfn|Baxter|1973|p=210}} Another scene towards the end showed Sister Jeanne masturbating with the charred [[femur]] of Grandier following his burning at the stake.<ref name="BBCstill">{{cite web|url=https://www.bbc.com/culture/article/20210802-why-x-rated-masterpiece-the-devils-is-still-being-censored|title=Why X-rated masterpiece The Devils is still being censored|last=Scovell|first=Adam|date=2 August 2021|publisher=[[BBC]]|access-date=9 October 2021}}</ref><ref name=burn>{{cite news|url=https://www.austinchronicle.com/daily/screens/2013-09-22/burn-witch-burn/|date=22 September 2019|title=Burn, Witch, Burn|newspaper=[[The Austin Chronicle]]|last=Savlov|first=Marc|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160131205828/http://www.austinchronicle.com/daily/screens/2013-09-22/burn-witch-burn/|archive-date=31 January 2016}}</ref> Due to its content, the former scene has been popularly referred to as the "Rape of Christ" sequence.<ref name=lister>{{cite news|newspaper=[[The Independent]]|url=https://www.independent.co.uk/news/media/russells-rape-of-christ-to-be-shown-128544.html|title=Russell's 'rape of Christ' to be shown|last=Lister|first=David|date=20 November 2002|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191104090843/https://www.independent.co.uk/news/media/russells-rape-of-christ-to-be-shown-128544.html|archive-date=4 November 2019}}</ref><ref name=gibbons>{{cite news|last=Gibbons|first=Fiachra|url=https://www.theguardian.com/uk/2002/nov/20/filmnews.film1|date=19 November 2002|title=Cut sequence from The Devils discovered|newspaper=[[The Guardian]]|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151027162920/http://www.theguardian.com/uk/2002/nov/20/filmnews.film1|archive-date=27 October 2015}}</ref>

The British theatrical cut, which runs 111 minutes, was given an 'X' certificate (no one under 18 years of age admitted);{{sfn|Crouse|2012|pages=123, 130–31}} despite the BBFC's approval of this version, it was banned in 17 [[Local government in England|local councils]] across Britain.{{sfn|Kermode|1996|p=39}} In the United States, it was truncated further for theatrical exhibition: The [[Motion Picture Association|Motion Picture Association of America]] (MPAA) edited to approximately 108 minutes, and also awarded an [[X rating#United States|'X' rating]].<ref name=afi/> Russell expressed frustration, commenting that they "killed the key scene" [the Rape of Christ] and that "Warner Brothers cut out the best of ''The Devils''."<ref name=afi/> Speaking on the American version of the film, Russell said the cuts "adversely affected the story, to the point where in America the film is disjointed and incomprehensible."{{sfn|Kermode|1996|p=55}} In 1973, in anticipation of a reissue of the film coinciding with their release of ''[[The Exorcist]]'' (1973), Warner Bros. resubmitted the American version of ''The Devils'' to the MPAA after further removing several allusions to [[lesbianism]] prior to Sister Jeanne's attempted suicide and a brief shot of Grandier's shattered legs during the torture scene; this version was given an 'R' rating.{{sfn|Lucas|1996|pp=40–1}} The film remained [[List of banned films|banned]] in Finland for over 30 years, until it was lifted in November 2001.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.elonet.fi//fi/elokuva/101400|website=[[Elonet]]|title=Devils, The|date=1971 |language=fi|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181014021123/https://www.elonet.fi//fi/elokuva/101400|archive-date=14 October 2018|url-status=live}}</ref>

The director's cut (approximately 117 minutes), with all aforementioned footage restored, was screened for the first time in London on 25 November 2002, along with a making-of documentary titled ''Hell on Earth'', produced for [[Channel 4]].<ref name=lister/> The extended version was procured by [[Mark Kermode]], who uncovered the footage in the Warner Bros. vaults.<ref name=gibbons/> In a 2014 videoblog titled "Kermode Uncut: What To Do About The Devils", Kermode revealed that Warner Bros. was responsible for blocking various efforts to allow the director's cut of the film to be released on home media, as they considered the tone of the deleted material, specifically the "Rape of Christ" sequence, to be "distasteful". ===Home media===
''The Devils'' has had a complicated release history in home media formats, with various cuts being made available in different formats.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|pages=163–164}} The film made its America home media debut circa 1979–1980, when [[ON TV (TV network)|ON TV]] made the 1973 'R' rated version available as part of its [[Late night television|late night programming]].{{sfn|Lucas|1996|p=40}} [[Warner Home Video]] released the film on VHS in the United States in 1983, labelled as featuring a 105-minute cut of the film;<ref>{{cite AV media|title=The Devils|publisher=[[Warner Home Video]]|year=1983|id=11110|medium=[[VHS]]}}</ref> however, this is a misprint, as this edition actually runs 103 minutes, due to time compression.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=164}} Warner reissued a VHS edition in 1995, with a corrected label of a 103-minute running time.<ref>{{cite AV media|title=The Devils|publisher=[[Warner Home Video]]|year=1995|asin=6300268918|medium=[[VHS]]}}</ref> On 30 May 1995, the UK theatrical version made its debut on British television as part of [[BBC2]]'s ''Forbidden Weekend'', a series of films with troubled censorship histories; the screening was introduced by Cox and then-BBFC director [[James Ferman]].<ref name="Forbidden" />{{sfn|Lucas|1996|p=40}} This version was shown in a partially [[letterboxed]] [[aspect ratio]] of 1.80:1, with the credits being shown in the correct 2.35:1 ratio. Prior to this broadcast, the UK theatrical version had been shown on [[Sat.1]] in Germany, with a 1.73:1 aspect ratio.{{sfn|Lucas|1996|p=43}} A VHS was issued in the United Kingdom by Warner in 2002 as part of their "Masters of the Movies" series, reportedly running 104 minutes.<ref>{{cite AV media|title=The Devils|publisher=[[Warner Home Video]]|year=2002|asin=B00004CIPH|medium=[[VHS]]}}</ref> However, according to film historian [[Richard Crouse]], the VHS version available in the United Kingdom is identical to the American VHS editions, despite the labelled runtimes.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=164}} Lucas notes that most VHS releases of the film featured either "sloppy" [[pan and scan]] work or poor letterboxing.{{sfn|Lucas|1996|p=41}}

A bootlegged [[NTSC]]-format DVD was released by Angel Digital in 2005, with the excised footage reinstated, along with the ''Hell on Earth'' documentary included as a bonus feature.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=173}} In 2008, it was suspected that Warner Home Video may have been planning a U.S. DVD release for the film, as cover artwork was leaked on the internet.<ref name=db>{{cite web|website=The Digital Bits|url=https://thedigitalbits.com/item/devils|title=Devils, The|date=4 October 2012|last=Jahnke|first=Adam|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121006073833/https://thedigitalbits.com/item/devils|archive-date=6 October 2012}}</ref> However, Warner responded to the claim by deeming the leaked news an "error".<ref name=db/> In June 2010, Warner released ''The Devils'' in a 108-minute version for purchase and rental through the [[iTunes Store]],<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.popmatters.com/130053-like-touching-the-dead-ken-russells-the-devils-1971-2496148474.html|website=[[PopMatters]]|title=Like Touching the Dead: Ken Russell's 'The Devils' (1971)|date=24 August 2010|last=Gibron|first=Bill|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190502230402/https://www.popmatters.com/130053-like-touching-the-dead-ken-russells-the-devils-1971-2496148474.html|archive-date=2 May 2019}}</ref> but the title was removed without explanation three days later.{{sfn|Crouse|2012|p=175}}

On 19 March 2012, the [[British Film Institute]] released a two-disc DVD featuring the 111-minute UK theatrical version (sped up to 107 minutes to accommodate the technicalities of [[PAL]] colour).<ref name=db/><ref name=bfidvd>{{cite web|url=http://filmstore.bfi.org.uk/acatalog/info_21668.html|title=DVD & Blu-ray: The Devils |website=BFI Filmstore|archive-url=https://archive.today/20120710232519/http://filmstore.bfi.org.uk/acatalog/info_21668.html|archive-date=10 July 2012}}</ref> The BFI release also includes the Kermode documentary ''Hell on Earth'', as well as a vintage documentary shot during the production entitled ''Directing Devils'', among other features.<ref name=bfidvd/>

In March 2017, streaming service [[Shudder (streaming service)|Shudder]] began carrying the 109-minute U.S. release version of ''The Devils''.<ref>{{cite web|last=Rife|first=Katie|title=Ken Russell's widely banned The Devils makes a surprise appearance on Shudder |url=https://avclub.com/article/ken-russells-banned-masterpiece-devils-makes-surpr-252125|website=[[The A.V. Club]]|date=15 March 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180929194547/https://news.avclub.com/ken-russell-s-widely-banned-the-devils-makes-a-surprise-1798259136|archive-date=29 September 2018}}</ref> In September 2018, [[FilmStruck]] began streaming the same U.S. cut and it was subsequently added to the [[The Criterion Collection|Criterion Channel]] in October 2019, almost a year after FilmStruck shut down.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/6602-coming-attractions-the-criterion-channel-s-october-2019-lineup|publisher=[[The Criterion Collection]]|date=30 September 2019|title=Coming Attractions: The Criterion Channel's October 2019 Lineup|url-status=live|archive-date=4 November 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191104090302/https://criterion.com/current/posts/6602-coming-attractions-the-criterion-channel-s-october-2019-lineup}}</ref> The film was removed from Criterion's offerings shortly afterwards, but re-uploaded in January 2024.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Search|url=https://www.criterionchannel.com/search?q=%22the%20devils%22|access-date=2021-01-24|website=The Criterion Channel}}</ref>

===Restoration===
In May 2026, [[Warner Bros.]] announced that a [[4K resolution|4K]] restoration of the director's cut of ''The Devils'', created from the original camera negative, would be released through its newly-launched [[Warner Bros. Clockwork]] label.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2026-05-05 |title=Cannes Classics 2026 |url=https://www.festival-cannes.com/en/press/press-releases/cannes-classics-2026/ |access-date=2026-05-05 |website=Festival de Cannes |language=en-US}}</ref> The restoration premiered in the Cannes Classics section of the [[2026 Cannes Film Festival]] on 14 May. Warner Bros. Clockwork subsequently scheduled a one-week theatrical release in North America beginning 16 October 2026, while [[British Film Institute|BFI Distribution]], in partnership with Clockwork, scheduled the film for release in the United Kingdom and Ireland on 30 October.<ref>{{cite web|title=Warner Bros Clockwork to release Cannes Classics selection 'Ken Russell's The Devils'|website=[[Screen Daily]]|first=Jeremy|last=Kay|date=2026-05-06|access-date=2026-05-06|url=https://www.screendaily.com/news/warner-bros-clockwork-to-release-cannes-classics-selection-ken-russells-the-devils/5216343.article}}</ref>

Ahead of its North American re-release, the restored version received an [[NC-17]] rating from the [[Motion Picture Association]] for "sexual content/graphic nudity".<ref>{{cite web |title=Introducing our first NC-17 rated feature: KEN RUSSELL’S THE DEVILS |url=https://x.com/clockwork_wb/status/2086845079881412769 |website=[[X (social network)|X]] |publisher=[[Warner Bros. Clockwork]] |date=10 August 2026 |access-date=10 August 2026}}</ref> The classification returned the film to the MPA’s adults-only category more than five decades after its original American release received an [[X rating|X]] rating; a further-cut version released in the United States in 1973 had received an [[Motion Picture Association film rating system|R]] rating.<ref>{{cite web |title=55 Years Later, ‘‘The Devils’’ Is Still An Audacious Shock To The System |url=https://www.inverse.com/entertainment/the-devils-ken-russell-55-year-anniversary |website=[[Inverse (website)|Inverse]] |date=16 July 2026 |access-date=10 August 2026}}</ref>

==Legacy==
''The Devils'' has been cited as one of the most controversial films of all time<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2017/03/devils-ken-russell-banned-horror-streaming-shudder-x-rated-1201793838/|website=[[IndieWire]]|title='The Devils': Ken Russell's Banned 1971 Religious Horror Film Finally Gets Streaming Release|last=Dry|first=Jude|date=15 March 2017|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180425072939/https://indiewire.com/2017/03/devils-ken-russell-banned-horror-streaming-shudder-x-rated-1201793838/|archive-date=25 April 2018}}</ref>
by such critics as [[Richard Crouse]], among others.<ref name=raising>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[The Hollywood Reporter]]|title=Raising Hell|url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-reviews/raising-hell-373942/|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130913054319/https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/raising-hell-373942|archive-date=13 September 2013|url-status=live|date=25 September 2012|last=Grey|first=Tobias}}</ref> [[Filmsite]] included it in their list of the 100 most controversial films ever made,<ref>{{cite web|website=[[Filmsite]]|url=https://www.filmsite.org/controversialfilms6.html|title=The 100+ Most Controversial Films of All-Time: 1970-1971|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181014125207/https://www.filmsite.org/controversialfilms6.html|archive-date=14 October 2018|url-status=live}}</ref> and in 2015, ''[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out]]'' magazine ranked it 47 on their list of the "50 Most Controversial Movies in History".<ref>{{cite magazine|url=https://www.timeout.com/newyork/film/the-50-most-controversial-movies-ever|magazine=[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out]]|location=New York|title=The 50 most controversial movies ever made|last1=Uhlich|first1=Keith|last2=Rothkopf|first2=Joshua|last3=Ehrlich|first3=David|last4=Fear|first4=David|date=12 August 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160725133826/https://timeout.com/newyork/film/the-50-most-controversial-movies-ever|archive-date=25 July 2016|url-status=live}}</ref>

Following his transition into [[experimental filmmaking]], Jarman produced the [[Super 8 film|Super 8]] short film ''The Devils at the Elgin'' (1974). Described by Sam Ashby as a "hypnotic, nightmarish monochrome that loops some of the more religious iconographic moments" from Russell's original, the film was captured from a screening of ''The Devils'' at the [[Elgin Theater]] in New York City. Jarman noted that Maddeline's escape from Loudun in the original film's ending gave the impression that "she walks into a blizzard of ashes" when rendered on Super 8 stock. When screening ''The Devils at the Elgin'' at festivals or his studio, Jarman would synchronize the film to a [[cassette tape]] recording of [[Nico (German singer)|Nico]]'s cover version of [[the Doors]]' song "The End".{{sfn|Ashby|2012|pp=17–18}}

Film historian Joel W. Finler described ''The Devils'' as Russell's "most brilliant cinematic achievement, but widely regarded as his most distasteful and offensive work."{{sfn|Finler|1985|p=240}} In 2002, when 100 filmmakers and critics were asked by ''[[Sight and Sound]]'' to cite what they considered to be the ten most important films ever made, ''The Devils'' featured in the lists submitted by Kermode and Cox;<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/sightandsound/topten/poll/voted.php?film=The%20Devils%20(K.%20Russell)|title=Sight and Sound Top Ten Poll 2002 – Who voted for which film: The Devils|publisher=[[British Film Institute]]|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171201154709/http://www.bfi.org.uk/news/50-greatest-films-all-time?film=The%20Devils%20%28K.%20Russell%29|archive-date=1 December 2017}}</ref> for the magazine's 2012 poll, the film appeared in the lists submitted by Kermode and Lucas, as well as those submitted by filmmaker [[Abel Ferrara]] and fellow critics [[Vic Pratt]], Billy Chainsaw and David Sorfa.<ref name="BFI2012">{{cite web|url=https://www2.bfi.org.uk/films-tv-people/4ce2b6a8c47c2/sightandsoundpoll2012|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180118221701/http://www.bfi.org.uk/films-tv-people/4ce2b6a8c47c2/sightandsoundpoll2012|archive-date=18 January 2018|publisher=British Film Institute|access-date=23 November 2022|title=Votes for The Devils (1972)}}</ref> In 2014, Mexican filmmaker [[Guillermo del Toro]] publicly criticized Warner Bros. for censoring the film and limiting its availability in home video markets.<ref>{{cite magazine|title=Guillermo del Toro Slams Warner Bros. for Censoring Ken Russell's 'The Devils'|magazine=[[The Hollywood Reporter]]|date=25 November 2014|last=Vlessing|first=Etan|url=https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/guillermo-del-toro-slams-warner-752240/|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180621065613/https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/guillermo-del-toro-slams-warner-752240|archive-date=21 June 2018}}</ref> The ''#FreeTheDevils'' campaign officially began in 2014. The movement, created by filmmaker Dave King with Ken Russell's widow Lisi Tribble Russell, gained global traction via the hashtag and found support from prominent film figures including Guillermo del Toro, Neil Gaiman, Lloyd Kaufman and Mark Kermode. King would also chart the history of the film with his article [https://outofdaveshead.com/2016/04/29/what-the-devils-is-wrong-with-warner-bros/ What The Devils is Wrong with Warner Bros.?]

Despite the controversy around the film and its lack of an official rerelease by Warner Bros., one of the nuns makes a cameo appearance in ''[[Space Jam: A New Legacy]]''.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.cracked.com/article_30771_space-jam-2s-hands-down-most-messed-up-cameo.html|title='Space Jam 2's Hands Down Most Messed up Cameo|date=18 July 2021|access-date=7 May 2026}}</ref>
