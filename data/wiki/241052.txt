'''''The Man Who Wasn't There''''' is a 2001 [[neo-noir]] [[crime film]] written, directed, produced and co-edited by [[Coen brothers|Joel and Ethan Coen]]. It stars [[Billy Bob Thornton]], [[Frances McDormand]], [[Michael Badalucco]], [[Richard Jenkins]], [[Scarlett Johansson]], [[Jon Polito]], [[Tony Shalhoub]], and [[James Gandolfini]]. The film is set in 1949 and tells the story of Ed Crane, a withdrawn barber who leads an ordinary life in a small [[California]] town with his wife, who he suspects is having an affair with her boss. Crane's situation changes when a stranger comes to the barbershop and offers him the opportunity to join him as a partner in a promising new business, in exchange for an investment of ten thousand dollars. Drawn to the idea, Crane plans to blackmail his wife's lover for the money.

The film is in [[black-and-white]] and employs voiceover narration, honoring classic [[film noir]]. It differs by including [[classical music]], setting the plot in a small town, and featuring a protagonist from outside the criminal underworld.<ref name="robson" /> The Coens began developing the idea from a 1940s haircut poster they saw while filming ''[[The Hudsucker Proxy]]''.<ref name="wsws" /> The plot was influenced by [[James M. Cain]]'s crime novels, primarily ''[[Double Indemnity (novel)|Double Indemnity]]'', ''[[The Postman Always Rings Twice (novel)|The Postman Always Rings Twice]]'', and ''[[Mildred Pierce]]''.<ref name="OrrChristopher2014" /> Aesthetically, ''The Man Who Wasn't There'' was inspired by films from the 1940s and 1950s—including ''[[Shadow of a Doubt]]''—along with science fiction films and documentaries of the period.<ref name="AdamsJeffrey2015" />

The film premiered and participated in the official selection at the [[2001 Cannes Film Festival]], where Joel Coen won the award for  [[Best Director Award (Cannes Film Festival)|best director]].<ref>{{cite web |last1=Elley |first1=Derek |last2=Stratton |first2=David |date=May 20, 2001 |title=Cannes finds "Room" at top |url=https://variety.com/2001/film/awards/cannes-finds-room-at-top-1117799673/ |website=Variety |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> Its theatrical response was lukewarm, although it was well-received by film critics, who praised Roger Deakins' cinematography and the performances, especially Thornton's.<ref>{{Cite Rotten Tomatoes |id=man_who_wasnt_there |type=movie |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> Media critics in ''[[The Guardian]]'', the [[BBC]], and ''[[The Austin Chronicle]]'' referred to it as one of the best films of the year.<ref name="bradshaw" /><ref name="bbc" /><ref name="auschron" /> The [[National Board of Review]] included it among its [[National Board of Review: Top Ten Films|top ten films of the year]] and awarded Thornton [[National Board of Review Award for Best Actor|best actor]].<ref>{{cite web |author=<!-- not stated --> |title=2001 Award Winners
 |url=https://nationalboardofreview.org/award-years/2001/ |website=National Board of Review |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> Deakins received a nomination for the [[Academy Award for Best Cinematography]] and the film achieved multiple nominations and awards from other organizations.<ref>{{cite web |author=<!-- not stated --> |title=Awards |website=IMDb |url=https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0243133/awards/ |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref>

==Plot==
In 1949 [[Santa Rosa, California]], Ed Crane is a quiet barber working in his brother-in-law Frank’s barbershop. His wife Doris, a bookkeeper, struggles with a drinking problem, and their marriage is strained. One day, a customer named Creighton Tolliver tells Ed about an investment opportunity in a new technology called [[Dry cleaning|dry cleaning]]. Tolliver persuades Ed to invest $10,000, but Ed, desperate for money, decides to blackmail Doris's boss, "Big Dave" Brewster, whom he suspects of having an affair with her. Ed anonymously demands money from Brewster, who embezzles funds from his department store to meet the blackmail demands.

However, Brewster soon uncovers the scheme and confronts Ed. After Tolliver implicates Ed in the plan, Brewster beats him to death. In a desperate attempt to protect himself, Ed fatally stabs Brewster with a cigar knife in self-defense. Despite this, the police discover discrepancies in the store’s financial records and arrest Doris, suspecting her of both embezzling the money and murdering Brewster.

Ed hires Freddy Riedenschneider, a [[Sacramento, California|Sacramento]] [[Criminal defense lawyer|defense attorney]], who arrives in town and immediately starts living lavishly on the defense fund Doris’s family raised by mortgaging the barbershop. On the day of Doris’s trial, she is found dead, having hanged herself in her jail cell. It is later revealed that Doris was pregnant when she died, though she and Ed had not been intimate for years. Ed’s world unravels further as Frank, now deeply in debt and consumed by grief, turns to alcohol.

Amid the chaos, Ed begins spending time with Rachel "Birdy" Abundas, a teenage girl and friend of the family, listening to her play the piano. Ed fantasizes about launching her musical career and becoming her manager, but his dreams are crushed when a music teacher bluntly informs him that Birdy has no talent. On the way home, Birdy makes an overt sexual advance toward Ed, causing him to lose control of his car and crash.

Ed wakes up in the hospital to find himself arrested for murder. Tolliver’s body, beaten and found with Ed’s investment contract, leads the police to believe that Ed coerced Doris into embezzling the money and murdered Tolliver when he discovered the scheme. With no resources left, Ed mortgages his house to hire Riedenschneider for his defense. However, during Riedenschneider’s opening statement, Frank attacks Ed in a fit of rage, and the judge declares a mistrial. With his defense in shambles, Ed throws himself at the mercy of the court, but the judge sentences him to death.

While awaiting execution on death row, Ed writes his life story to sell to a pulp magazine. One night, he sees a [[Unidentified flying object|UFO]] outside the prison, which he walks away from. As Ed is led to the [[electric chair]], he reflects on his life and decisions, finding peace with his past. He regrets nothing and holds hope that, in the afterlife, he and Doris will be free from the imperfections of the mortal world.

==Background==
The idea of writing a film starring a barber was inspired by a poster that the [[Coen brothers]] saw while filming ''[[The Hudsucker Proxy]]'' (1994). "We filmed a scene in a barbershop, and there was a poster on the wall showing all the different 1940s-style haircuts," recalled Joel Coen. "It was a fixture on the set, and we were always looking at it. So we started thinking about the guy who actually did the haircuts, and the story began to take shape. It really evolved from that haircut poster."<ref name="wsws" >{{cite web |url=https://www.wsws.org/en/articles/2001/11/man-n13.html |title=The Barber of Santa Rosa|date=November 13, 2001 |first=David |last=Walsh |website=[[World Socialist Web Site]] |access-date=January 17, 2022}}</ref> The directors took the title of the film from the 1899 poem written by [[William Hughes Mearns]] entitled "[[Antigonish (poem)|Antigonish]]" and they chose it in reference to the character of the barber, to reflect his emotional emptiness.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://lwlies.com/articles/the-man-who-wasnt-there-underrated-coen-brothers-movie/|title=Is this the Coen brothers' most underrated movie?|date=February 18, 2017|first=Dan|last=Einav|website=[[Little White Lies (magazine)|Little White Lies]]|access-date=January 17, 2022}}</ref>{{sfn|Allen|2006|p=152}}

{{Poem quote|
Yesterday, upon the stair,
I met a man who wasn't there!
He wasn't there again today,
I wish, I wish he'd go away!
|source=First lines of the poem "Antigonish" (1899) by William Hughes Mearns}}

Set in 1949, the plot—according to Joel Coen—"is heavily influenced" by the work of writer [[James M. Cain]], in particular, the novels ''[[Double Indemnity (novel)|Double Indemnity]]'', ''[[The Postman Always Rings Twice (novel)|The Postman Always Rings Twice]]'', and ''[[Mildred Pierce]]''.<ref name="OrrChristopher2014">{{cite web |url=https://www.theatlantic.com/entertainment/archive/2014/09/30-years-of-coens-the-man-who-wasnt-there/380377/|url-access=limited |title=30 Years of Coens: ''The Man Who Wasn't There'' |date=September 18, 2014 |first=Christopher |last=Orr |author-link=Christopher Orr (film critic) |website=[[The Atlantic]] |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171122215705/https://www.theatlantic.com/entertainment/archive/2014/09/30-years-of-coens-the-man-who-wasnt-there/380377/ |archive-date=November 22, 2017 |access-date=January 17, 2022}}</ref> Despite being a neo-noir story, The Coens replaced the underworld setting with a typical American town with ordinary people. "The criminal element here is sort of inadvertent. The hero sort of stumbles into it," Ethan Coen explained. This storytelling device was an homage to Cain, whose stories usually featured characters with conventional jobs who, motivated by need or greed, ended up involved in a crime. Other conventions were present in ''The Man Who Wasn't There'', such as the plan to get money quickly, sexual misadventures, and coincidences of fate. The failure of a "perfect plan" to raise money appears in ''Double Indemnity'' and ''The Postman Always Rings Twice'', both novels narrated by down-and-out working-class men during the [[Great Depression]].<ref name="robson" >{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=PFs83yglo8YC|access-date=January 17, 2022|title=Coen Brothers – Virgin Film|publisher=[[Random House]]|first=Eddie|last=Robson|author-link=Eddie Robson|year=2011|page=253|isbn=9780753547700 }}</ref><ref name="AdamsJeffrey2015">{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=GATSBgAAQBAJ|access-date=January 17, 2022|title=The Cinema of the Coen Brothers: Hard-Boiled Entertainments|publisher=[[Columbia University Press]]|first=Jeffrey|last=Adams|year=2015|isbn=9780231850810 }}</ref>

[[File:Joseph Cotten in Shadow of a Doubt trailer.jpg|thumb|One of the film's influences was ''[[Shadow of a Doubt]]'' (1943), also set in Santa Rosa, California.<ref name="AdamsJeffrey2015" />]]

The Coens named ''[[Shadow of a Doubt]]'' as a reference to the film and had in mind the science fiction genre of the 1950s.<ref name="AdamsJeffrey2015" /><ref>{{cite web|url=https://uproxx.com/hitfix/roger-deakins-recalls-the-man-who-wasnt-there-and-film-noir-favorites/|title=Roger Deakins recalls ''The Man Who Wasn't There'' and film noir favorites|date=December 19, 2014 |first=Kristopher |last=Tapley |website=[[Uproxx]] |access-date=January 17, 2022}}</ref> The film's aesthetics were influenced by science fiction cinema and "cheap documentaries of the '50s". Cinematographer Roger Deakins drew inspiration for his work from the 1940s and 1950s films such as ''[[This Gun for Hire]]'', ''[[The Blue Dahlia]]'', ''[[Kiss Me Deadly]]'', and ''[[Touch of Evil]]''.<ref name="AdamsJeffrey2015" /><ref>{{cite web|url=https://observer.com/2001/11/yesterday-he-was-nobody-today-hes-a-wanted-man/|url-access=limited|title=Yesterday He Was Nobody, Today He's a Wanted Man|date=November 5, 2001|first=Andrew|last=Sarris|author-link=Andrew Sarris|website=[[observer.com|The Observer]]|access-date=January 17, 2022}}</ref> Likewise, ''[[The Atlantic]]''{{'s}} [[Christopher Orr (film critic)|Christopher Orr]] compared the voiceover and Crane's reserved character with the character of [[Robert Mitchum]] in the film ''[[Out of the Past]]'' (1947).<ref name="OrrChristopher2014" /> The Coens referred to other works through character names, as in the department store Nirdlinger or Dietrichson —surnames used in ''Double Indemnity''—Riedenschneider —the surname of a character from ''[[The Asphalt Jungle]]'' — or the name of Tolliver's hotel, "The Hobart Arms", an homage to the novel ''[[The Big Sleep]]'' by [[Raymond Chandler]].<ref name="AdamsJeffrey2015" /><ref>{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=R4MlJ8n-B4wC|access-date=January 17, 2022|title=The Brothers Grim: The Films of Ethan and Joel Coen|publisher=[[Scarecrow Press]]|first=Erica|last=Rowell|year=2007|page=303|isbn=9781461664086 }}</ref>

In addition to Cain's novels and film noir, the story borrowed ideas from existentialist literature, such as [[Albert Camus]]'s ''[[The Stranger (Camus novel)|The Stranger]]''. In addition to noting the similarities in both works, authors and critics compared the disconnection with reality and Ed's attitude with that of the protagonist of Camus's novel.<ref name="AdamsJeffrey2015" /><ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.notcoming.com/reviews/themanwhowasntthere/|title=The Man Who Wasn't There|date=November 16, 2010|first=Michael|last=Nordine|website=[[Not Coming to a Theater Near You]]|access-date=January 17, 2022}}</ref> Through his main character, ''The Man Who Wasn't There'' explores the existentialist view of despair, a theme reminiscent of philosopher [[Søren Kierkegaard]].<ref>{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=r4tG17_O8VkC|access-date=January 17, 2022|title=The Philosophy of the Coen Brothers|publisher=[[University Press of Kentucky]]|first=Mark T.|last=Conard|year=2008|page=244|isbn=9780813138695 }}</ref> Authors Jean-Pierre Boulé and Enda McCaffrey compared Crane's attempt to escape from his routine life with [[Jean-Paul Sartre]]'s idea of a "search for transcendence". The film represented the protagonist's relationship with the dry cleaning business, Abundas, and UFOs.<ref>{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=Qk7LRRIygTEC|access-date=January 17, 2022|title=Existentialism and Contemporary Cinema: A Sartrean Perspective|publisher=[[Berghahn Books]]|first1=Jean-Pierre|last1=Boulé|first2=Enda|last2=McCaffrey|year=2011|pages=75–76|isbn=9780857453211 }}</ref> Although ''The Man Who Wasn't There'', cataloged as postmodern, inherited film noir thematic and narrative elements, at the same time it explored concepts of the post [[World War II]] period, portraying America's early postwar years. The story was contextualized by events such as [[Unidentified flying object|UFOs]], references to the [[atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki|bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki]], [[Werner Heisenberg]]'s reflections on humanity, and the idea of succeeding in an age of opportunity. The plot alludes to Heisenberg's [[uncertainty principle]] through the character of defense attorney  Riedenschneider, who refers to that principle more than once.<ref>{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=iwdC-o-W_KIC|access-date=January 17, 2022|title=The Philosophy of Neo-Noir|publisher=[[University Press of Kentucky]]|first=Mark T.|last=Conard|year=2007|isbn=9780813137179 }}</ref>

==Production==
===Development===
The Coen brothers began writing the script in the mid-1990s, after filming ''[[The Big Lebowski]]''. The brothers moved to Ireland — where [[Frances McDormand]] was working — to continue writing.{{sfn|Robson|2007|p=252-253}} The Coens sent the script to producers [[Eric Fellner]] and [[Tim Bevan]] of [[Working Title]]; they intended to start production in 1999, but taking advantage of [[George Clooney]]'s availability, they dedicated themselves to ''[[O Brother, Where Art Thou?]]''{{sfn|Robson|2007|p=256}} Following completion of ''O Brother,'' ''The Man Who Wasn't There'' was financed independently by Working Title, [[Gramercy Pictures]], and [[Good Machine]], raising an approximate budget of $20 million.{{sfn|Nathan|2012|p=60}}

The Coens wrote the characters of Doris and Frank for McDormand and [[Michael Badalucco]], respectively, and cast Billy Bob Thornton, an actor with whom they had not worked before, for the title role.{{sfn|Robson|2007|p=254}}<ref>{{cite web |last=Pulver |first=Andrew |date=October 13, 2001 |title=Pictures that do the talking |url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2001/oct/13/features.weekend |website=The Guardian |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> Thornton accepted the role before reading the script: "I knew that it would be good. There are certain people you know you can't go wrong with."<ref name=cinefile>{{cite web |title=Man Who Wasn't There, The |url=http://www.urbancinefile.com.au/home/view.asp?a=5644&s=Features |website=Urban Cinefile |date=December 27, 2001 |access-date=September 7, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200605094448/http://www.urbancinefile.com.au/home/view.asp?a=5644&s=Features |archive-date=June 5, 2020}}</ref> The Coens had to convince [[James Gandolfini]], who had just finished filming ''[[The Mexican (film)|The Mexican]]'' and was about to return to ''[[The Sopranos]]'', to join the cast as Big Dave Brewster; he finally agreed after reading the script and finding Big Dave "different from anything else I've ever done. He's kind of a big lug, a bit of a loudmouth, and clotheshorse kind of guy."<ref name=cinefile/> Coens regulars [[Jon Polito]] and [[Tony Shalhoub]] soon joined the cast, followed by [[Adam Alexi-Malle]], Katherine Borowitz, [[Richard Jenkins]], and [[Scarlett Johansson]].<ref name=cinefile/>  Jenkins initially declined the casting call because he had been rejected from three previous Coen productions.<ref name=jenkins>{{cite web|access-date=September 7, 2025 |date=August 10, 2010 |first=Andy |last=Seifert |title=Random roles: Richard Jenkins |url=http://www.avclub.com/article/richard-jenkins-44017 |website=The A.V. Club}}<!-- auto-translated from unknown (Italian or Spanish) by Module:CS1 translator --></ref> 

===Filming===
Filming began on June 26, 2000, in California and ended on September 1, after ten weeks.<ref name=prodnotes>{{cite web |date=2001 |title=About the Production |url=http://www.hollywoodjesus.com/man_who_wasnt_2.htm |website=Hollywood Jesus |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210127052833/http://www.hollywoodjesus.com/man_who_wasnt_2.htm |archive-date=January 27, 2021 |access-date=September 23, 2025}}</ref> Deakins' cinematography was simple and traditional. Most of the shots were taken with the camera at eye level, with normal lenses and a long depth of field.<ref name=cinephilia>{{cite news|title="The Man Who Wasn’t There": A Lovely, Artistic Exhibition of the Coens’ Filmmaking Prowess|url=http://www.cinephiliabeyond.org/the-man-who-wasnt-there/ |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151227212320/https://www.cinephiliabeyond.org/the-man-who-wasnt-there/ |archive-date=December 27, 2015 |website=Cinephilia & Beyond |access-date=September 23, 2025}}</ref> Compared to older American film noir, Deakins used a wide range of grays and attempted to create low contrast without many strong shadows, using fewer and larger lights.<ref name=indiewire>{{cite web |title=INTERVIEW: Shooting Coens; D.P. Roger Deakins and "The Man Who Wasn't There" |first=Ryan |last=Mottesheard |url=http://www.indiewire.com/article/interview_shooting_coens_d.p._roger_deakins_and_the_man_who_wasnt_there |website=IndieWire |date=October 30, 2001|access-date=September 23, 2025}}</ref><ref name=prodnotes/> He used contemporary technologies and wanted the film to reflect the era in which it was made, noting that "we're not trying to make an old movie."<ref name=prodnotes/> It was filmed with color 35mm film and converted to black and white during post-production. This procedure was due, in part, to technical reasons since in recent decades the availability of black and white film rolls had become scarce.{{sfn|Allen|2006|p=157}} However, due to contractual and marketing requirements, it was released in color in some countries.<ref>{{cite web |last=Fischer |first=Russ |date=December 4, 2013 |website=/Film |url=http://www.slashfilm.com/see-some-of-the-coen-brothers-the-man-who-wasnt-there-in-color-video/ |title=See Some of the Coen Brothers’ "The Man Who Wasn’t There" in Color [Video] |access-date=September 23, 2025}}</ref> Joel Coen noted that "the film wasn't made to be seen in color" and that in color it would look "horribly out of place" due to grayscale neutralizing colors outside the time frame of the plot.<ref>{{cite AV media |date=2004 |title=The Coen Brothers interview about The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jg27F1iWTt0 |trans-title= |format= |work=Film4 |access-date=September 23, 2025}}</ref> 

{{Blockquote|For a lot of intangible reasons that are not easy to explain, black and white seemed to be appropriate for the plot. It's a movie from a historical period and the black and white helps the feel of that time. It's evocative for a story like this in ways color photography isn't.<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Holben |first=Jay |title=The Root(s) of All Evil |url=https://archive.org/details/sim_american-cinematographer_2001-10_82_10/page/49 |magazine=American Cinematographer |date=October 2001 |volume=82 |issue=10 |page=49}}</ref>|Joel Coen}}

The set and costume design by Dennis Gassner and Mary Zophres, respectively, had to adapt to the absence of color, avoiding distracting high contrasts. Black and white also affected the actors, intensifying some elements: "Just a close-up is very striking because of the shadows and the sense of depth," said McDormand.<ref name=prodnotes/> However, the team did not lose sight of the color version and designed the locations avoiding bright colors and preferring browns and grays.{{sfn|Robson|2007|p=258}} Ed Crane's clothing consisted of sports jackets, [[gabardine]] and [[rayon]] shirts. Typical of those years, McDormand's stockings had no elastic at the top, and — like the extras — she used garters to hold them up. Women's clothing included seamed stockings, girdles, and pointy bras.<ref name=prodnotes/> Defense attorney Freddy Riedenschneider's unusual for the time peaked lapel [[double-breasted suit]] was inspired by [[Salvador Dalí]], according to costume designer Mary Zophres, to suggest opulence.<ref name=prodnotes/> 

To compose his character's look, Thornton looked at images of figures from that decade and borrowed some elements from [[Raymond Burr]] and [[Frank Sinatra]].<ref name=EW>{{cite web |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=http://ew.com/topic/man-who-wasnt-there/2/ |website=Entertainment Weekly |date=October 24, 2001 |access-date=September 23, 2025 |archive-date=April 6,  2020 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200406192046/https://ew.com/topic/man-who-wasnt-there/2/}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |last=Mundhra |first=Smriti |title=Man About Town |url=http://www.ign.com/articles/2001/11/03/man-about-town |website=IGN |date=November 3, 2001 |access-date=September 23, 2025}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last=Wilson |first=George M. |title=Seeing Fictions in Film: The Epistemology of Movies |date=2011 |publisher=OUP Oxford |isbn=978-0199594894 |page=197|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=_r2dEl2tzZYC}}</ref> The actor commented, "Once you get the right look, everything in your attitude changes."<ref name=prodnotes/>  When Ed appears on screen, he is almost always seen smoking a [[Chesterfield (cigarette)|Chesterfield]].<ref name="OrrChristopher2014"/> Professional barbers trained Thornton and Badalucco.<ref name=EW/><ref name=prodnotes/>

====Locations====
[[File:Homes on Michigan Ave., Bungalow Heaven 03.JPG|thumb|The Bungalow Heaven neighborhood located in the city of Pasadena (California) was used to shoot exterior scenes of Ed Crane's house.]]

Filming began in the abandoned [[Lincoln Heights Jail]] in Los Angeles to set up a Santa Rosa city jail cell. It continued in East Los Angeles and the Musso & Frank Grill restaurant on Hollywood Boulevard, where Ed Crane meets his attorney for the first time. Production moved to Thousand Oaks for two days to shoot the country wedding scenes; Michael Badalucco had to practice extensively for the scene where he rides a pig.{{sfn|Robson|2007|p=259}} The production then returned to Los Angeles to film in a Presbyterian church on Wilshire Boulevard and to Downtown Los Angeles, including an apartment complex that was used to make the hotel lobby scenes and an abandoned Bank of America used for the scenes where Ed goes to the bank.{{sfn|Robson|2007|p=259}} The Nirdlingers building, where Doris works as an accountant, was created in an abandoned furniture store located in Glendale,{{sfn|Robson|2007|p=260}} as production designer Dennis Gassner recalled:

{{Blockquote|We had to modernize it in a certain way, keeping what was already in the architecture and making it work for the modern style of 1949. That space was like a quarry where I could extract what I needed. In fact, I used some chrome art deco bulwark designs that had been kept on the walls and various wall moldings.<ref name=prodnotes/>}}

A day of filming took place in the city of [[Orange, California|Orange]], which was used to represent the exteriors of the town of Santa Rosa, where the majority of the film is set. Although only in Orange for one day, the team worked for more than two weeks dressing the streets for the film's 1949 setting: traffic signs were replaced, facades were modified,  and minor street repairs were made.{{sfn|Robson|2007|p=259}} The exterior scenes of Ed Crane's house were filmed in the [[Pasadena]] neighborhood of Bungalow Heaven, a popular and affordable location in the mid-twentieth century.{{sfn|Robson|2007|p=260}} The Coens chose a house with a lower ceiling to achieve the impression of a smaller space and represent the economic situation typical of a barber.{{sfn|Bergan|2016|p=Small Town, Hollywood}} The scenes in the piano teacher's room were also shot in Pasadena, in the Green Hotel building complex. The film was mostly filmed on location, but a few scenes, such as those at the barbershop, were shot at the end of principal photography on a set built by Gassner at [[Paramount Studios]].{{sfn|Robson|2007|p=260}}

===Music===
The film's soundtrack consists of classical music, primarily piano sonatas by Ludwig van Beethoven, interspersed with seven new compositions by Carter Burwell, in his ninth collaboration with the Coens.<ref name=Soundtrack>{{cite web |url=http://www.soundtrack.net/album/the-man-who-wasnt-there/ |title=The Man Who Wasn't There - Soundtrack |first=Dan |last=Goldwasser |access-date=September 7, 2025 |date=October 21, 2001 |website=Soundtrack.net}}</ref> In addition to Beethoven, the soundtrack included a Mozart composition, "[[Sull'aria ... che soave zeffiretto]];" the atypical inclusion of classical music distinguishes the film from others in the noir genre.<ref>{{cite book |last=Hibberd |first=Sarah |date=January 28, 2013 |title=Melodramatic Voices: Understanding Music Drama |url= |location=Farnham |publisher=Ashgate Publishing, Ltd |isbn=9781409494768 }}</ref> Music editor Todd Kasow was tasked with selecting suitable sonatas for the soundtrack while Burwell began work on a "pianocentric" composition. Some of the compositions feature "cyclical" structures that symbolize the situation the protagonist finds himself in and his difficulty in freeing himself from it, but at the same time, the Coens felt it was essential to the character that the music suggests "a vague longing."<ref>{{cite web |last=Burwell |first=Carter |title=Carter's Notes - The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://www.carterburwell.com/projects/MWWT.shtml |website=CarterBurwell.com |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> Journalist Dan Goldwasser described Burwell's work as "dark and solemn" and wrote that the main composition, "The Trial of Ed Crane", has "a kind of romanticism with just a hint of hope".<ref name=Soundtrack />

The Decca Records label released the soundtrack album on October 30, 2001.<ref>{{cite web |author=<!-- not stated -->|title=Carter Burwell – The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://www.discogs.com/release/2571417-Carter-Burwell-The-Man-Who-Wasnt-There |website=Discogs |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref>

==Release==
===Premiere===
[[File:COEN Brothers (cannesPH).jpg|thumb|The Coen Brothers at the film's premiere at the 2001 Cannes Film Festival. Joel Coen shared the festival award for best director with David Lynch.{{efn|The [[Coen brothers]] directed the film together, but [[Directors Guild of America]] rules only allowed for Joel to be credited}}]]

The film premiered at the Cannes Film Festival on May 13, 2001, followed by other European film festivals that year, such as Edinburgh, Flanders, Warsaw, and Vienna. On October 31, 2001, it premiered in Los Angeles and New York, followed by a limited release in American cinemas by [[USA Films]].<ref>{{cite web |last=Habboub |first=Walid |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=http://www.boxofficeprophets.com/tickermaster/listing.cfm?TMID=605:The_Man_Who_Wasn_t_There |website=Box Office Prophets |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> It was the first Coen film to be released in black and white,<ref>{{cite book |last=Rowell |first=Erica |date=2007 |title=The Brothers Grim: The Films of Ethan and Joel Coen |location=Maryland |publisher=Scarecrow Press |isbn=9781461664086}}</ref> something the directors had tried unsuccessfully with ''[[Blood Simple]]'' (1984) and ''The Hudsucker Proxy'' (1994) in the face of the advantages of color distribution.<ref>{{cite book |last=Luhr |first=William |title=The Coen Brothers' Fargo |date=2004 |publisher=[[Cambridge University Press]] |location=Cambridge |isbn=9780521005012 |page=5 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=_cV8OI89FAcC}}</ref>

===Home media===
The first home edition was available on DVD released by [[USA Home Entertainment]] in 2002. DVD extras included commentary by the Coen brothers and Thornton recorded on January 8, 2002, a sixteen-minute making-of, an interview with Deakins, five deleted scenes, a photo gallery, and promotional videos.<ref name=dvdjournal>{{cite web |last=Dorr |first=Gregory P. |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=http://www.dvdjournal.com/reviews/m/manwhowasntthere.shtml |website=The DVD Journal |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20030412152003/http://www.dvdjournal.com/reviews/m/manwhowasntthere.shtml |archive-date=April 12, 2003 |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref><ref name=dvdtalk>{{cite web |last=Bovberg|first=Jason |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://www.dvdtalk.com/reviews/3842/man-who-wasnt-there-the/|website=DVD Talk |date=May 8, 2002|access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> 

In September 2002, a three-disc DVD edition was released in France, with the original, black and white version on the first disc, the color version on the second disc, and a third disc of extras, which included a fifty minute documentary entitled ''The Film Noir Universe''.<ref>{{cite web |title="The Man Who Wasn't There" DVD Comparison Color vs. Black and White |last=Tooze |first=Gary |url=http://www.dvdbeaver.com/film/DVDCompare/manwho.htm |website=DVDBeaver |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |last=Dowell |first=Pat |title="The Man Who Wasn't There" |url=https://www.npr.org/templates/story/story.php?storyId=3874296 |website=NPR |date=August 27, 2004 |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> 

In September 2015, [[Universal Pictures Home Entertainment]] released a Blu-ray edition of the film.<ref>{{cite web |last=Reuben |first=Michael |date=September 11, 2015 |title=The Man Who Wasn't There Blu-ray Review |url=http://www.blu-ray.com/movies/The-Man-Who-Wasnt-There-Blu-ray/91536/|website=Blu-ray.com |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> 

[[The Criterion Collection]] released a 4K Blu-ray edition on February 24th, 2026. It reproduces most of the special features of the 2002 USA Home Entertainment release plus a newly-recorded 40 minute interview between crime novelist [[Megan Abbott]] and the Coen brothers about the film and its influences.

==Reception==
===Box office===
''The Man Who Wasn't There'' grossed $7.5 million in the United States and Canada, and $11.4 million in other territories, for a worldwide total of $18.9 million against its $20 million budget.<ref name="BOM" /> The film was the Coen Brothers' worst-performing film at the box office since ''The Hudsucker Proxy'' (1994).<ref name=doom>{{cite book |last=Doom |first=Ryan P. |title=The Brothers Coen: Unique Characters of Violence |date=2009 |publisher=Bloomsbury Academic |isbn=9780313355981 |page=123 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=7Ou8-IpAHBUC}}</ref> Some speculated that the poor returns were because the film is in black and white.<ref name=doom/> In its opening weekend in the United States, it grossed $664,404 from thirty-nine theaters.<ref name="BOM" />

===Critical response===
{{Rotten Tomatoes prose|81|7.1|159|Stylish but emotionally distant, ''The Man Who Wasn't There'' is a clever tribute to the film noir genre.|ref=yes|access-date=January 17, 2022}} [[Metacritic]], which uses a [[Weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average]], assigned the {{#switch:  | episode = episode | series = series | season = season | film }} a score of 73 out of 100, based on 33 critics, indicating "generally favorable" reviews.<ref name="Metacritic">{{cite web |title=''The Man Who Wasn&#39;t There'' |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-man-who-wasnt-there |website=[[Metacritic]] |publisher=[[Fandom (website)#Fandom, Inc.|Fandom, Inc.]] |access-date= |archive-url= |archive-date= |url-status=}}</ref><ref>{{cite Metacritic|type=movie|id=the-man-who-wasnt-there|title=The Man Who Wasn't There|access-date=January 17, 2022}}</ref>

Many critics praised the film for its technique and performances. [[Richard Schickel]] of [[Time (magazine)|''Time'']] wrote "affectlessness is not a quality much prized in movie protagonists, but Billy Bob Thornton, that splendid actor, does it perfectly as Ed Crane, a taciturn small-town barber, circa 1949."<ref>{{cite web |last=Schickel |first=Richard |date=October 29, 2001 |title=Cinema: The Man Who Wasn’t There |url=https://time.com/archive/6665109/cinema-the-man-who-wasnt-there/ |website=Time |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> [[Emanuel Levy]], of ''[[Screen International|Screen Daily]]'' wrote that Thornton "acquits himself marvellously in a demanding role that mostly calls for reaction rather than action", adding that he comes across as "a tormented and soulful Montgomery Clift."<ref>{{cite web |last=Levy |first=Emanuel |date=May 15, 2001 |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://www.screendaily.com/the-man-who-wasnt-there/405725.article |website=Screen Daily |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> [[Todd McCarthy]] of ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' wrote that Thornton's Ed Crane "sets new standards for opaqueness and passivity."<ref name="mccarthy">{{cite web |last=McCarthy |first=Todd |date=May 13, 2001 |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://variety.com/2001/film/awards/the-man-who-wasn-t-there-2-1200468394/ |website=Variety |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref>

[[Jonathan Rosenbaum]] of the ''[[Chicago Reader]]'' wrote that "Joel and Ethan Coen stay true to their bent for dense heroes and neonoir, and to their unshakable conviction that life usually turns out to be splendidly horrific."<ref name="jro">{{cite web |last=Rosenbaum |first=Jonathan |date=November 1, 2001 |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://jonathanrosenbaum.net/2020/11/the-man-who-wasnt-there/ |website=Chicago Reader |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> [[Roger Ebert]] of the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'' commented that the film "is so assured and perceptive in its style, so loving, so intensely right, that if you can receive on that frequency, [it] is like a voluptuous feast."<ref>{{cite web |last=Ebert |first=Roger |date=November 2, 2001 |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/the-man-who-wasnt-there-2001 |website=[[Chicago Sun-Times]] |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> Similarly, [[Peter Travers]] of ''[[Rolling Stone (magazine)|Rolling Stone]]'' named it one of the best films of the year and expressed that it is "devilishly funny" and highlighted the photography and performances.<ref>{{cite web |last=Travers |first=Peter |date=November 2, 2001 |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://www.rollingstone.com/tv-movies/tv-movie-reviews/the-man-who-wasnt-there-95134/ |website=Rolling Stone |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> Travers included ''The Man Who Wasn't There'' at number eight on his list of the ten best indie films of 2001.<ref>{{cite web |last=Travers |first=Peter |date=December 27, 2001 |title=The Best of 2001 |url=https://www.rollingstone.com/tv-movies/tv-movie-news/the-best-of-2001-176107/ |website=Rolling Stone |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> [[Peter Bradshaw]] of ''The Guardian'' described it as "the best American film of the year"<ref name="bradshaw" >{{cite web |last=Bradshaw |first=Peter |date=October 26, 2001 |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2001/oct/26/coenbrothers |website=The Guardian |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> and [[Philip French]], in another review for ''[[The Guardian]]'', also wrote that it was the best film of the year up to that point.<ref>{{cite web |last=French |first=Philip |date=October 28, 2001 |title=The Coens raise Cain
|url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2001/oct/28/coenbrothers.philipfrench |website=The Guardian |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> Likewise, Marc Savlov of the ''[[Austin Chronicle]]'' called it "the best-looking film of the year" and added that Thornton's performance "is dazzling, a dull diamond in the gutter rough."<ref name="auschron" >{{cite web |last=Savlov |first=Marc |date=November 2, 2001 |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://www.austinchronicle.com/events/film/2001-11-02/the-man-who-wasnt-there/ |website=Austin Chronicle |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref> The [[BBC]] critic Nev Pierce also described it as "one of the best films of the year" and added about its viewing that it was "a unique, peculiar, riveting experience."<ref name="bbc" >{{cite web |last=Pierce |first=Nev |date=October 1, 2001 |title=The Man Who Wasn't There |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/films/2001/10/01/the_man_who_wasnt_there_2001_review.shtml |website=BBC |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref>

Deakins's black-and-white photography was singled out by several critics. The ''[[Chicago Reader]]'' compared the photography to Federico Fellini's ''8½''<ref name="jro" /> and, despite writing a lukewarm review, McCarthy's ''Variety'' review praised Deakins' work, Dennis Gassner's sets, and Mary Zophres' costumes that create a "a superior post-war, small-town period feel."<ref name="mccarthy" />

McCarthy concluded that "the film holds the interest, to be sure, but more due to the sure sense of craft and precise effect that one expects from the Coens than from genuine involvement in the story."<ref name="mccarthy" /> Similarly, [[David Denby]] of ''[[The New Yorker]]'' called it a “dud academic exercise”.<ref>{{cite web |last=Denby |first=David |date=February 18, 2008|url=https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2008/02/25/killing-joke-denby |title=The Coen Brothers' Twists and Turns |website=[[The New Yorker]] |access-date=September 7, 2025 }}</ref> Michael Sragow of ''[[The Baltimore Sun]]'' was also critical, writing that the film "is an intellectualized, aestheticized, altogether hollow period piece. Despite its superb, claustrophobically controlled black-and-white look, it has all the impact of a cap pistol."<ref>{{cite web |last=Sragow |first=Michael |date=November 9, 2001 |title=Too many flaws kill off the excitement |url=http://articles.baltimoresun.com/2001-11-09/features/0111090086_1_coen-jon-polito-brothers |website=The Baltimore Sun |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160806065440/http://articles.baltimoresun.com/2001-11-09/features/0111090086_1_coen-jon-polito-brothers |archive-date=August 6, 2016 |access-date=September 7, 2025}}</ref>
