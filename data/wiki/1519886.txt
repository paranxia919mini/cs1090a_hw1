'''''Sisters''''' (released as '''''Blood Sisters''''' in the United Kingdom) is a 1972 American [[neo-noir]] [[psychological horror film]] directed by [[Brian De Palma]] and starring [[Margot Kidder]], [[Jennifer Salt]], [[Charles Durning]], [[William Finley (actor)|William Finley]], and [[Lisle Wilson]]. It follows Dominique Blanchion, the separated [[conjoined twin]] of a [[French-Canadian]] model, Danielle Breton, who is suspected of having committed a brutal murder witnessed by Grace Collier, a newspaper reporter in [[Staten Island]], New York City.

Co-written by De Palma and Louisa Rose, the screenplay for the film was inspired by the [[Soviet Union|Soviet]] conjoined twins [[Masha and Dasha Krivoshlyapova]] and features narrative and visual references to several films by [[Alfred Hitchcock]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.avclub.com/chris-dumas-un-american-psycho-brian-de-palma-and-the-1798173826|website=[[The A.V. Club]]|title=Un-American Psycho: Brian De Palma And The Political Invisible |last=Semley|first=John|date=August 13, 2012|access-date=July 30, 2018}}</ref> Filmed on location in Staten Island, the film prominently features [[Split screen (video production)|split-screen]] compositions (also present in subsequent De Palma films such as ''[[Carrie (1976 film)|Carrie]]''), and was scored by frequent Hitchcock collaborator [[Bernard Herrmann]].

Released theatrically on April 18, 1973, ''Sisters'' was praised by critics, who noted its adept performances and use of [[homage (arts)|homage]]. It was the first of a series of shocking thrillers for De Palma, and, in the years after its release, became a [[cult following|cult film]].<ref name=afi/>

==Plot==
Advertising manager Philip Woode wins dinner for two at a [[Manhattan]] restaurant on a ''[[Candid Camera]]''-style television show. Danielle Breton, a young [[French-Canadian]] model and aspiring actress who was part of the prank, flirts with Philip and he agrees to take her as his date. After dinner, they retire to her [[Staten Island]] apartment to have sex. The next morning, Danielle tells Philip that Dominique, her [[identical twin]] sister, has come to celebrate their birthday. At her request, he goes to the drug store to refill a prescription and picks up a birthday cake at a bakery on his way back. When he returns, he is stabbed to death by the crazed Dominique. Before he dies, he tries to alert a neighbor in the apartment across the way by writing "help" in his own blood on a window.

The neighbor, a reporter named Grace Collier, calls the police. Meanwhile, Danielle's ex-husband Emil helps her clean up and hide Philip's body by folding it inside a [[sofa bed]]. Grace accompanies the skeptical Detective Kelly and his partner on a search of Danielle's apartment, but Danielle insists that she has been alone since the previous night. Certain that Danielle is hiding the murderer, Grace persuades her editor to let her investigate the story on the basis that the police are ignoring her because Philip was [[African American|black]]. She hires Joseph Larch, a [[private investigator]], to gain access to the apartment. He determines that the couch contains Philip's body. He also finds a thick file from the Loisel Institute on the Blanchion Twins, Canada's first [[conjoined twins]]. Grace's own investigations uncover that the twins were separated only recently, and that Dominique apparently died during the operation.

As Larch pursues the truck that Emil called to haul the couch away, Grace tails Emil and Danielle to a remote mental hospital. When she is caught, Emil convinces the staff that she is a new patient. He sedates and hypnotizes Grace and places Danielle on the bed beside her, who is revealed to be barren due to an incident that Danielle refuses to talk about. Emil promises to "tell her everything": revelations that play out in a [[Black and white|monochrome]] sequence in which Grace, in her drugged state, hallucinates herself to be Dominique.

Danielle and Dominique were orphaned conjoined twins. As adults, the shy, timid Dominique quickly became a burden to the more outgoing Danielle, who was married to Emil. After several sexual encounters, where Dominique is drugged unconscious against her will by Emil at Danielle's request so that the couple are uninterrupted, Danielle becomes pregnant with his child. Dominique snaps after finding out that her sister is pregnant and stabs her in the stomach to kill her [[fetus]]. Emil arranges for a surgeon to separate Dominique from Danielle, the latter of whom is now badly bleeding from the wound that has also left her barren. However, Dominique dies during the procedure, much to Danielle's horror. This causes Danielle to snap and create a [[split personality]] representing her sister. Whenever Danielle has a sexual experience, the violent "Dominique" personality takes over. The pills she took kept Dominique at bay.

Emil kisses Danielle passionately to bring forth "Dominique" so he can question her, but she slashes him in the groin with a scalpel and he bleeds to death. Grace awakens to find the sorrowful Danielle tenderly embracing Emil's bloody body and screams in horror. Detective Kelly arrests Danielle, who denies knowledge of the murders and says that her sister is dead. Kelly interviews Grace, who is still under Emil's hypnotic spell, repeating lines that he fed her to deny there was a murder. However, Larch tracks the sofa to a remote railway station in Canada.

==Analysis==
Scholarly discussion of ''Sisters'' has centered largely on its prevalent theme of [[voyeurism]] as well as a perceived commentary on the [[women's liberation movement]].{{sfn|Wood|2003|pages=134–136}} Film critic and scholar [[Robin Wood (critic)|Robin Wood]] wrote that the film "analyzes the ways in which women are oppressed within patriarchy society on two levels, the professional (Grace) and the psychosexual (Danielle/Dominique)."{{sfn|Wood|2003|p=134}} He adds: "If the monster is defined as that which threatens normality, it follows that the monster of ''Sisters'' is Grace as well as Danielle/Dominique{{en dash}}a point the film acknowledges in a cinematic hallucination/flashback sequence wherein Grace becomes Dominique... Simply, one can define the monster of ''Sisters'' as women's liberation."{{sfn|Wood|2003|p=134}}

The prominent allusions to works by [[Alfred Hitchcock]] have also been noted by critics such as Bruce Kawin, who wrote in 2000: {{blockquote|''Sisters''... makes intelligent reference to ''[[Rope (film)|Rope]]'' (1948), ''[[Rear Window]]'' (1954), ''[[Psycho (1960 film)|Psycho]]'' (1960), and even ''[[The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari]]'' (1920). The film ends with a shot of a detective looking through binoculars at what might be called the scene of the crime, intently but fruitlessly watching a couch that no one will ever incriminate themselves with by picking up. From start to finish, ''Sisters'' is charged with scenes of looking—from seeing a murder through a window to seeing another person's memories in one's own mind.<ref name=kawin/>}}

==Production==
===Development===
[[Brian De Palma]] was inspired to write the screenplay for ''Sisters'' after reading an article in ''[[Life (magazine)|Life]]'' magazine in 1966 about the lives of the [[Soviet Union|Soviet]] conjoined twins [[Masha and Dasha Krivoshlyapova]]: {{blockquote|At the end of the article there was a picture of the two girls sitting on a couch and the caption said that apart from the fact that they were joined at the hip both girls were physiologically normal, but as they were getting older they were developing psychological problems. One of the twins had a very surly, disturbing look on her face and the other looked perfectly healthy and smiling. And this strong visual image started the whole idea off in my mind.{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=3}}}}

The script, which De Palma co-wrote with Louisa Rose, features structural elements inspired by Hitchcock, such as killing off a prominent character early into the film, alternating points of view, and the involvement of a third party observer in solving a crime.{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=3}} In writing the exposition of the film which details the twins' history and institutionalization, De Palma was influenced by [[Roman Polanski]]'s ''[[Rosemary's Baby (film)|Rosemary's Baby]]'' (1968), specifically the scene in which Rosemary is raped and conceives her child.{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=3}}

===Filming===
''Sisters'' was shot over a period of eight weeks in New York City in spring 1972,<ref name=afi/> primarily in the borough of [[Staten Island]].{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=11}} The apartment interiors were filmed on a set, with additional exterior photography of the [[1271 Avenue of the Americas|Time-Life Building]] in [[Manhattan]].{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=11}} The film was shot using [[Mitchell Camera|Mitchell BNC camera]]s with [[Panavision]] lenses.{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=11}} According to De Palma, the film was lit with a "truly classical style", with scenes sometimes taking 45 minutes to set up.{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=11}} Some sequences were shot on [[16 mm film]] by De Palma himself, such as the scene in which Emil Breton ([[William Finley (actor)|William Finley]]) speaks directly to the camera during the finale's hallucination sequence.{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=12}}

===Visual style===
The film uses unusual [[point of view shot]]s and [[Split screen (film)|split screen]] effects to show two events happening simultaneously,<ref name=kawin>{{cite web|last=Kawin|first=Bruce|publisher=[[The Criterion Collection]]|url=https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/90-sisters|title=Sisters|date=October 2, 2000|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110706122324/https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/90-sisters|archive-date=July 6, 2011}}</ref>{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=9}} as well as long [[tracking shot]]s,<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[The Guardian]]|title=Sisters review – Philip French on Brian De Palma's Hitchcock-indebted 1973 classic|url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2014/jun/22/sisters-review-philip-french-brian-de-palma-hitchcock|last=French|first=Philip|date=June 21, 2014|access-date=August 2, 2018}}</ref> some in excess of six minutes in length.{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=7}} The extended tracking shot in Danielle Breton ([[Margot Kidder]])'s apartment following the murder of Phillip Woode ([[Lisle Wilson]]) was influenced by [[Max Ophüls]] and directly references Hitchcock's ''Rope''.{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=7}} The theme of voyeurism is represented in the alternating points-of-view and distortions of perspective within the narrative [[diegesis]]; De Palma commented: "I really got the idea from watching the [[Vietnam War|Vietnam war]] on television {{en dash}} watching a war that nobody really knew about except that we watched it every night on the 7 o'clock news. It was really a very voyeuristic war, and I think it says a lot about the way we perceive things. We are very much controlled by the media which present things to us. And those can be manipulated."{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=10}}

In order to accomplish the image of both twins conjoined onscreen in the film's finale (both played by Kidder), De Palma had Kidder photographed seated in two different positions, and then joined the images together via [[Optical filter|optical editing]].{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=11}}

===Musical score===
While editing the film in post-production, editor Paul Hirsch and De Palma listened to musical scores by [[Bernard Herrmann]] (particularly for ''Psycho'', ''[[Marnie (film)|Marnie]]'', and ''[[Vertigo (film)|Vertigo]]'') and played them along with the film's key scenes.{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=4}} This led to De Palma inquiring about Herrmann composing the film's musical score. At the time Herrmann was semi-retired, but admired the screenplay enough to agree to score the film.{{sfn|Rubinstein|2003|p=4}}

==Release==
''Sisters'' had its world premiere at [[Filmex]] in Los Angeles on November 18, 1972.<ref name=afi/> It was released theatrically in the United States by [[American International Pictures]], opening in Los Angeles on April 18, 1973.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.allmovie.com/movie/sisters-v44919|website=[[AllMovie]]|title=Sisters|access-date=August 1, 2018}}</ref> It would later expand, opening in New York City on September 26, 1973,<ref name=afi/> where it received "rave reviews", and continued to screen into November 9, 1973.<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[Chicago Tribune]]|title=Tower Ticker|first=Aaron|last=Gold|date=November 9, 1973|page=2|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22415080/chicago_tribune/}} {{open access}}</ref> It was also selected for the [[33rd Venice International Film Festival]] in 1975.

===Critical response===
====Contemporaneous====
The film was met with critical praise; [[Roger Ebert]] noted that the film was "made more or less consciously as an homage to Alfred Hitchcock", but said it "has a life of its own" and praised the performances of both Kidder and [[Jennifer Salt]].<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/sisters-1973|newspaper=[[Chicago Sun-Times]]|title=Reviews: Sisters|last=Ebert|first=Roger|author-link=Roger Ebert|date=March 27, 1973|access-date=May 31, 2020|via=[[RogerEbert.com]]}}</ref> [[Vincent Canby]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' called it "a good, substantial horror film" and stated "De Palma reveals himself here to be a first-rate director of more or less conventional material", also noting the film's references to ''[[Repulsion (film)|Repulsion]]'' (1965) and ''Psycho'' (1960).<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1973/09/27/archives/sisters-goes-soft-on-horror.html|newspaper=[[The New York Times]]|last=Canby|first=Vincent|author-link=Vincent Canby|date=September 27, 1973|access-date=October 7, 2012|title=Sisters' Goes Soft on Horror}}</ref> Meanwhile, ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'', while stating it was "a good psychological murder melo-drama", said that "Brian De Palma's direction emphasizes exploitation values which do not fully mask script weakness."<ref>{{cite magazine|url=https://variety.com/review/VE1117794924?refcatid=31|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=December 31, 1973|archive-date=August 14, 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140814035058/https://variety.com/1972/film/reviews/sisters-1200422963/|access-date=October 7, 2012|title=Review: 'Sisters'}}</ref> The ''[[Los Angeles Times]]''{{'}}s [[Kevin Thomas (film critic)|Kevin Thomas]] praised it as a "witty homage to Hitchcock" and a "low budget but high style scare show," as well as praising the performances and musical score.<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[Los Angeles Times]]|page=30|title=A Witty Homage to Hitchcock|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22413735/the_los_angeles_times/|via=Newspapers.com|date=April 18, 1973|last=Thomas|first=Kevin|author-link=Kevin Thomas (film critic)}} {{open access}}</ref> George McKinnon of ''[[The Boston Globe]]'' was less laudatory, writing: "It is difficult to determine what De Palma had in mind in this morbid horror film. Did he intend it all as a [[parody]] or a straightforward ''Psycho''-type movie? ...  If it is to be taken as a tongue-in-cheek romp, it doesn't work and if meant as a horror film it is run-of-the-mill."<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[The Boston Globe]]|last=McKinnon|first=George|page=30|date=April 27, 1973|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22415202/the_boston_globe/|via=Newspapers.com|title='Sisters' at Pi Alley: For real, or a romp?}} {{open access}}</ref>

The film received honors from the U.S. Film Festival in [[Dallas]], Texas, on April 13, 1973.<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[Fort Lauderdale News]]|page=7E|title='Sisters' Honored At Film Festival|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22413925/fort_lauderdale_news/|via=Newspapers.com|date=April 13, 1973|author-link=Kevin Thomas (film critic)}} {{open access}}</ref> Kidder also received an award for Best Actress at the [[Atlanta International Film Festival]].<ref>{{cite news|page=B7|url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/22414047/austin_americanstatesman/|newspaper=[[Austin American-Statesman]]|date=October 11, 1973|title=AIP Wins Film Awards|via=Newspapers.com}} {{open access}}</ref>

====Retrospective====
Critical reassessment of the film in the 21st century has largely been favorable, with critic Robin Wood writing in 2003 that ''Sisters'' was "one of the great American films of the '70s,"{{sfn|Wood|2003|p=134}} while G. Allen Johnson of the ''[[San Francisco Chronicle]]'' considers it a key film in Kidder's career.<ref>{{cite web|newspaper=[[San Francisco Chronicle]]|last=Johnson|first=G. Allen|date=June 27, 2018|title=De Palma's 'Sisters' a key film in Margot Kidder's career|url=https://www.sfgate.com/movies/article/De-Palma-s-Sisters-a-key-film-in-Margot-13029018.php|access-date=August 2, 2018}}</ref> [[Richard Brody]] wrote of the film in ''[[The New Yorker]]'' in 2016: {{blockquote|De Palma weaves his own obsession with movies into the dramatic fabric of ''Sisters'' by means of a scene involving a documentary about the twins that Grace views in the offices of ''Life'' magazine; this film-within-a-film becomes embedded in her unconscious mind and threatens to warp her consciousness as well. Though De Palma's own images can't rival Hitchcock's in shot-by-shot psychological power, the intricate multiple-perspective split-screen sequences of ''Sisters'' offer a dense and elaborate counterpoint that conjures a sense of psychological dislocation and information overload belonging to De Palma's own generation and times.<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[The New Yorker]]|date=May 30, 2016|title=Blood Relatives|url=https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2016/05/30/brian-de-palmas-hitchcock-mashup|author-link=Richard Brody|last=Brody|first=Richard|access-date=July 9, 2018}}</ref>}}

In 2016, [[Justin Chang]] of the ''Los Angeles Times'' ranked the film as De Palma's most underrated of the 1970s, writing that "for all its low-budget creakiness, [it] feels fully formed—from its sly opening bit of misdirection to its adroit use of split-screen to its memorably churning Bernard Herrmann score. De Palma's choice of subject matter couldn't have been more appropriate: With this film he effectively conjoined himself to Hitchcock, announcing himself as a skillful mimic with a mischievous side all his own."<ref>{{cite news|newspaper=[[Los Angeles Times]]|url=https://latimes.com/entertainment/movies/la-et-mn-brian-de-palma-underrated-gems-20160609-snap-story.html|title=Director Brian De Palma's underrated gems, decade by decade|last1=Chang|first1=Justin|last2=Olsen|first2=Mark|date=June 10, 2016|access-date=August 2, 2018}}</ref> {{Rotten Tomatoes prose|86||50|consensus=Clever yet clearly indebted to the masters of the genre, ''Sisters'' offers an early glimpse of De Palma at his stylishly crafty peak.|access-date=October 21, 2025}}<ref>{{cite web |title=Sisters |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/sisters_1973 |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |access-date=October 21, 2025}}</ref> [[Metacritic]], which uses a [[Weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average]], assigned the film a score of 70 out of 100, based on 16 critics, indicating "generally favorable" reviews.<ref name="Metacritic">{{cite web |title=''Sisters'' |url=https://www.metacritic.com/ |website=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=October 21, 2025}}</ref><ref>{{Cite Metacritic |title=Sisters |id=sisters-1973 |type=movie |access-date=October 21, 2025}}</ref>

===Home media===
''Sisters'' was released on VHS and Betamax videocassettes by [[Warner Bros. Discovery Home Entertainment|Warner Home Video]] in the 1980s, and again in 2000 by [[Home Vision|Homevision]]. The film was released on [[DVD]] by [[The Criterion Collection]] on October 3, 2000 in a new widescreen digital transfer.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://criterion.com/films/377-sisters|title=Sisters|publisher=[[The Criterion Collection]]|access-date=March 31, 2011}}</ref> On July 16, 2018, Criterion announced a [[Blu-ray]] release of the film featuring a new [[4K resolution|4K transfer]] which released on October 23, 2018.<ref>{{cite web|website=[[Bloody Disgusting]]|last=Squires|first=John|url=https://bloody-disgusting.com/home-video/3510331/brian-de-palmas-sisters-coming-criterion-collection-new-4k-digital-restoration/|date=July 16, 2018|access-date=August 1, 2018|title=Brian De Palma's 'Sisters' Coming Back to Criterion Collection With New 4K Digital Restoration}}</ref>

The film was remade in 2006 [[Sisters (2006 film)|under the same title]], with [[Lou Doillon]], [[Stephen Rea]], and [[Chloë Sevigny]] in the leading roles.
