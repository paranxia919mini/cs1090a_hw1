'''''The Long Goodbye''''' is a 1973 American [[Satire (film and television)|satirical]] [[neo-noir]] film directed by [[Robert Altman]], adapted by [[Leigh Brackett]] from [[Raymond Chandler]]'s 1953 novel [[The Long Goodbye (novel)|of the same name]]. The film stars [[Elliott Gould]] as [[Philip Marlowe]] along with [[Nina van Pallandt]], [[Sterling Hayden]], [[Mark Rydell]], [[Henry Gibson]], [[David Arkin]], and [[Jim Bouton]], and has an early, uncredited appearance by [[Arnold Schwarzenegger]].

The story's setting was moved from the 1940s to 1970s [[Hollywood, Los Angeles|Hollywood]]. The film has been called "a study of a moral and decent man cast adrift in a selfish, self-obsessed society where lives can be thrown away without a backward glance ... and any notions of friendship and loyalty are meaningless."<ref>{{cite book|last=O'Brien |first=Daniel |title=Robert Altman: Hollywood Survivor |location=London |publisher=B.T. Batsford |year=1995 |page=53 |isbn=9780713474817}}</ref><ref>{{cite book|last=Phillips |first=Gene C. |year=2000 |title=Creatures of Darkness: Raymond Chandler, Detective Fiction and Film Noir |location=Lexington, KY |publisher=The University Press of Kentucky |page=[https://archive.org/details/creaturesofdarkn00phil/page/156/mode/2up?q=%22a+study+of+a+moral+and+decent+man+cast+adrift%22 156], [https://archive.org/details/creaturesofdarkn00phil/page/265/mode/1up?q=%22o%27brien%2C+robert+altman%22 265] |isbn=0813121744}}</ref>

[[United Artists]] released the film on March 7, 1973. It received mixed to positive reviews upon release, but its critical assessment has grown over time.<ref name="RT" /> In 2021, the [[Library of Congress]] selected the film for preservation in the United States [[National Film Registry]] as "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".<ref>{{cite web|url=https://deadline.com/2021/12/national-film-registry-2021-list-star-wars-return-of-the-jedi-fellowship-of-the-ring-sounder-nightmare-on-elm-street-wall-e-1234890666/|title=National Film Registry Adds ''Return Of The Jedi'', ''Fellowship Of The Ring'', ''Strangers On A Train'', ''Sounder'', ''WALL-E'' & More|date=December 14, 2021|first=Nancy|last=Tartaglione|website=[[Deadline Hollywood]]|access-date=December 14, 2021|archive-date=July 2, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220702231423/https://deadline.com/2021/12/national-film-registry-2021-list-star-wars-return-of-the-jedi-fellowship-of-the-ring-sounder-nightmare-on-elm-street-wall-e-1234890666/|url-status=live}}</ref>

== Plot ==
Terry Lennox shows up at the [[Los Angeles]] apartment of his close friend, down-on-his-luck [[Private investigator|private eye]] [[Philip Marlowe]], claiming he is in trouble and asking for a ride to [[Tijuana, Mexico]]. Returning from Tijuana, Marlowe learns that Terry's wife Sylvia was murdered. He is arrested on suspicion of being an accomplice, but soon released after Mexican authorities report that Terry has committed suicide in Mexico and admitted to killing his wife in a suicide note. Marlowe refuses to believe Terry was guilty.

Meanwhile, Eileen Wade hires Marlowe to find her missing husband, macho, alcoholic, and self-destructive novelist Roger Wade. Marlowe learns that the Wades were the Lennoxes' neighbors in [[Malibu, California#Malibu Colony|Malibu Colony]]. He finds Roger at a rehab clinic and brings him home, but before they leave, the clinician, Dr. Verringer, demands payment for his services and vows to see Roger again.

Local gangster Marty Augustine and his henchmen rough Marlowe up at his apartment and demand that he help recover $355,000 that Terry was hired to deliver to Augustine's associates in [[Mexico City]]. Once they leave, Marlowe follows them to the Wades' house, where he sees Eileen and Augustine having a heated conversation. This leads him to conclude that the Wades are connected to the Lennoxes' deaths. He later learns that Roger owes Augustine a significant sum of money.

Marlowe travels to Mexico, but the Mexican police stick to their story. He returns to Malibu, where the Wades are hosting a party. Dr. Verringer crashes the party, once again demanding payment and slapping Roger. Although Roger drunkenly insults him, he pays Dr. Verringer and ends the party. Later that night, he walks into the ocean to commit suicide. Eileen and Marlowe rush into the water after him but cannot save him.

Hoping that Terry is still alive, Marlowe theorizes that Roger was having an affair with Sylvia and killed her to hide his infidelity, forcing Terry to flee the country. He badgers Eileen, who reluctantly confirms his theory. Marlowe asks the police to reopen the Lennox investigation; they dismiss his theory but confirm that Roger met with Sylvia before going to rehab.

Marlowe receives a goodbye letter from "Terry", with a rare [[$5,000 bill]] enclosed. Marlowe meets Augustine in his office, where upon discovering the bill, Augustine grows suspicious, noting that the money Terry was delivering included three such bills. Augustine threatens to cut Marlowe's genitalia off, but Terry's money arrives in the nick of time. Augustine lets Marlowe go and gives him the bill for his troubles. Outside, Marlowe glimpses Eileen driving away. He runs after her, but she does not acknowledge him, and he is hit by a car during the pursuit. Following a brief hospital stay, he finds that Eileen has sold her house and disappeared.

Marlowe returns to Mexico, where upon bribing local officials with the $5,000 bill, he learns that they were paid by a man with another $5,000 bill to fake Terry's suicide. Marlowe tracks Terry down at a Mexican villa. Terry explains that there was in fact an affair, but it was between him and Eileen. Roger discovered the affair and told Sylvia, who confronted Terry. Terry then killed Sylvia during a violent argument and fled. Terry gloats that no one cares about the case anymore since he is [[legally dead]] and Augustine got his money back. Marlowe asks Terry why he was framed as a suspect, but Terry dismissively calls him a "born loser". In response, Marlowe fatally shoots Terry.

As Marlowe walks away, he passes Eileen, who is driving to meet Terry. She is shocked to see him there, but continues on. Having obtained his revenge, Marlowe happily strolls down the road.

==Development==
It took a number of years for a film of ''The Long Goodbye'' to be made, although a television production with [[Dick Powell]] was released in 1954.<ref>{{cite news|title=Television in Review: 'Climax': Raymond Chandler Play Opens C.B.S. Series Teresa Wright and Dick Powell in Lead Roles
|first=Jack |last=Gould |newspaper=The New York Times |date=8 October 1954 |page=34}}</ref> On Chandler's death in 1959, the only of his Marlowe novels that had not been made into films were ''[[The Little Sister]]'', ''The Long Goodbye'', and ''[[Playback (novel)|Playback]]''. ''Playback'' remains unadapted.

In October 1965 it was announced that producers [[Elliott Kastner]] and [[Jerry Gershwin]] held the rights to ''Goodbye'' and would film it the following year in Los Angeles and Mexico.<ref>{{cite news|title=Gene Kelly Readies Musical |last=Martin |first=Betty |newspaper=Los Angeles Times |date=13 October 1965 |page=d12}}</ref> In 1967, producer [[Gabriel Katzka]] had the rights.<ref>{{cite news|title=Bobby Morse to Co-Star|last=Martin |first=Betty |newspaper=Los Angeles Times |date=10 June 1967 |page=b7}}</ref> He wanted to make it as a follow-up to the adaptation of ''[[The Little Sister]]'' (which became ''[[Marlowe (1969 film)|Marlowe]]'').<ref>{{cite news|title=MOVIE CALL SHEET: 'Little Sister' on Schedule
|last=Martin |first=Betty |newspaper=Los Angeles Times |date=12 March 1968 |page=c13}}</ref> [[Stirling Silliphant]], who wrote ''Marlowe'', wrote a script, but the film was not made and MGM let its option lapse.<ref>{{cite news|title=Fade-out on Raymond Chandler |last=Lochte |first=Richard S. |newspaper=Chicago Tribune |date=14 December 1969 |page=60}}</ref> Kastner and producer Jerry Bick bought the rights back and made a production deal with [[United Artists]] to finance a film.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=360}} Chandler's books were selling as well in the early 1970s as they had while he was alive.<ref name="hate"/>

===Leigh Brackett===
The producers commissioned a screenplay from Leigh Brackett, who had been Kastner's client when he was an agent and had written the script for the [[Humphrey Bogart]] version of ''[[The Big Sleep (1946 film)|The Big Sleep]]''. Brackett:
<blockquote>set the deal with United Artists, and they had a commitment for a film with Elliott Gould, so either you take Elliott Gould or you don't make the film. Elliott Gould was not exactly my idea of Philip Marlowe, but anyway there we were. Also, as far as the story was concerned, time had gone by—it was twenty-odd years since the novel was written, and the private eye had become a cliché. It had become funny. You had to watch out what you were doing. If you had Humphrey Bogart at the same age that he was when he did ''The Big Sleep'', he wouldn't do it the same way. Also, we were faced with a technical problem of this enormous book, which was the longest one Chandler ever wrote. It's tremendously involuted and convoluted. If you did it the way he wrote it, you would have a five-hour film.<ref name="leigh"/></blockquote>
[[Brian G. Hutton]], who had made a number of movies for Kastner, was originally attached as director and Brackett says Hutton wanted the script structured so that "the heavy had planned the whole thing from the start" but when writing it she found the idea contrived.<ref name="leigh">{{Cite web |url=http://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?docId=ft0z09n7m0&chunk.id=d0e1020&toc.depth=1&brand=ucpress |title=''Backstory 2: Interviews with Screenwriters of the 1940s and 1950s'' |first=Patrick |last=McGilligan |page=23 |website=UC Press E-Books Collections |access-date=2015-10-31 |archive-date=2016-03-04 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304082933/http://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?docId=ft0z09n7m0&chunk.id=d0e1020&toc.depth=1&brand=ucpress |url-status=live}}</ref>

The script deviates markedly from Chandler's novel; Brackett took many liberties with the story, plot, and characters. In the film, Marlowe kills his best friend, Terry Lennox. Lennox did kill his own wife, because she discovered he was having an affair with Eileen, and he admits this to Marlowe. Neither Sylvia Lennox, her sister, nor her father appears in the film; Roger Wade commits suicide, rather than being murdered; and gangster Marty Augustine and his subplots are additions to the film. Bernie Ohls, Marlowe's sometime friend on the LAPD, is also absent.

The film quotes from the novel when Marlowe, under police interrogation, asks, "Is this where I'm supposed to say, 'What's all this about?' and he [the cop] says, 'Shut up! I ask the questions'?"<ref>{{cite book|last=Chandler |first=Raymond |author-link=Raymond Chandler |title=[[The Long Goodbye (novel)|The Long Goodbye]] |year=1953 |publisher=Houghton Mifflin |page=28}}</ref>

Throughout the film are stylistic nods to the Chandler novels and 1950s American culture. Marlowe drives a 1948 [[Lincoln Continental]] convertible Cabriolet, in contrast with the contemporary cars others drive. Marlowe also [[chain smoking|chain smokes]] in the film, in contrast with a health-conscious California; no one else smokes on screen.

A "making-of" featurette on the DVD is titled "Rip van Marlowe", a reference to the character [[Rip Van Winkle]], to emphasize the contrast between Marlowe's anachronistic 1950s behavior and the film's 1970s setting.<ref name="rip van marlowe">{{cite AV media|title=The Long Goodbye |chapter=Rip van Marlowe |medium=DVD |publisher=MGM Home Entertainment |people=Carson, Greg (Director) |date=2002}}</ref>

===Robert Altman===
The producers offered the script to both [[Howard Hawks]] and [[Peter Bogdanovich]] to direct. Both declined, but Bogdanovich recommended Robert Altman.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=360}} United Artists president David Picker may have picked Gould to play Marlowe as a ploy to get Altman to direct. At the time, Gould was in professional disfavor because of his rumored troubles on the set of ''[[A Glimpse of Tiger]]'', in which he bickered with costar [[Kim Darby]], fought with director [[Anthony Harvey]], and acted erratically. Consequently, he had not worked in nearly two years; nevertheless, Altman convinced Bick that Gould suited the role.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=360}} United Artists had Gould undergo the usual employment medical examination, and a [[psychological examination]] attesting to his mental stability.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=361}} In January 1972 it was announced that Altman and Gould would make the film.<ref>{{cite news|title=Gould, Altman Team for 'Long Goodbye' |last=Haber |first=Joyce |newspaper=Los Angeles Times |date=27 January 1972 |page=f12}}</ref> Altman called it "a satire in melancholy".<ref name="unite">{{cite news|title=Altman, Gould Reunited for 'Goodbye' |last=Warga |first=Wayne |newspaper=Los Angeles Times |date=23 July 1972 |page=u1}}</ref>

Brackett had problems with Chandler's plot, which she felt was "riddled with cliches", and faced the choice of making it a period piece or updating it.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=363}} Altman received a copy of the script while shooting ''[[Images (film)|Images]]'' in [[Ireland]]. He liked the ending because it was so out of character for Marlowe. He agreed to direct as long as the ending was not changed.{{sfn|Thompson|2005|p=75}} Brackett recalled meeting Altman while he was making ''Images'': "We conferred about ten o'clock in the morning and yakked all day, and I went back to the hotel and typed all the notes and went back the next day. In a week we had it all worked out. He was a joy to work with. He had a very keen story mind."<ref name="leigh"/> Altman and Brackett spent a lot of time discussing the plot. Altman wanted Marlowe to be a loser. He even nicknamed Gould's character Rip Van Marlowe, as if he had been asleep for 20 years, awakened, and was wandering around Los Angeles in the early 1970s but "trying to invoke the morals of a previous era".{{sfn|Thompson|2005|p=76}}

Brackett said her first draft was too long, and she shortened it, but the ending was inconclusive.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=363}} She had Marlowe shooting Terry Lennox.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=364}} Altman made several changes to the script, like having Roger Wade commit suicide and having Marty Augustine smash a Coke bottle across his girlfriend's face.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=364}} Altman said, "it was supposed to get the attention of the audience and remind them that, in spite of Marlowe, there is a real world out there, and it is a violent world".{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=365}} "Chandler fans will hate my guts", Altman said. "I don't give a damn."<ref name="hate">{{cite news|title=Goodbye, Mr. Marlowe |last=Michaels |first=Ken |newspaper=Chicago Tribune |date=3 December 1972 |page=88}}</ref>

==Production==
===Casting===
Many of the film's roles were unconventionally cast. [[Jim Bouton]] was not an actor; he was a former [[Major League Baseball]] pitcher and the author of the bestselling book ''[[Ball Four]]''. [[Nina van Pallandt]] was best known at the time as the ex-lover of writer [[Clifford Irving]], who had written a fake autobiography of [[Howard Hughes]] that turned into a major scandal.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.independent.co.uk/news/obituaries/clifford-irving-dead-died-author-literary-hoax-howard-hughes-a8132121.html |archive-url=https://ghostarchive.org/archive/20220618/https://www.independent.co.uk/news/obituaries/clifford-irving-dead-died-author-literary-hoax-howard-hughes-a8132121.html |archive-date=2022-06-18 |url-access=subscription |url-status=live|website=The Independent|title=Clifford Irving: Author whose literary hoax fooled America in the Seventies and inspired a Hollywood film|first=Harrison|last=Smith|date=December 29, 2017|accessdate=December 1, 2021}}</ref> [[Mark Rydell]] had an acting background, but was better known as the director of films like ''[[The Cowboys]]'' and ''[[The Reivers (film)|The Reivers]]''.<ref name=TCM/> [[Henry Gibson]], who was then best known for his work in the comedy ensemble of ''[[Rowan and Martin's Laugh-In]]'', was cast as Dr. Verringer.<ref name=TCM>{{cite web|url=https://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/15871/the-long-goodbye#articles-reviews?articleId=1083595 |website=[[Turner Classic Movies|Turner Classic Movie Database]] |date=April 16, 2015|last=Axmaker |first=Sean |title=The Long Goodbye |access-date=September 4, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150530234404/http://www.tcm.com/tcmdb/title/15871/The-Long-Goodbye/articles.html |archive-date=May 30, 2015 |url-status=dead}}</ref>

In May 1972, it was announced that [[Dan Blocker]] would appear.<ref>{{cite news|title=MOVIE CALL SHEET: Blocker, Gould to Costar |newspaper=Los Angeles Times |date=5 May 1972 |page=g22}}</ref> He was cast as Roger Wade but died before filming started. The film is dedicated to his memory in the closing credits. In June [[Vilmos Zsigmond]] was confirmed as cinematographer.<ref>{{cite news|title=MOVIE CALL SHEET: Redford Picked as 'Gatsby' |last=Murphy |first=Mary |newspaper=Los Angeles Times |date=8 June 1972 |page=h17}}</ref>

Near the end of the film, during a scene in which Marlowe meets with Marty Augustine, Augustine orders everyone to strip and [[Arnold Schwarzenegger]] appears in briefs in a non-speaking role as a thug working for Augustine;<ref>{{Cite web |date=2021-06-17 |title=Robert Altman's 20 best films – ranked! |url=http://www.theguardian.com/film/2021/jun/17/robert-altmans-20-best-films-ranked |access-date=2022-12-26 |website=The Guardian |language=en |archive-date=2022-12-26 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221226231310/https://www.theguardian.com/film/2021/jun/17/robert-altmans-20-best-films-ranked |url-status=live }}</ref> Schwarzenegger did not receive screen credit for this appearance.

===Filming===
Altman did not read all of Chandler's book and instead used ''Raymond Chandler Speaking'', a collection of letters and essays, copies of which he gave the cast and crew, advising them to study the essays.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=364}} The opening scene with Marlowe and his cat came from a story a friend of Altman's told him about his cat only eating one type of cat food. Altman saw it as a comment on friendship.{{sfn|Thompson|2005|p=75}} He decided that the camera should never stop moving and put it on a dolly.{{sfn|Thompson|2005|p=77}} The camera movements counter the characters' actions so that the viewer feels like a voyeur. To compensate for Southern California's harsh light, Altman gave the film a soft pastel look reminiscent of postcards from the 1940s.{{sfn|Thompson|2005|p=77}}

Altman had Gould and Hayden ad lib most of their dialogue because, according to the director, Hayden was drunk and stoned on [[marijuana]] most of the time.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=364}} Altman was reportedly thrilled by Hayden's performance. Altman's home in Malibu Colony was used as Wade's house. "I hope it works", Altman said during filming. "We've got a script but we don't follow it closely."<ref name="hate"/>

As a reference to the American iconography of Chandler's novels, Marlowe wears a tie with [[American flag]]s on it (the tie looks plain red in the movie due to Zsigmond's [[Post-production|post]]-[[Flashing (cinematography)|flashing]]).<ref name="rip van marlowe" />

== Music ==
The soundtrack of ''The Long Goodbye'' features two songs, "[[Hooray for Hollywood]]" and "The Long Goodbye", composed by [[John Williams]] and [[Johnny Mercer]]. It was Altman's idea to have every occurrence of the latter song arranged differently, from hippie chant to supermarket [[Elevator music|muzak]] to radio music, setting the mood for Marlowe's encounters with eccentric Californians while pursuing his case.{{sfn|Thompson|2005|p=80}}

==Release==
''The Long Goodbye'' was previewed at the Tarrytown Conference Center in [[Tarrytown, New York]]. [[Judith Crist]], then the film critic for ''[[New York (magazine)|New York]]'' magazine, hosted the gala.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=365}} The film was not well received by the audience, except for Nina van Pallandt's performance. Altman attended a question-and-answer session afterward, where the mood was "vaguely hostile", reportedly leaving the director "depressed".{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=365}} ''The Long Goodbye'' was not well received by critics during its limited release in Los Angeles, Chicago, Philadelphia, and Miami.<ref name="sleeper" />{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=365}} The New York opening was canceled at the last minute after several advance screenings had already been held for the press. The film was abruptly withdrawn from release with rumors that it would be reedited.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=365}}

Studio executives analyzed the reviews for months, concluding that the reason for the film's failure was the misleading advertising campaign in which it had been promoted as a "detective story". They spent $40,000 on a new release campaign that included a poster by ''[[Mad (magazine)|Mad]]'' magazine artist [[Jack Davis (cartoonist)|Jack Davis]];<ref name="sleeper">{{cite news |last=Gardner |first=Paul |date=November 8, 1973 |title=''Long Goodbye'' Proves a Big Sleeper Here |newspaper=[[The New York Times]] |page=59 |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1973/11/08/archives/long-goodbye-proves-a-big-sleeper-here.html |access-date=4 September 2024}}</ref>{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|p=367}} the new poster contained caricatures and comic book word balloons to underline the film as a satire.<ref name="TCM" /> When the film screened on TV in 1977, ABC cut Marlowe's shooting of Lennox.<ref name="Deeb">{{cite news |last1=Deeb |first1=Gary |date=29 July 1977 |title='Long Goodbye' was a short one |url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/78797188/ |newspaper=Chicago Tribune |page=II-12 |via=Newspapers.com}}</ref>

MGM Home Entertainment released the film on DVD on September 17, 2002.<ref>{{cite magazine|last=Rivero |first=Enrique |date=May 23, 2002 |title=MGM Preserves Zsigmond's Vision With 'Goodbye |url=http://www.hive4media.com/news/html/product_article.cfm?article_id=3170 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20020604225436/http://www.hive4media.com/news/html/product_article.cfm?article_id=3170 |archive-date=June 4, 2002 |access-date=September 10, 2019 |magazine=Video Store Magazine |via=hive4media.com}}</ref> [[Varèse Sarabande]] released selections from Williams's score on a CD in 2004 paired with the album rerecording of Williams's music from ''[[Fitzwilly]]''; in 2015 Quartet Records issued a CD entirely devoted to ''The Long Goodbye''.

==Reception==
===Critical response===
Contemporaneous reviews complained of Altman's handling of the noir genre and Gould's portrayal of Marlowe. ''[[Time (magazine)|Time]]'' magazine's [[Jay Cocks]] wrote, "Altman's lazy, haphazard putdown is without affection or understanding, a nose-thumb not only at the idea of Philip Marlowe but at the genre that his tough-guy-soft-heart character epitomized. It is a curious spectacle to see Altman mocking a level of achievement to which, at his best, he could only aspire".<ref name="cocks">{{cite magazine | last = Cocks | first = Jay |author-link=Jay Cocks | title = A Curious Spectacle | magazine = [[Time (magazine)|Time]] | date = April 9, 1973 | url = http://www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,903961,00.html | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20050113135708/http://www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,903961,00.html | url-status = dead | archive-date = January 13, 2005 | access-date = 2010-01-03 }}</ref> [[Charles Champlin]] of the ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' found the film "quite sleek, marvelously and inventively photographed ... The problem is that the Altman-Brackett Marlowe, played by Elliott Gould, is an untidy, unshaven, semiliterate, dim-wit slob who could not locate a missing skyscraper and would be refused service at a hot dog stand. He is not Chandler's Marlowe, or mine, and I can't find him interesting, sympathetic or amusing, and I can't be sure who will."<ref>{{cite news |last=Champlin |first=Charles |author-link=Charles Champlin |date=March 8, 1973 |title=A Private Eye's Honor, Blackened |newspaper=[[Los Angeles Times]] |at=Part IV, p. 1}}</ref> Gary Arnold of ''[[The Washington Post]]'' wrote that the film "is not a movie version of the Chandler mystery that anyone with a liking for Chandler could possibly enjoy ... If you are not prepared to shuffle along from scene to pointless, protracted scene with klutzy old Elliott, there will be little to occupy your time or offset your annoyance."<ref>{{cite news|last=Arnold |first=Gary |date=March 22, 1973 |title=The Long Goodbye |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |page=D19}}</ref>

[[Roger Ebert]] gave the film three out of four stars and praised Gould's performance, "particularly the virtuoso ten-minute stretch at the beginning of the movie when he goes out to buy food for his cat. Gould has enough of the paranoid in his acting style to really put over Altman's revised view of the private eye".<ref name= "ebert">{{cite news | last = Ebert | first = Roger |author-link=Roger Ebert | title = The Long Goodbye |work = [[Chicago Sun-Times]] | date = March 7, 1973 | url = https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/the-long-goodbye-1973 | access-date = 2010-01-03 | archive-date = 2013-03-30 | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20130330210342/http://rogerebert.suntimes.com/apps/pbcs.dll/article?AID=%2F19730307%2FREVIEWS%2F301010320%2F1023 | url-status = live }}</ref> [[Gene Siskel]] also liked the film and gave it three-and-a-half out of four stars, calling it "a most satisfying motion picture" with Gould displaying "surprising finesse and reserve" in his performance, though he faulted the "convoluted and too quickly resolved plot".<ref>{{cite news|last=Siskel |first=Gene |author-link=Gene Siskel |date=March 27, 1973 |title=The Long Goodbye |newspaper=[[Chicago Tribune]] |at=Section 2, p. 4}}</ref>

When ''The Long Goodbye'' was rereleased, reviewer [[Vincent Canby]] wrote, "it's an original work, complex without being obscure, visually breathtaking without seeming to be inappropriately fancy".<ref name="canby">{{cite news | last = Canby | first = Vincent |author-link=Vincent Canby |title=For ''The Long Goodbye'' A Warm Hello |newspaper=[[The New York Times]] | date = November 18, 1973 |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1973/11/18/archives/for-the-long-goodbye-a-warm-hello-for-the-long-goodbye-a-warm-hello.html |access-date=4 September 2025}}</ref> [[Pauline Kael]]'s review in ''[[The New Yorker]]'' said the film was "comic and melancholy"<ref>{{Cite book |last=Kael |first=Pauline |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=tkShTL84MrcC&q=the+age+of+movies+selected+writings+of+pauline+kael |title=The Age of Movies: Selected Writings of Pauline Kael: A Library of America Special Publication |date=2011-10-27 |publisher=Library of America |isbn=978-1-59853-171-8 |pages=385 |language=en}}</ref> and called the film "a high-flying rap on Chandler and the movies", hailed Gould's performance as "his best yet", and praised Altman for achieving "a self-mocking fairy-tale poetry".<ref>{{cite magazine|last=Kael |first=Pauline |author-link=Pauline Kael |date=October 22, 1973 |title=The Current Cinema |magazine=[[The New Yorker]] |pages=133–137 |url=https://scrapsfromtheloft.com/movies/the-long-goodbye-pauline-kael/ |access-date=September 4, 2025 |via=Scraps from the Loft}}</ref>

Despite her effusive endorsement and its influence on younger critics, ''The Long Goodbye'' was relatively unpopular and did poorly at the box office in the rest of the U.S. ''The New York Times'' included it on its Ten Best List for film that year and Zsigmond was awarded the [[National Society of Film Critics]]' prize for Best Cinematographer.{{sfn|McGilligan|1989|pp=362, 367}} Ebert later ranked it among his Great Movies collection, writing, "Most of its effect comes from the way it pushes against the genre, and the way Altman undermines the premise of all private eye movies, which is that the hero can walk down mean streets, see clearly, and tell right from wrong".<ref name="ebert b">{{cite news | last = Ebert | first = Roger | title = Great Movies: ''The Long Goodbye'' | work = [[Chicago Sun-Times]] | date = April 23, 2006 | url = https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/great-movie-the-long-goodbye-1973 | access-date = 2010-01-03 | archive-date = 2010-11-21 | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20101121021737/http://rogerebert.suntimes.com/apps/pbcs.dll/article?AID=/20060423/REVIEWS08/604230301 | url-status = live }}</ref>

On [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film has an approval rating of 93% based on 58 reviews. The site's critical consensus reads, "An ice-cold noir that retains Robert Altman's idiosyncratic sensibilities, ''The Long Goodbye'' ranks among the smartest and most satisfying Marlowe mysteries."<ref name="RT">{{cite web |title=The Long Goodbye (1973) |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/1031440-long_goodbye |access-date=September 10, 2021 |work=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |publisher=[[Fandango Media]] |archive-date=November 12, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201112021451/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/1031440-long_Goodbye |url-status=live }}</ref> On [[Metacritic]], the film has a score of 87 out of 100, based on 17 critics, indicating "universal acclaim".<ref>{{cite Metacritic |id=the-long-goodbye |type=movie |access-date=September 4, 2025}}</ref>

==Proposed sequel==
After the film's release, Gould said, "originally, Bob and I had talked about doing a series, another Chandler/Marlowe story every other year, all set in the present", but this never came to pass.<ref name="Zelazny">{{Cite web |author=Zelazny, Jon |date=November 14, 2008 |url=https://cinephiliabeyond.org/long-goodbye-robert-altman-leigh-bracketts-unique-fascinating-take-chandler-film-noir/ |title=Elliott Gould remembers Robert Altman |website=EightMillionStories.com |access-date=November 22, 2025 |via=Cinephilia & Beyond}}</ref><ref name="Busack">{{Cite web |author=von Busack, Richard |date=March 1, 2012 |url=https://www.metrosiliconvalley.com/interview-elliott-gould/ |title=Interview: Elliott Gould |newspaper=[[Metro Silicon Valley]] |access-date=November 22, 2025}}</ref>

The two later envisioned a sequel called ''It's Always Now'' that would center on an old, arthritic Marlowe with Gould set to reprise the role. Though he did not initially specify which Chandler story would be used, Gould said it was "obscure", written before ''[[The Big Sleep]]'', and did not involve Marlowe specifically—though Chandler's estate had granted permission to use the character.<ref>{{cite web|last=Fear|first=David|url=http://newyork.timeout.com/articles/film/4086/ace-in-the-hole|title=Ace in the hole|website=[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out New York]]|issue=576|date=October 12–18, 2006|access-date=March 23, 2024|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090501131335/http://newyork.timeout.com/articles/film/4086/ace-in-the-hole|archive-date=May 1, 2009}}</ref><ref name="Busack"/> He completed a synopsis, and continued to work on it with Altman until the director's death in 2006. [[Alan Rudolph]] then wrote the first draft of a script, and for a time was attached to direct it.<ref name="Zelazny"/><ref>{{Cite web |author=Morgan, Kim |date=January 14, 2019 |url=https://thenewbev.com/blog/2019/01/elliott-gould-the-long-goodbye-interview/ |title=Elliott Gould: The Long Goodbye Interview |publisher=[[New Beverly Cinema]] |access-date=November 22, 2025}}</ref> In 2008, ''[[The New York Times]]'' reported that ''It's Always Now'' was based on Chandler's short story "The Curtain", first published in the September 1936 issue of ''[[Black Mask (magazine)|Black Mask]]'' magazine.<ref>{{Cite web |author=Lim, Dennis |date=August 1, 2008 |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2008/08/01/movies/01goul.html |title=An Angsty Leading Man Who Caught the Spirit of His Times |newspaper=[[The New York Times]] |access-date=November 22, 2025}}</ref> The story features a young boy as the murder suspect.<ref name="Connelly">{{Cite video |author=Connelly, Michael |author-link=Michael Connelly |date=April 6, 2012 |url=https://youtube.com/watch?v=A5mfNmbs7IA |title=THE LONG GOODBYE Elliott Gould discusses the film with Michael Connelly.mov |access-date=November 22, 2025 |time=29:50 |via=[[YouTube]] |quote=[The apartment is] still there, I mean, I still—until I can't—when I'm looking at it, I'm interested... I have the sequel to this, where it's still [set] now. [...] the name of the material—the source material of Raymond Chandler's—is ''The Curtain''. And this was pre-Philip Marlowe, and this is where a ten-year-old is the killer, but now, I mean, I'm... and I've worked with the Chandler estate who gave me the rights to it for a dollar, I don't even think I ever gave them the buck, but, you know, there's no money for it, and it's more than interesting, and it's called ''It's Always Now'', and I understand that apartment is still there and he would still be living in that apartment, [but] he wouldn't have that car anymore. [...] at the end of ''It's Always Now'', after I know that this ten-year-old is the killer and that I can't take him away from his mother—I mean this is the story of a human kind; the child and his mother—and so I'm out and I'm walking now every cat in the community, in the neighborhood, every cat is following this character. We'll see. [...] I would doubt that it's gonna be made, but I never give up, and it's a really good idea.}}</ref>

In a March 2012 interview, Gould described his continued efforts to realize the film, saying, "I'm still trying to make the sequel to ''The Long Goodbye'' but I can't find a nickel to make it!" He added, "I'd like to do it in a way we've never seen the character of Marlowe before. The values don't age."<ref name="Busack"/> During a ''Long Goodbye'' post-screening Q&A the next month, he said the location of Marlowe's [[Hollywood Heights, Los Angeles|Hollywood Heights]] apartment still existed and could appear in the sequel, but said he doubted it would be made.<ref name="Connelly"/> By 2019, Gould had asked [[Steven Soderbergh]] to direct the sequel.<ref>{{cite web |last1=Lattanzio |first1=Ryan |title=Elliott Gould Texted Steven Soderbergh About Making the Sequel to 'The Long Goodbye' |url=https://www.indiewire.com/features/general/elliott-gould-steven-soderbergh-long-goodbye-sequel-1202177390/ |website=[[IndieWire]] |access-date=4 September 2025 |date=2019-09-29}}</ref>
