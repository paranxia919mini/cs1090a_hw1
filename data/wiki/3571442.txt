'''''The Gleaners and I''''' ({{langx|fr|Les glaneurs et la glaneuse}}<!-- , lit. "The gleaners and the female gleaner",{{cn}} --><!-- The film's producers clearly expressed the intended interpretation of the title with its presented English translation; whose interpretation is this, and why is it important enough to appear in the lead's opening? -->) is a 2000 French [[documentary film]] directed by [[Agnès Varda]] that explores the subject of [[gleaning]]—historically, the gathering of farm produce left after formal harvest,<ref name=MWglean>{{Cite web |date=2024-11-18 | author = Eds. Meriiam-Webster  |title=Glean—verb |url=https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/glean |access-date=2024-11-22 |website=www.merriam-webster.com |language=en| quote =  <small>to gather grain or other produce left by reapers ... When it was first used in English in the 14th century, glean carried both the sense of 'to gather grain or other produce left by reapers' and the more figurative meaning of 'to gather information or material bit by bit,' reflecting the slow, gradual, painstaking work of scouring the fields.</small>}}</ref> here including rural-urban reclamation of disposed food and objects.<ref name="Zeitgeist Films" /> ''The Gleaners and I'' was an  "Official Selection" of the [[2000 Cannes Film Festival]], and later went on to win several major film awards.

In a 2014 ''[[Sight & Sound]]'' poll, film critics voted ''The Gleaners and I'' the eighth best documentary film of all time.<ref name="BBC-doc">{{cite web |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/entertainment-arts-28602506 |title=Silent film tops documentary poll |access-date=1 August 2014 |work=BBC News|date=August 2014 }}</ref> In 2016, the film appeared at No. 99 on [[BBC]]'s [[100 Greatest Films of the 21st Century|list of the 100 greatest films of the 21st century]].<ref>{{cite news |url=http://www.bbc.com/culture/story/20160819-the-21st-centurys-100-greatest-films |title= The 21st Century's 100 Greatest Films |work=[[BBC]] |date=23 August 2016}}</ref> The film was included for the first time in 2022 on the critics' poll of ''Sight and Sound''<nowiki/>'s list of the greatest films of all time, at number 67.<ref>{{Cite web |title=The Gleaners and I (2000) |url=https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/8257a6dd-eb35-587a-bb5f-1bdf9dada3c7/the-gleaners-and-i |access-date=2022-12-01 |website=BFI |language=en}}</ref> In 2025, it ranked number 88 on ''[[The New York Times]]''{{'}} list of "The 100 Best Movies of the 21st Century."<ref name="Times">{{cite web | url=https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2025/movies/best-movies-21st-century.html | title=The 100 Best Movies of the 21st Century | website=[[The New York Times]] | date=June 23, 2025 }}</ref>

In 2002, Varda released a follow-up, ''The Gleaners and I: Two Years Later'' ({{langx|fr|Les Glaneurs et la glaneuse... deux ans après}}), in which she revisited some of the people and themes of this film.

==Plot—the subjects==
For the film, Varda traveled throughout rural and urban [[France]] to document various types of [[gleaning|gleaners]] who, whether due to necessity or for artistic or ethical reasons, gather crops left in the field after the harvest or food and objects that have been thrown out. She also included some of the people on the peripheries of the gleaning culture. There are interviews with, among others, a [[Michelin Guide|Michelin]] 2-star chef who gleans and a wealthy restaurant owner whose ancestors were gleaners; the owners of a few [[vineyard]]s, among whom are [[psychoanalyst]] [[Jean Laplanche]] and the great-grandson of [[physiologist]] and [[chronophotographer]] [[Étienne-Jules Marey]]; artists that incorporate recycled materials into their work, including [[Louis Pons]], who explains that junk is a "cluster of possibilities"; lawyers who discuss the French laws regarding gleaning versus abandoned property; and an urban gleaner named Alain, who has a master's degree and teaches French to immigrants. Two of the most notable symbols from the film are the numerous heart-shaped potatoes Varda finds in a field and a clock without hands she finds on the street.

In order to find the subjects, Varda claimed her method was to ask all the people she knew to talk to everyone—"the peasants, the owners, the farmers, the fruit growers—about our film. I said to my assistant, 'Call everybody you know.'" Referring to these subjects, Varda stated that "The more I met them, the more I could see I had nothing to make as a statement. They make the statement; they explain the subject better than anybody."<ref>{{Cite journal|last=Anderson|first=Melissa|date=2001|title=The Modest Gesture of the Filmmaker|url=https://excocinema.files.wordpress.com/2008/06/agnes_verda_interview.pdf|journal=Cineaste|volume=26|pages=25}}</ref>

==Technique==
''Gleaners'' was Varda's first film to use a [[digital camera]] (specifically a Sony TRV900 Mini DV Camcorder.)<ref>{{Cite web|last=Pagliari|first=Valentina|date=February 23, 2021|title=Aesthetics of Waste in Agnés Varda's 'Les Glaneurs et la glaneuse' (The Gleaners and I) (2000)|url=https://movies.mxdwn.com/feature/aesthetics-of-waste-in-agnes-vardas-les-glaneurs-et-la-glaneuse-the-gleaners-and-i-2000/|access-date=April 28, 2021|website=mxdwn.com}}</ref> Varda referred to her filmmaking process as "cinécriture" ("cinema-writing")<ref name="Zeitgeist Films" />, saying that the process of encountering subjects, choosing shots and music, editing, and writing narration is "all chance working with me, all this is the film writing that I often talk about."<ref name="Zeitgeist Films">{{Cite web |title='The Gleaners and I' Press Kit |url=https://zeitgeistfilms.com/media/films_old/gleanersandi/presskit.pdf |access-date=2026-03-26 |website=Zeitgeist Films}}</ref> As such, she said the objects she found, such as the heart-shaped potato, were "[strokes] of luck",<ref name="Zeitgeist Films"/> as were some of the shots. For example, in one scene, Varda forgot to turn off her camera, and as the camera hung at her side, it filmed the shifting ground and the dangling lens cap—footage that Varda chose to present in the finished film (with a jazz music background, as "The Dance of the Lens Cap").

In addition to footage relating to "gleaning", ''Gleaners'' also includes more self-referential footage, such as a scene in which Varda films herself combing her newly discovered gray hair, or the several closeups of her aging hands. In one scene, she "catches" numerous trucks on the freeway, first encircling each truck by putting her hand in front of the camera lens and then closing her hand as she drives past, "just to play."

==Production==
The film was shot between September 1999 and April 2000 in [[Beauce, France|Beauce]], [[Jura (department)|Jura]], [[Provence]], the [[Pyrenees]], and the suburbs of [[Paris]]. In the press kit, Varda wrote that she and her team would travel and shoot for roughly two weeks at a time and immediately proceed to edit while scouting for additional locations. She also traveled alone to get many of her "gleaned" shots and would go to markets between 2 and 4 p.m.

Varda produced the film under Cine-Tamaris, a company she founded in 1954 and that had produced most of her previous films. It was distributed by [[Zeitgeist Films]] in New York, a company that has distributed films from such directors as [[Christopher Nolan]] and the [[Brothers Quay]].<ref name="Zeitgeist Films"/>

==Release & reception==
''The Gleaners and I'' debuted out of competition at the [[2000 Cannes Film Festival]] ("Official Selection 2000"). Later that year, it had its North American premiere at the [[2000 Toronto International Film Festival|Toronto International Film Festival]].<ref>Portuges, Catherine. "The Gleaners and I." American Historical Review 106.1 2001. 305. Web. 17 Nov 2010. History Cooperative Complete. Retrieved at American University.</ref> It was acclaimed by critics, achieving a score of 83/100 on [[Metacritic]]<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-gleaners-i|title=The Gleaners & I Reviews|publisher=Metacritic|access-date=February 2, 2017}}</ref> and a 92% approval rating on [[Rotten Tomatoes]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/gleaners_and_i/|title=The Gleaners and I (2001)|publisher=Rotten Tomatoes|access-date=February 2, 2017}}</ref> Peter Rainer dubbed the film "lyrically ramshackle". Edward Guthmann of the ''[[San Francisco Chronicle]]'' argued that "Varda's subject matter is surprisingly rich, but it's her own energetic, curious nature that gives the film its snap."<ref name="RT Top">{{cite web|url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/the_gleaners_and_i/reviews/?type=top_critics|title=The Gleaners and I Reviews: Top Critics|publisher=Rotten Tomatoes|access-date=February 2, 2017}}</ref> In the ''[[Chicago Tribune]]'', Michael Wilmington wrote: "In its frames, we see [Varda's] empathy, skill, curiosity, wit, poetry and passion for life: everything she has gleaned from a lifetime of love and movies."<ref name="RT Top"/>

In Paris, the film attracted 43,000 movie-goers during "the first nine weeks of its summer release."<ref name="Darke, Chris 2001">Darke, Chris. "Refuseniks (Agnes Varda's DV Documentary, the 'Gleaners and I')." ''SIGHT AND SOUND'' 11.1 (2001): 30-3. Print.</ref> In some places, it was in theaters for over eight months.<ref name="Rich, B. Ruby 2001"/> [[Haden Guest]], the director of the [[Harvard Film Archive]], hailed the film as "one of Varda's most powerful and popular films."<ref name=Guest>Guest, Haden. "Emotion Picture: Agnes Varda's Self-Reflexive the Beaches of Agnes and the Cinema of Generosity." ''Film Comment'' 45.4 (2009). Print.</ref>{{rp|p.47}} Even Varda, herself, remarked at the film's success, saying: "I've never in my entire career felt that people have loved a film of mine as much as this one.”<ref>Darke, Chris. "Refuseniks (Agnes Varda's DV Documentary, the 'Gleaners and I')." ''SIGHT AND SOUND'' 11.1 (2001): 30-3.</ref>

In 2025, it was one of the films voted for the "Readers' Choice" edition of ''[[The New York Times]]''{{'}} list of "The 100 Best Movies of the 21st Century," finishing at number 315.<ref>{{cite web |title=Readers Choose Their Top Movies of the 21st Century |url=https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2025/movies/readers-movies-21st-century.html |website=The New York Times |access-date=2 July 2025}}</ref>

==Cinematic significance==
Ruby Rich believes that the appeal of ''The Gleaners and I'' "is due in considerable part to Agnès Varda’s own presence."<ref>Rich, B. Ruby. "Gleaners Over Gladiators." ''The Nation'' 272.14 (2001): 33.</ref> Guest argues that the ease with which Varda blends documentary and narrative technique is a key reason her films continue to be so relevant, especially "as we witness a resurgence of documentary and a particularly strong interest in hybridized modes of fiction/nonfiction cinema".<ref name=Guest/>{{rp|p.48}} Jake Wilson, on the other hand, conjectures that Varda, perhaps not fully realizing it, tapped into the cultural zeitgeist and constructed a film that "embodies a quasi-anarchist ethos" built on a "resistance to consumerism, a suspicion of authority, and a desire to reconnect politics with everyday life."<ref>{{Cite web |last=Wilson |first=Jake |date=2002-12-01 |title=Trash And Treasure: The Gleaners And I – Senses of Cinema |url=https://www.sensesofcinema.com/2002/feature-articles/gleaners/ |access-date=2026-03-26 |website=Senses of Cinema |language=en-US}}</ref>

In making a film about gleaning, Varda recognized that she, too, was a gleaner. "I'm not poor, I have enough to eat," she said, but she pointed to "another kind of gleaning, which is artistic gleaning. You pick ideas, you pick images, you pick emotions from other people, and then you make it into a film."<ref name=Cinéaste2001>Anderson, M., & A. Varda. "The Modest Gesture of the Filmmaker—An Interview With Agnes Varda." ''Cinéaste'' 26.4 (2001): 24-27.</ref> For her gleaning, Varda chose to use a digital video camera, and there are several scenes in which she shows and discusses the camera itself; in so doing, she transforms a film about waste into a reflexive meditation on the art of digital documentary. While Varda did not pioneer the reflexive documentary—that honor goes to [[Dziga Vertov]] and his 1929 masterpiece ''[[Man with a Movie Camera]]''<ref>Chapman, Jane. ''Issues in Contemporary Documentary''. p. 144 Cambridge: Polity, 2009. Print.</ref>—her work had long been notable for its "reflexive and first-person tendencies."<ref name=Guest/>{{rp|p.44}}

Also notable is the fact that a filmmaker of Varda's stature chose to abandon high-end film equipment for low-end digital video. As she noted in an interview with Melissa Anderson for ''[[Cineaste (magazine)|Cinéaste]]'', "I had the feeling that this is the camera that would bring me back to the early short films I made in 1957 and 1958. I felt free at that time. With the new digital camera, I felt I could film myself, get involved as a filmmaker.”<ref name=Cinéaste2001/> Yet, for Varda, “the first-person, artisan film-making encouraged by digital video [was] nothing new.”<ref name="Darke, Chris 2001"/> While she acknowledged video's convenience, she downplayed any larger significance: "What's missing in all this talk of digital technologies is the understanding that [...] they're not ends in themselves."<ref name="Darke, Chris 2001"/> Digital cameras and editing equipment were simply tools that enabled her "to collapse the time lapse between wanting to film something and actually being able to do it."<ref name="Darke, Chris 2001"/>
