'''Washington, D.C., hardcore''', commonly referred to as '''D.C. hardcore''', sometimes styled in writing as '''harDCore''',{{citation needed|date=May 2023}} is the [[hardcore punk]] scene of [[Washington, D.C.]] Emerging in late 1979, it is considered one of the first and most influential punk [[Punk subculture|scenes]] in the United States.<ref name="SG_13DCHC">{{cite web |last=Norton |first=Justin M. |date=October 17, 2012 |url=http://www.stereogum.com/1179562/13-essential-dc-hardcore-albums/franchises/list/ |title=13 Essential DC Hardcore Albums | website=[[Stereogum]] |access-date=April 11, 2016 }}</ref>

== History ==
Punk in Washington, D.C., found its origins in the district's former centers of [[Counterculture of the 1960s|1960s counterculture]].{{dubious|date=May 2023}} [[Georgetown University]] became a key location due to its heavy student population and student radio station, [[WGTB]]. The station was run under little supervision from the university administration, and therefore became a voice in the early 1970s for cultural radicalism that had faded since the end of the 1960s. [[Dupont Circle]], becoming more known for its diverse and [[LGBT]]-inclusive community, also became important, as well as socially-restless [[Adams Morgan]].<ref name="DanceofDays_pp14-15">{{Cite book|title=Dance of Days: Two Decades of Punk in the Nation's Capital|edition=4th|last1=Andersen|first1=Mark|last2=Jenkins|first2=Mark|date=2009-01-01|publisher=[[Akashic Books]]|pages=14–15|isbn=9781933354996|oclc=437084126}}</ref>

Washington, D.C., first saw touring punk bands such as [[the Ramones]] in 1976. D.C.'s punk {{clarify|text=scene emerged|reason=Did it emerge from mud? Where was it? Was it something in people's heads or was it a thing?|date=May 2023}}{{citation needed|date=May 2023}} that year with groups like [[the Slickee Boys]] and Overkill, who were soon joined by the Look, the Controls, the Razz, White Boy, [[Urban Verbs]], the Shirkers, the Penetrators, [[Tru Fax and the Insaniacs]], and others.<ref name="Andersen-Jenkins">{{Cite book|title=Dance of Days: Two Decades of Punk in the Nation's Capital|edition=4th|last1=Andersen|first1=Mark|last2=Jenkins|first2=Mark|date=2009-01-01|publisher=[[Akashic Books]]|isbn=9781933354996|oclc=437084126}}</ref><ref name=Davis>{{Cite journal|last=Davis|first=John R.|date=2020-06-01|title=I want something new: Limp Records and the birth of DC punk, 1976‐80|url=https://www.ingentaconnect.com/content/10.1386/punk_00030_1|journal=Punk & Post Punk|language=en|volume=9|issue=2|pages=177–204|doi=10.1386/punk_00030_1|s2cid=216239855 |issn=2044-1983|url-access=subscription}}</ref> [[Limp Records]], run by record producer and record store owner [[Skip Groff]], released several of the earliest D.C. punk singles, as well as the compilation, '':30 Over Washington'', all of which helped lay the foundation for the larger scene to come.<ref name=Davis/> Also crucial to the scene was the founding of [[Inner Ear Studios]] by recording engineer [[Don Zientara]]. The studio would soon produce records for both the Look and Urban Verbs.<ref name="DanceofDays_pp14-15" />

The Atlantis, located in the rear room of the Atlantic Building's ground floor at 930 F Street NW, was a short-lived venue, but was significant in the development of the punk scene. The first D.C. venue to host primarily punk and new wave bands, the Atlantis' first punk concert featured the Slickee Boys, Urban Verbs, and White Boy on January 27, 1978.<ref name="DanceofDays_pp14-15"/> By early 1979, the Atlantis had closed, but the space would reopen under new ownership on May 31, 1980, as the Nightclub 9:30—soon known as [[9:30 Club]]—and serve as an important part of the D.C. punk scene's foundation.

Among the earliest Washington, D.C., punk bands formed in the early 1980s were [[Iron Cross (American band)|Iron Cross]], [[Don Fleming (musician)|the Velvet Monkeys]], [[Bad Brains]], [[the Teen Idles]], [[Untouchables (punk band)|the Untouchables]], [[Minor Threat]], [[State of Alert|S.O.A.]] (fronted by [[Henry Rollins]]), [[Chalk Circle (American band)|Chalk Circle]], [[Void (band)|Void]], [[The Faith (American band)|the Faith]], [[Youth Brigade (Washington, D.C. band)|Youth Brigade]], [[Government Issue]], [[Scream (band)|Scream]], and [[Marginal Man]].<ref name="Andersen-Jenkins" /><ref name="SG_13DCHC" /> Bad Brains, who were the first hardcore punk band to form on the east coast of the United States, would influence a wave of new, more aggressive sounding bands in the city.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://homepages.nyu.edu/~cch223/usa/badbrains_main.html |work=homepages.nyu.edu |publisher=[[New York University]] |title=Bad Brains |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090101152139/http://homepages.nyu.edu/~cch223/usa/badbrains_main.html |archive-date=January 1, 2009 |access-date=April 16, 2008 }}</ref> One such band was the Slinkees. Despite the Slinkees only performing live once, three of their members would go on to form the Teen Idles in September 1979. The Teen Idles' 1980 EP ''[[Minor Disturbance]]'' was the earliest non-single release in the D.C. hardcore scene.<ref>{{cite AV media |people= |date= |title=Salad Days |trans-title= |type=Documentary |language=English |url=https://www.amazon.com/Salad-Days-Decade-Washington-1980-90/dp/B078SH4WZ5 |access-date= October 8, 2021}}</ref> The group broke up in November 1980, and band members [[Ian MacKaye]] and [[Jeff Nelson (musician)|Jeff Nelson]] quickly formed Minor Threat, who debuted in December 1980.

Minor Threat profoundly influenced the hardcore punk genre, their contributions to the music, ethics, aesthetics, and ethos widely acknowledged by other hardcore bands.<ref>Robb, John. "Are Minor Threat One of the Most Influential Bands of the Last Thirty Years?" Louder Than War, July 13, 2011, http://louderthanwar.com/are-minor-threat-one-of-the-most-influential-band-for-the-last-thirty-years/.</ref> The band used faster rhythms and more aggressive, less melodic riffs than was common at the time. Minor Threat inspired the [[straight edge]] movement with its song "[[Straight Edge (song)|Straight Edge]]", which spoke out against alcohol, drugs and promiscuity.<ref>{{cite book|last=Cogan|first=Brian|title=The Encyclopedia of Punk|year=2008|publisher=Sterling|isbn=978-1-4027-5960-4|location=New York}}</ref><ref>{{cite book|last=Azerrad|first=Michael|title=Our Band Could Be Your Life: Scenes from the American Indie Underground, 1981–1991|url=https://archive.org/details/ourbandcouldbeyo00mich|url-access=registration|year=2001|publisher=Little, Brown and Company|isbn=0-316-78753-1|location=New York|page=[https://archive.org/details/ourbandcouldbeyo00mich/page/121 121]}}</ref> MacKaye and Nelson ran their own record label, [[Dischord Records]], which released records by D.C. hardcore bands, commonly referred to as harDCore. The ''[[Flex Your Head]]'' compilation, released in January 1982, was a seminal document of the early 1980s D.C. hardcore scene. The record label was run out of the Dischord House, an [[Arlington County, Virginia|Arlington, Virginia]] [[punk house]]. Henry Rollins, who would come to prominence as lead singer of the California-based [[Black Flag (band)|Black Flag]], as well as his own later [[Rollins Band]], grew up in Washington, D.C., and was influenced by the music of Bad Brains and the bands of his childhood friend, Ian MacKaye.<ref>Azerrad, Michael. ''[[Our Band Could Be Your Life|Our Band Could Be Your Life: Scenes from the American Indie Underground, 1981–1991]]''. Little Brown and Company, 2001. {{ISBN|0-316-78753-1}}.</ref>

The tradition of holding all ages shows at small D.I.Y. spaces, has roots in the early Washington, D.C., straight edge movement. It emerged from the idea that people of all ages should have access to music, regardless of if they're old enough to drink alcohol.<ref>Bray, R., &amp; Comaratta, L. (May 19, 2014). All access: An oral history of DC's 9:30 Club. Retrieved March 1, 2021, from https://consequence.net/2014/05/all-access-an-oral-history-of-dcs-930-club/2/</ref>

===Post-hardcore===
{{main|Post-hardcore}}

====Origins and Revolution Summer====
{{main|Revolution Summer (music)}}
[[File:Guitarist Guy Piccioto of Fugazi c1990s.jpg|thumb|[[Guy Picciotto]] was in multiple pioneering post-hardcore bands from D.C. including [[Rites of Spring]] and [[Fugazi]]|150px]]
When [[The Faith (American band)|the Faith]] (with [[Alec MacKaye]]) put out the EP ''[[Subject to Change (EP)|Subject to Change]]'' in 1983, it marked a critical evolution in the sound of D.C. hardcore and punk music in general.<ref name='Subject'>{{cite web | url = http://files.nyu.edu/cch223/public/usa/albums/faith_subjecttochange.html | title = Subject to Change 12" EP | access-date = August 11, 2012 | work = Kill from the Heart | url-status = dead | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20141217021327/https://files.nyu.edu/cch223/public/usa/albums/faith_subjecttochange.html | archive-date = December 17, 2014 }}</ref> [[AllMusic]] writer Steve Huey described their music as "hint[ing] at what was to come, softening the standard-issue hardcore approach somewhat with better-developed melodies and a more inward-looking perspective" <ref>{{cite web|title=All Music Bio the Faith|website=[[AllMusic]]|url=https://www.allmusic.com/artist/the-faith-mn0002284467/biography|accessdate=2 May 2018}}</ref>

Amy Pickering of [[Fire Party]], who worked at Dischord records, proposed a concept to a number of musicians, which would entail a "re-birthing" the D.C. hardcore scene in the mid-1980s. This took shape under the name "[[Revolution Summer (music)|Revolution Summer]]" in the summer of 1985.<ref name="Grubbs">{{cite book |title=POST: A Look at the Influence of Post-Hardcore-1985-2007 |last=Grubbs |first=Eric |year=2008 |publisher=[[iUniverse]], Inc. |pages=22–28 |location=[[Bloomington, Indiana|Bloomington, IN]] |isbn=978-0-595-51835-7 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=Ku91l_blqCUC&pg=PP1 |access-date= March 25, 2011}}</ref> This movement was led by bands associated with Dischord Records. According to the Dischord website: "The violence and nihilism that had become identified with punk rock, largely by the media, had begun to take hold in DC and many of the older punks suddenly found themselves repelled and discouraged by their hometown scene,"<ref name="ROSDischord">{{cite web |url=http://www.dischord.com/band/rites-of-spring |title=Rites of Spring |work=[[Dischord Records]] |access-date=March 19, 2011}}</ref> leading to "a time of redefinition."<ref name="ROSDischord" /> During these years, a new wave of bands started to form, including [[Rites of Spring]], Lunchmeat (later to become [[Soulside]]), [[Gray Matter (band)|Gray Matter]], Mission Impossible (with [[Dave Grohl]] who later joined Scream), [[Dag Nasty]] (formed by [[Brian Baker (musician)|Brian Baker]] of Minor Threat with members of [[Bloody Mannequin Orchestra]] and Shawn Brown later in [[Swiz]]), [[Beefeater (band)|Beefeater]], and [[Embrace (American band)|Embrace]] (with Ian MacKaye and members of the Faith).<ref name="SG_13DCHC" /><ref name='Subject' /><ref name="DischordHist2">{{cite web |url=http://www.dischord.com/history/page02 |title=Dischord History |author=MacKaye | page= 2 |access-date=March 19, 2011}}</ref><ref name='Faith'>{{cite web | url = http://drownedinsound.com/releases/16529/reviews/4143647 | title = Faith Subject to Change and First Demo | access-date = August 11, 2012 | date = September 26, 2011 | work = [[Drowned in Sound]] | archive-date = December 17, 2011 | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20111217171337/http://drownedinsound.com/releases/16529/reviews/4143647 | url-status = dead }}</ref> Rites of Spring has been described as the band that "more than led the change",<ref name="ROSDischord" /> challenging the "macho posturing that had become so prevalent within the punk scene at that point", and "more importantly", defying "musical and stylistic rule".<ref name="ROSDischord" /> Journalist Steve Huey writes that while the band "strayed from hardcore's typically external concerns of the time{{snd}}namely, social and political dissent{{snd}}their musical attack was no less blistering, and in fact a good deal more challenging and nuanced than the average three-chord speed-blur",<ref name="ROSAllmusic">{{cite web |url=http://www.allmusic.com/artist/rites-of-spring-p11541/biography |title= Rites of Spring – Biography|last=Huey|first=Steve |work=AllMusic |access-date=March 19, 2011}}</ref> a sound that, according to Huey, mapped out "a new direction for hardcore that built on the innovations" brought by [[Hüsker Dü]]'s ''[[Zen Arcade]]''.<ref name="ROSAllmusic" /> Other bands have been perceived as taking inspiration from genres such as [[funk]] (as in the case of Beefeater)<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.allmusic.com/album/plays-for-lovers-r320703/review |title= Plays for Lovers – Beefeater – Review |last=Raggett|first=Ned |work=AllMusic |access-date=March 19, 2011 |quote=Drawing from funk as much as punk, Beefeater cooks up a groovy combination on their debut album.}}</ref> and 1960s pop (such as the example of Gray Matter).<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.allmusic.com/artist/gray-matter-p13081/biography |title=Gray Matter – Biography |last=Foster|first=Patrick |work=AllMusic |access-date=March 19, 2011 |quote=The recordings, which revealed the influence of early-D.C. punk ('Gray Matter', 'Caffeine Blues'), also warned about the dangers of punk nostalgia ('Retrospect') and featured a surprising cover of [[the Beatles]]' '[[I Am the Walrus]]' was the first hint of the band's strong pop streak.}}</ref>

According to Eric Grubbs, a nickname was developed for the new sound, with some considering it "post-hardcore", but another name that floated around the scene was "[[emo]]-core".<ref name="Grubbs" /> The latter, mentioned in skateboarding magazine ''[[Thrasher (magazine)|Thrasher]]'', would come up in discussions around the D.C. area.<ref name="Grubbs" /> While some of these bands have been considered contributors to the birth of emo,<ref name="DefinitionAbout">{{cite web |url=http://punkmusic.about.com/od/punktionary/g/posthardcore.htm |title=Post-Hardcore – A Definition |last=Cooper |first=Ryan |work=[[About.com]] |access-date=April 5, 2011 |archive-date=March 4, 2016 |url=http://www.allmusic.com/explore/style/emo-d4525 |title=Explore: Emo |work=AllMusic |access-date=March 19, 2011 |archive-date=October 17, 2010 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101017032450/http://www.allmusic.com/explore/style/emo-d4525 |url-status=dead }}</ref><ref name="UKHC">{{cite web |url=http://www.viceland.com/int/v16n10/htdocs/this-is-ukhc-not-la-202.php |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121019134037/http://www.vice.com/read/this-is-ukhc-not-la-202-v16n10 |url-status=dead |archive-date=October 19, 2012 |title=This is UKHC, Not LA |last=Capper|first=Andy |work=[[Vice (magazine)|Vice]] |access-date=March 20, 2011}}</ref> with Rites of Spring sometimes being named as the first or one of the earliest emo acts,<ref name="treblezine">{{cite web |url=http://www.treblezine.com/features/107.html |title=The 90-Minute Guide – Post-Hardcore |last=Terich|first=Jeff |date=April 24, 2007 |work=Treblezine |access-date=March 20, 2011 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110717094932/http://www.treblezine.com/features/107.html |archive-date=July 17, 2011 }}</ref><ref name="ROSAllmusic" /> musicians such as the band's former frontman [[Guy Picciotto]] and MacKaye himself have voiced their opposition against the term.<ref name="Grubbs" /><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.markprindle.com/picciotto-i.htm |title=Guy Picciotto interview |last=Prindle|first=Mark |year=2003 |work=Markprindle.com |access-date=March 19, 2011 |quote=Well, first of all, I don't recognize that attribution. I've never recognized 'emo' as a genre of music. I always thought it was the most retarded term ever. But honestly, I just thought that all the bands I played in were punk rock bands. The reason I think it's so stupid is that – what, like the [[Bad Brains]] weren't emotional? What – they were robots or something? It just doesn't make any sense to me.}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last=Cogan |first=Brian |title=The Encyclopedia of Punk |year=2008 |publisher=Sterling |isbn=978-1-4027-5960-4 |page= 97}}</ref>

====Subsequent developments====
The second half of the 1980s saw the formation of several bands in D.C., which included [[Shudder to Think]], [[Jawbox]], [[the Nation of Ulysses]] and [[Fugazi]].<ref name="DischordHist2" /> MacKaye described this period as the busiest that the Dischord Records label had ever seen.<ref name="DischordHist2" /> Most of these acts, along with earlier ones, would contribute to the 1989 compilation ''[[State of the Union (album)|State of the Union]]'',<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.dischord.com/release/32 |title=Various Artists – State of the Union |work=[[Dischord Records]] |access-date=March 20, 2011}}</ref> a release that documented the new sound of the late 1980s D.C. punk scene.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.allmusic.com/album/state-of-the-union-dc-benefit-compilation-r23103/review |title=State of the Union: D.C. Benefit Compilation – Various Artists – Review |last=Bregman|first=Adam |work=AllMusic |access-date=March 20, 2011}}</ref> Fugazi gained "an extremely loyal and numerous global following",<ref name="FugaziBio">{{cite web |url=http://www.allmusic.com/artist/fugazi-p4319/biography |title=Fugazi – Biography |last=Kellman|first=Andy |work=AllMusic |access-date=March 20, 2011}}</ref> with reviewer Andy Kellman summarizing the band's influence with the statement: "To many, Fugazi meant as much to them as [[Bob Dylan]] did to their parents."<ref name="FugaziBio" /> The band, which included MacKaye, Picciotto, and former Rites of Spring drummer [[Brendan Canty]] along with bassist [[Joe Lally]], issued in 1989 ''[[13 Songs (Fugazi album)|13 Songs]]'', a compilation of their earlier [[Fugazi (EP)|self-titled]] and ''[[Margin Walker]]'' EPs, which is now considered a landmark album.<ref name="13s">{{cite web |url=http://www.allmusic.com/album/13-songs-r7861/review |title=13 Songs – Fugazi – Review |last=Kellman|first=Andy |work=AllMusic |access-date=March 20, 2011}}</ref> Similarly, the band's debut full-length album, 1990's ''[[Repeater (album)|Repeater]]'', has also been "generally" regarded as a classic.<ref name="FugaziBio" /> Fugazi garnered recognition for their activism, cheaply priced shows and CDs, and their resistance to mainstream outlets.<ref name="FugaziBio" /> On the other hand, Jawbox had been influenced by "the tradition of Chicago's thriving early-'80s scene",<ref name="JawboxBio">{{cite web |url=http://www.allmusic.com/artist/jawbox-p13316/biography |title= Jawbox – Biography |last=Kellman|first=Andy |work=AllMusic |access-date=March 20, 2011}}</ref> while The Nation of Ulysses are "best remembered for lifting the motor-mouthed revolutionary rhetoric of the [[MC5]]" with the incorporation of "elements of [[Rhythm and blues|R&B]] (as filtered through the MC5) and [[Avant-garde jazz|avant jazz]]" combined with "exciting, volatile live gigs", and being the inspiration for "a new crop of bands both locally and abroad".<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.allmusic.com/artist/the-nation-of-ulysses-p13761/biography |title=The Nation of Ulysses |last=Huey|first=Steve |work=AllMusic |access-date=March 20, 2011}}</ref>

== Wider influence ==
[[Dischord Records]], owned and run by [[Jeff Nelson (musician)|Jeff Nelson]] and [[Ian MacKaye]], both formerly of [[Minor Threat]], is responsible for the distribution of a multitude of D.C. hardcore records, both early and current. As a result of Dischord's prominence, very few D.C.-based bands who were not on Dischord have received much attention from outside of the D.C. metro area.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://archive.org/details/ourbandcouldbeyo00mich|title=Our band could be your life : scenes from the American rock underground 1981-1991|last=Azzerad|first=Michael|date=2001-01-01|publisher=Little, Brown|isbn=0316063797|oclc=45804603|url-access=registration}}</ref>
