'''''Lost Highway''''' is a 1997 [[Surrealist cinema|surrealist]] [[neo-noir]] [[Horror film|horror]] film directed by [[David Lynch]], who co-wrote it with [[Barry Gifford]]. The film stars [[Bill Pullman]], [[Patricia Arquette]], and [[Balthazar Getty]], as well as [[Robert Blake (actor)|Robert Blake]], [[Jack Nance]], and [[Richard Pryor]] in all of their final film roles. It follows a man who receives unmarked [[VHS]] tapes showing footage of his home before he is abruptly arrested for his wife's murder, at which point he mysteriously disappears and is replaced by a young man leading a different life.

Financed by French production company [[Ciby 2000]] and Lynch's own Asymmetrical Productions,<ref name="Bifi" /> the film was largely shot in [[Los Angeles]], where Lynch collaborated with cinematographer [[Peter Deming]] and frequent producer and editor [[Mary Sweeney]]. The film's surreal [[narrative structure]] has been likened to a [[Möbius strip]], while Lynch has described it as a "[[Dissociative fugue|psychogenic fugue]]" rather than a conventionally logical story. [[Angelo Badalamenti]] and [[Barry Adamson]] scored the film, while [[Lost Highway (soundtrack)|the soundtrack]], produced by [[Trent Reznor]] of [[Nine Inch Nails]], features songs by [[David Bowie]], [[Lou Reed]], and [[Rammstein]], as well as new recordings from Nine Inch Nails, [[Marilyn Manson (band)|Marilyn Manson]], and [[the Smashing Pumpkins]].

''Lost Highway'' received mixed reviews upon release, with most critics initially dismissing it as incoherent; it has since been reappraised, garnering a [[cult film|cult following]] and scholarly interest. It was a [[Box-office bomb|box office failure]], grossing $3.8 million on a budget of $15 million after a modest three-week run. It is the first of three Lynch films set in [[Los Angeles]], followed by ''[[Mulholland Drive (film)|Mulholland Drive]]'' (2001) and his final film ''[[Inland Empire (film)|Inland Empire]]'' (2006). The film was adapted into [[Lost Highway (opera)|an opera]] by Austrian composer [[Olga Neuwirth]] in 2003.

==Plot==
{{David Lynch sidebar}}
Saxophonist Fred Madison receives a cryptic message on his intercom in the [[Hollywood Hills]]: "Dick Laurent is dead." Moments later, he hears tires screeching and sirens racing past his house. The next morning, his wife Renee finds a [[VHS]] tape on their porch that shows eerie footage of their house. That night, Fred struggles to have sex with Renee and confesses that he had a dream about her being attacked. As he looks at her, he briefly sees the face of a pale old man.

The following day, another tape arrives, this time showing footage of Fred and Renee sleeping in bed. They call the police, but the detectives offer little help. Fred and Renee attend a party hosted by her friend Andy, where Fred encounters a pale man who acts strangely and claims they have met before. The man insists he is currently at Fred's house, which he demonstrates by answering Fred's call to his home phone. Andy identifies the man as an acquaintance of Dick Laurent. Unnerved, Fred leaves the party with Renee.

The next morning, a new tape arrives that shows Fred standing over Renee's dismembered body. Fred is arrested and sentenced to death for her murder. On death row, Fred experiences intense headaches and visions of the pale man and a burning cabin in the desert, before being enveloped in light. The prison guards check on him, only to discover that he has inexplicably been replaced by Pete Dayton, a young auto mechanic from [[Van Nuys]]. Pete, seemingly unaware of how he ended up there, is released to his parents but remains under surveillance by detectives.

Pete returns to work, where his gangster client Mr. Eddy (identified by a detective as Laurent) visits for car repairs. Mr. Eddy takes Pete on a drive, where he and his men violently assault another driver, before offering Pete a pornographic VHS. The next day, Mr. Eddy arrives at the garage with his mistress, Alice Wakefield, who resembles Renee. Later that night, Alice returns alone and seduces Pete, initiating an affair. Alice grows fearful that Mr. Eddy suspects them and devises a plan to rob Andy and flee town. Meanwhile, Pete's girlfriend Sheila discovers the affair and leaves him. Pete later receives a disturbing call from Mr. Eddy and the pale man, which pushes him to agree to Alice's plan.

Pete confronts Andy at his house, but Andy dies after falling headfirst into the corner of a glass table. Pete discovers a photograph of Dick (who looks identical to Mr. Eddy), Andy, Alice, and Renee together, which causes his nose to bleed. Disoriented, he retreats to the bathroom but finds himself wandering the hallways of a hotel instead. Pete and Alice arrive at a remote desert cabin to sell stolen goods. They have sex outside, during which Alice taunts Pete by saying "you'll never have me" before disappearing into the cabin. In a flash of light, Pete transforms back into Fred.

Fred searches the cabin and finds the pale man, who tells him that "Alice" is in fact Renee. He pursues Fred with a video camera, but Fred escapes and finds Dick Laurent having sex with Renee at the Lost Highway Hotel. After Renee leaves, Fred kidnaps Dick and takes him to the desert. The pale man reappears and hands Fred a knife, with which he slits Dick's throat. Dick, confused, asks what they want. The pale man plays a pornographic video made by Dick which features Renee. [[Cutaway (filmmaking)|Cutaways]] show Renee and Dick watching the video, aroused. Back in the desert, the pale man shoots Dick dead and whispers something inaudible to Fred before vanishing, leaving Fred with the pistol.

When police investigate Andy's death, Alice is absent from the photograph Pete found. Fred drives to his home, buzzes the intercom, and says, "Dick Laurent is dead." As the detectives arrive, Fred flees and leads police on a frantic chase through the desert. He screams helplessly amid flashes of light as the scene dissolves to a darkened highway.

==Style and influences==
The film's screenplay refers to it as a "21st-Century [[Film noir|Noir]] Horror".<ref>{{Cite Instagram|user=deboulay.mxf|postid=DK5y5aXxlrH|date=June 14, 2025|title=I absolutely love this film. Ebert did not. 😂 From @rogerebertofficial • Luis Bunuel, the Spanish surrealist, once made a film in which two actresses played the same role interchangeably, in the appropriately titled "That Obscure Object of Desire" (1977). He made absolutely no attempt to explain this oddity. One woman would leave a room and the other would re-enter. And so on.|access-date=July 20, 2025}}</ref>

Although ''Lost Highway'' is generally classified as a [[Surrealist cinema|surrealist]] [[neo-noir]] [[Horror film|horror]] film,<ref>{{cite web | url= https://www.filmaffinity.com/us/film657196.html | title=Lost Highway (1997) - FilmAffinity }}</ref><ref>{{cite web | url= https://www.allmovie.com/movie/lost-highway-am6615 | title=Lost Highway (1997) - David Lynch &#124; Synopsis, Characteristics, Moods, Themes and Related &#124; AllMovie }}</ref><ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.cbr.com/david-lynch-lost-highway-cult-classic-neo-noir-horror/| title=David Lynch's 28-Year-Old Neo-Noir Horror Was a Box Office Disaster (But It Remains a Cult Classic) |author-first1=Kelsey|author-last1=Yoor|website=CBR|publisher=Valnet Publishing Group|date=January 26, 2025 }}</ref><ref name="FilmNoir"/><ref name="Popmatters noir"/><ref name="20th anniversary"/> the film borrows elements from other genres, including [[German expressionist cinema|German Expressionism]] and the [[French New Wave]].<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> The term [[psychological thriller]]<ref name="The Five Best Films"/><ref name="Lost Highway at 20"/> has also been used to describe its narrative elements.<ref name="Metroactive"/><ref>{{cite web |url=https://floodmagazine.com/120765/david-lynch-lost-highway-essay/ |access-date=November 28, 2022 |title=The Sound and Vision of David Lynch's Lost Highway |date=October 31, 2022 |first=Ad |last=Amorosi |website=[[Flood Magazine]]}}</ref> Thomas Caldwell of ''Metro Magazine'' described Fred Madison as "a typical film noir hero, inhabiting a doomed and desolate world characterised by an excess of sexuality, darkness and violence".<ref name="Lost in Darkness"/> Another film noir feature of the film is the ''[[femme fatale]]'' of Alice Wakefield, who misleads Pete Dayton into dangerous situations.<ref name="Variety review"/> The film was also noted for its graphic violence and sexual themes, which Lynch defended by stating that he was simply being honest with his own ideas for the film.<ref name="Lynch and Reznor"/>

Some of the film's elements reference earlier works: the film ''[[Detour (1945 film)|Detour]]'' (1945) also focuses on a disturbed nightclub musician.<ref name="20th anniversary"/> The film's setting and mysterious recorded messages were seen as a reference to the film ''[[Kiss Me Deadly]]'' (1955), while its nightmarish atmosphere has been compared to [[Maya Deren]]'s short film ''[[Meshes of the Afternoon]]'' (1943).<ref name="20th anniversary"/> Like [[Alfred Hitchcock]]'s film ''[[Vertigo (film)|Vertigo]]'' (1958), the film examines male obsessions with women who merely represent emotions that relate to them.<ref name="20th anniversary"/> Sean Murphy of [[PopMatters]] considers the film a possible homage to [[Ambrose Bierce]]'s short story "[[An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge]]" (1890), in which much of the main narrative is imagined by the protagonist as he is executed.<ref name="Lynch Dilemma"/>

==Interpretations==
The film's circular narrative has been likened to a [[Möbius strip]].<ref name="Slate DVD"/><ref name="AV Club cult"/> Lynch described it as a "[[psychogenic fugue]]"<ref name="Slate DVD"/> and insisted that, while the film is about "identity",<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> it is abstract and is left to the audience to interpret.<ref name="Positif interview"/><ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> However, when asked by an interviewer in 1997 whether Pullman's character is "trapped in [a] [[time loop]], doomed to repeat his murders and mistakes forever and ever", Lynch replied, "Well, maybe not forever and ever, but you can see how it would be a struggle. Yeah, that's it."<ref name=road>{{cite news |last1=Wells |first1=Dominic |title=Road to Hell |work=Time Out |date=13 July 1997 |pages=27–28, 30}}</ref> He agreed to a comparison with the [[Buddhist]] conception of [[saṃsāra]], elaborating that "it's a fragment of the story [...] it's not so much a circle as like a spiral that comes around, the next loop a little bit higher than the one that precedes it".<ref name=road/>

Co-writer [[Barry Gifford]], in contrast, endorsed a rational explanation for the film's surreal events. According to him, Fred is experiencing a literal psychogenic fugue, a mental break which is manifested when he transforms into Pete.<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> Arquette described her own interpretation: "To me, it's sort of a movie about looking at women through the eyes of a [[misogynist]]... so he's totally obsessed with her, can't love her enough, can't have her to himself enough, can't kill her enough times."<ref>"Lost Highway: Bill Pullman and Patricia Arquette interview" (1997). ''U TV''.</ref> She later elaborated, "He hates women, he doesn't quite trust [Renee], even though she is his wife. He kills her but can't remember it, then he recreates himself as this virile young man and meets her again. And now, she actually wants to fuck him and she is in love with him. But even in this version, she is a dirty whore. In this man's mind, a woman is always the monster. No matter what."<ref>{{cite web |last1=Balaga |first1=Marta |title=Patricia Arquette Finally, Finally Explains 'Lost Highway': 'In This Man's Mind, a Woman Is Always the Monster' |url=https://variety.com/2024/film/global/patricia-arquette-david-lynch-viggo-mortensen-1235948429/ |website=Variety |date=March 21, 2024 |access-date=22 January 2025}}</ref>

Jeremiah Kipp of ''[[Slant Magazine]]'' called the film "an embodiment of a pensive male anxiety" and ascribed its mixed reception to the fact that "for some cultural reason it's easier for audiences to accept female hysteria than the insecurities of men".<ref name="Slant DVD"/> Kipp noted that most critics interpreted the film as concerning the "distortion and reshaping of memory".<ref name="Slant DVD"/> Philosopher and critic [[Slavoj Žižek]] considered the narrative's circularity as analogous to a [[psychoanalysis|psychoanalytic]] process: "There is a symptomatic key phrase ['Dick Laurent is dead'] (as in all of Lynch's films) that always returns as an insistent, traumatic, and indecipherable message ([[the Real]]), and there is a temporal loop, as with analysis, where the protagonist at first fails to encounter the self, but in the end is able to pronounce the symptom consciously as his own."<ref name="Slavoj Zizek"/> He also interprets the film's bipartite structure as exploiting "the opposition of two horrors: the phantasmatic horror of the nightmarish noir universe of perverse sex, betrayal, and murder, and the (perhaps much more unsettling) despair of our drab, alienated daily life of impotence and distrust".<ref name="Emma Wilson"/>

==Production==
===Development===
[[File:Barry Gifford.jpg|right|thumb|upright|Author [[Barry Gifford]] {{nowrap|co-wrote}} the screenplay with Lynch.]]
''Lost Highway'' was directed by [[David Lynch]] as his first feature film since ''[[Twin Peaks: Fire Walk with Me]]'' (1992), a prequel to his television series ''[[Twin Peaks]]'' (1990–1991; 2017).<ref name="Lynch and Reznor"/> He came across the phrase "lost highway" in the book ''Night People'' (1992) by Barry Gifford.<ref name="Regain the Peak"/> As Lynch knew Gifford well and had previously adapted his novel ''[[Wild at Heart (novel)|Wild at Heart]]'' (1990) into [[Wild at Heart (film)|a film of the same name]],<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> he told Gifford that he loved the phrase as a title for a film, and the two agreed to write a screenplay together.<ref name="Positif interview"/> Each initially had their own ideas of what ''Lost Highway'' should be, but ended up rejecting all of them.<ref name="Positif interview"/> Lynch then told Gifford that, during the last night of shooting ''Fire Walk with Me'', he had a thought about videotapes and a couple in crisis.<ref name="Positif interview"/> This idea would develop into the first part of the film until Fred is put on death row. Lynch and Gifford then realized that a transformation had to occur and another story, which would have several links to the first one while differing from it, developed.<ref name="Positif interview"/> It took them one month to finish the script.<ref name="Positif interview"/>

''Lost Highway'' was partially inspired by the [[murder trial of O. J. Simpson]] and Simpson's ability to return to his normal life afterward.<ref name="AV Club cult"/> The scene where Fred hears the words "Dick Laurent is dead" over his intercom was inspired by a similar incident that happened to Lynch at [[David Lynch Compound|his own house]], which he ended up using as a shooting location for the film.<ref name="Positif interview"/> Unlike the sinister nature of the scene in the film, Lynch assumed the stranger who left a message on his intercom had simply mistaken his house for that of actor [[David Lander]], who lived next door.<ref name="Positif interview"/> Lynch said the idea of the Mystery Man "came out of a feeling of a man who, whether real or not, gave the impression that he was supernatural".<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> The film was financed by the French production company [[Ciby 2000]]<ref name="Premiere article"/> and Lynch's own Asymmetrical Productions.<ref name="Premiere article"/>

===Casting===
Lynch cast his friend and neighbor [[Bill Pullman]] as Fred.<ref name="Toronto Sun"/> [[Patricia Arquette]] agreed to take the dual role of Renee and Alice because she was interested in portraying a sexually desirable and dangerous woman,<ref name="W interview"/> which she had never done before.<ref name="Baker scene"/> She had also been a fan of Lynch for a long time and felt honored to work with him.<ref name="W interview"/><ref name="Baker scene"/> [[Balthazar Getty]] was cast after Lynch saw a picture of him in a magazine and said he was "the guy for the job".<ref name="Room to Dream"/> As the script was open to interpretation, Getty and Arquette did not know what kind of film ''Lost Highway'' was supposed to be, with Getty saying that "part of David's technique is to keep his actors guessing, because it creates a certain atmosphere on set".<ref name="Room to Dream"/>

[[Robert Blake (actor)|Robert Blake]] was cast as The Mystery Man because Lynch liked his previous work and had long been interested in working with him.<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> Blake did not understand the script at all, but was responsible for the look and style of his character, for which Lynch told him to use his imagination; Blake cut his hair short, parted it in the middle, shaved his eyebrows off, applied white ''[[kabuki]]'' make-up on his face, and put on a black outfit. He then approached Lynch, who loved it.<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> [[Robert Loggia]], who had previously expressed interest in portraying [[Frank Booth (Blue Velvet)|Frank Booth]] in Lynch's ''[[Blue Velvet (film)|Blue Velvet]]'', was cast in the dual role of Mr. Eddy and Dick. Mr. Eddy's road rage scene was inspired by an incident in which Loggia, upon learning that he had lost the role in ''Blue Velvet'' to [[Dennis Hopper]], launched a profanity-laden rant at Lynch.<ref name="Guardian Loggia"/> ''Lost Highway'' also features the final film roles of Blake, [[Jack Nance]], and comedian [[Richard Pryor]].<ref name="AV Club cult"/> [[Dennis Woodruff]] claimed that he was cast in a small speaking role as a prison inmate but was cut from the film.<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Rowe |first=Sam |date=April 2016 |title=Meet Hollywood's Greatest Failure |url= |magazine=[[Empire (magazine)]] |location= |publisher=[[Bauer Media Group]] |access-date=}}</ref>

===Filming===
Filming took place largely in [[Los Angeles]].<ref name="Sight Sound article"/> It began on November 29, 1995, and finished on February 22, 1996.<ref name="Premiere article"/> Some of the film's exterior and driving scenes were shot in [[Griffith Park]],<ref name="Premiere article"/> while the scenes of the Lost Highway Hotel were filmed at the [[Amargosa Opera House and Hotel]] in [[Death Valley]].<ref name="Lost Highway Hotel"/> Scenes set in Fred and Renee's mansion were filmed at [[David Lynch Compound|Lynch's own home]] in the Hollywood Hills.<ref name="Lynch and Reznor"/> The house was configured in a particular way to meet the requirements of the film. A corridor that leads to the bedroom was added, and the façade was remodeled with slot windows to make Fred's point of view very limited.<ref name="Positif interview"/> The paintings on the wall above the couch were made by [[Mary Sweeney]], who produced and edited much of Lynch's work (including ''Lost Highway'') and was briefly married to him.<ref name="Premiere article"/>

Scenes involving sex and nudity proved difficult for Arquette, who considered herself modest and shy; she later said she felt protected by Lynch and the film crew, who were ready to give her a bathrobe at any time.<ref name="W interview"/> The sex scene between her and Getty in the desert, which was shot on a dry lake bed 20 miles outside [[Baker, California]],<ref name="Baker scene"/> was a closed set with only key crew members allowed.<ref name="W interview"/> The sequence where Fred transforms into Pete was not [[Computer-generated imagery|CGI]], but rather accomplished with in-camera techniques: a make-up expert constructed a fake head that was covered with artificial brain matter, which was then intercut with shots of Pullman.<ref name="Cinematographer article"/> The final car chase was shot with two different cameras running at different [[frame rate]]s. The footage was then sped up to make the scene more aggressive.<ref name="Cinematographer article"/>

Lynch worked with cinematographer [[Peter Deming]] to give the film a surreal look.<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> Because the script did not include many descriptions, the film's visual approach evolved as shooting progressed.<ref name="Positif interview"/> Deming would occasionally pull out the lenses of his camera to defocus a particular scene,<ref name="Filmmaker interview"/> while Lynch would often listen to music in his headset and to a scene at the same time to visualize the screenplay.<ref name="W interview"/> According to him, "Sound and picture working together is what films are [...] so every single sound has to be supporting that scene and enlarging it. A room is, say, nine by twelve [feet], but when you're introducing sound to it, you can create a space that's giant."<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> The notion of a psychogenic fugue was incorporated into the film after the unit publicist read about it in a book on mental illnesses. Lynch felt it was a musical term, stating that "a [[fugue]] starts off one way, takes up on another direction, and then comes back to the original, so it [relates] to the form of the film".<ref name="Filmmaker interview"/>

Lynch originally wanted to shoot ''Lost Highway'' in [[black and white]], but the idea was discarded due to the financial risks it could cause. The film was instead shot in varying levels of darkness, featuring few daylight scenes.<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> Some sequences became so dark that it was difficult for viewers to see what was happening. According to Deming, "The thing I wanted to achieve was giving the feeling that anything could come out of the background, and to leave a certain question about what you're looking at. The film is working under the surface while you're watching it."<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/>

===Editing===
The film's darkness levels were intentionally left alone during post-production.<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> The first cut of the film ran for 180 minutes, and a [[test audience]] of 50 people was given a preview to allow Lynch an idea of what needed to be cut.<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> The film was ultimately cut down to 130 minutes. Most of the deleted scenes were about Pete's life, including scenes where he visited a [[drive-in]] and a bowling alley with his friends.<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/>

===Soundtrack===
{{Main|Lost Highway (soundtrack)}}
The film's original score was composed by frequent Lynch collaborator [[Angelo Badalamenti]],<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> with additional music by [[Barry Adamson]].<ref name="CoS soundtrack"/> Although most of the score was recorded in [[Prague]], additional compositions were done in [[London]].<ref name="Cinefantastique article"/> In [[New Orleans]], Lynch collaborated with [[Nine Inch Nails]] frontman [[Trent Reznor]] to provide additional music, which eventually accompanied the scenes in which Fred and Renee watch the mysterious VHS tapes.<ref name="Reznor interview"/> The Nine Inch Nails songs "[[The Perfect Drug]]" and "Driver Down" were specifically composed for the film.<ref name="CoS soundtrack"/> Reznor then produced [[Lost Highway (soundtrack)|a soundtrack album]] that includes the film's score and songs by artists such as [[David Bowie]], [[Lou Reed]], [[Marilyn Manson (band)|Marilyn Manson]], [[The Smashing Pumpkins]], and [[Rammstein]].<ref name="AV Club soundtrack"/> Additionally, [[This Mortal Coil]]'s recording of "[[Song to the Siren]]" appears in the film but is not included on the soundtrack.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Aston |first=Martin |date=November 17, 2011 |title=Song to the Siren's irresistible tang |url=https://www.theguardian.com/music/2011/nov/17/song-to-the-siren-classic |access-date=March 8, 2026 |website=[[The Guardian]]}}</ref>

Marilyn Manson's contributions include a cover of [[Screamin' Jay Hawkins]]' "[[I Put a Spell on You]]", which was previously released on the EP ''[[Smells Like Children]]'' (1995), and "[[Apple of Sodom (song)|Apple of Sodom]]", which was specifically written for the film.<ref name="AV Club soundtrack"/> Smashing Pumpkins frontman [[Billy Corgan]] wrote the song "[[Eye (song)|Eye]]" for the film after Lynch rejected an early version of the song "Tear" from the band's album ''[[Adore (The Smashing Pumpkins album)|Adore]]'' (1998).<ref name="AV Club soundtrack"/> The Rammstein songs "[[Rammstein (song)|Rammstein]]" and "Heirate Mich" were included after Lynch listened to their album ''[[Herzeleid]]'' (1995) while exploring locations for the film.<ref name="AV Club soundtrack"/> The track "Insensatez", an instrumental version of the [[bossa nova]] song "[[How Insensitive]]" by [[Antônio Carlos Jobim]], was also included as part of the film's soundtrack.<ref name="AV Club soundtrack"/> The album was released on November 26, 1996,<ref name="MTV soundtrack"/> reaching No. 7 on the [[Billboard 200|''Billboard'' 200]] chart and being certified [[Recording Industry Association of America|Gold]] in the U.S. and [[Music Canada|Platinum]] in Canada.<ref name="AV Club soundtrack"/><ref>{{Cite web |title=Gold & Platinum |url=https://www.riaa.com/gold-platinum/?tab_active=default-award&ar=Soundtrack&ti=Lost+Highway&format=Album&type=#search_section |access-date=2024-02-28 |website=RIAA |language=en-US}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |date=1997-09-22 |title=Gold/Platinum |url=https://musiccanada.com/gold-platinum/?_gp_search=Lost%20Highway%20soundtrack |access-date=2024-02-28 |website=Music Canada |language=en-US}}</ref>

==Release==
===Box office===
''Lost Highway'' was released in France on January 15, 1997.<ref name="Bifi" /> The film received its North American [[premiere]] in January 1997 at the [[Sundance Film Festival]].<ref name="Sundance Film Festival"/> It was then given a [[limited release]] in 12 theaters on February 21, 1997, grossing nearly $213,000 at the U.S. box office weekend.<ref name="Box Office Mojo"/> The film [[Wide release|expanded]] a week later in 212 theaters and, after a modest three-week run, went on to make $3.7 million in North America.<ref name="Box Office Mojo"/> ''Lost Highway'' was released in Russia on May 19, 2017, two decades after its initial release, and grossed $28,347.<ref name="The Numbers"/> Overall, the film grossed nearly $3.8 million worldwide.<ref name="The Numbers"/>

===Home media===
''Lost Highway'' was released on [[DVD]] on March 25, 2008, by [[Universal Studios Home Entertainment]]. The DVD is presented in [[Anamorphic format|anamorphic]] widescreen in the 2.35:1 ratio with [[Dolby Digital]] 5.1 audio.<ref name="Slant DVD"/> The film was then released on [[Blu-ray]] format in France in 2010, and in Japan and the United Kingdom in 2012.<ref name="Blu-ray JP"/><ref name="Blu-ray UK"/> The British edition includes a collection of short, experimental films that Lynch had previously sold on his website. However, it was encoded in [[1080i]] resolution at a 50 [[Hz]] [[frame rate]], as opposed to the [[1080p]] resolution at 24 [[frames per second]] of the French and Japanese editions.<ref name="Blu-ray UK"/> In the United States, ''Lost Highway'' was released on Blu-ray on June 25, 2019, by [[Kino International (company)|Kino Lorber]] using the 2008 Universal master.<ref name="US Blu-ray"/> Lynch did not participate in the release, saying, "It was made from old elements and not from a restoration of the original [[Negative (photography)|negative]]. I hope that a version from the restoration of the original negative will happen as soon as possible."<ref name="IndieWire Blu-ray"/> Kino Lorber responded the release was sourced from the Universal Pictures master, and that they had intended to work with Lynch on the release but had "sent email after email without one response".<ref name="IndieWire Blu-ray"/> [[The Criterion Collection]] released a [[Ultra HD Blu-ray|4K Blu-ray]] with a restoration supervised and approved by Lynch on October 11, 2022.<ref name="Criterion"/>

==Critical reception==
===Contemporaneous reviews===
Upon release, ''Lost Highway'' received mixed reviews. [[Gene Siskel]] and [[Roger Ebert]] gave the film "two thumbs down", which Lynch would later tout as "two more great reasons to see" the film.<ref name="DV review"/> Ebert argued that Lynch effectively puts images on the screen and uses a strong soundtrack to create mood, but that the film does not make sense and that it is "about design, not cinema".<ref name="Ebert review"/> Similarly, [[Kenneth Turan]] of the ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' wrote that ''Lost Highway'' is a "beautifully made but emotionally empty" film that "exists only for the sensation of its provocative moments".<ref name="LAT review"/> Both [[Stephanie Zacharek]] of ''[[Salon (website)|Salon]]'' and [[Owen Gleiberman]] of ''[[Entertainment Weekly]]'' felt that the film was superficial, especially when compared to ''Blue Velvet''.<ref name="Salon review"/><ref name="EW review"/> Zacharek felt that Lynch had "traded some of his disturbing originality for noir formula and schticky weirdness",<ref name="Salon review"/> while Gleiberman compared the film's sex scenes to those of "mediocre Hollywood thrillers".<ref name="EW review"/>

''[[The New York Times]]'' journalist [[Janet Maslin]] felt that, while the film's perversity is unoriginal and resembles that of ''Blue Velvet'', it still "holds sinister interest of its own" and "invites its audience to ponder".<ref name="NYT review"/> ''[[Metro Silicon Valley|Metro]]'' editor [[Richard von Busack]] praised ''Lost Highway'' as a "true horror" film due to its confusing and unpleasant screenplay.<ref name="Metroactive"/> He explained that horror "ought to transcend logic and ordinary reality" and, unlike with popular horror films like ''[[Scream (1996 film)|Scream]]'' (1996), where the difference between screen violence and real violence is obvious, Lynch "present[s] horror as horror, willing to baffle us, willing to wound us".<ref name="Metroactive"/> In another positive review, Andy Klein of the ''[[Dallas Observer]]'' felt that ''Lost Highway'' was a return to form for Lynch and considered it his best work since ''Blue Velvet''.<ref name="DO review"/> Klein compared the film's unanswerable concerns to the "Star Gate" sequence from ''[[2001: A Space Odyssey (film)|2001: A Space Odyssey]]'' (1968), stating that ''Lost Highway'' is "better absorbed and experienced than analyzed".<ref name="DO review"/>

Writing for the ''[[Chicago Reader]]'', critic [[Jonathan Rosenbaum]] felt that ''Lost Highway'' was "an audacious move away from conventional narrative and back toward the formal beauty of ''[[Eraserhead]]''".<ref name="CR review"/> He credited Lynch's "masterful and often powerful fusions of sound and image" for giving the film a very [[Expressionism|expressionist]] style.<ref name="CR review"/> However, he criticized the noir [[iconography]] for its lack of historical context. For example, he explained that, while Arquette's clothes fit in a noir setting, The Mystery Man's video camera is very contemporary and feels out of place.<ref name="CR review"/> [[Todd McCarthy]] of ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' concluded that, although ''Lost Highway'' is "uneven and too deliberately obscure in meaning to be entirely satisfying", the result "remains sufficiently intriguing and startling to bring many of Lynch's old fans back on board".<ref name="Variety review"/>

At the 1997 [[Stinkers Bad Movie Awards]], ''Lost Highway'' was nominated for Worst Picture and Worst Director, but lost to ''[[Batman & Robin (film)|Batman & Robin]]'' in both categories.<ref name="Stinkers Awards"/> In 1998, it was nominated for the [[Belgian Film Critics Association]]'s [[Grand Prix (Belgian Film Critics Association)|Grand Prix]], but lost to ''[[Lone Star (1996 film)|Lone Star]]''.<ref name="Grand Prix nom"/>

===Legacy===
''Lost Highway'' has retrospectively attracted both critical praise and scholarly interest.<ref name="Lost Highway at 20"/><ref name="Slate DVD"/> On review aggregator [[Rotten Tomatoes]], 70% of 63 critics gave the film a positive review. The website's critical consensus reads, "Marking a further escalation in David Lynch's surrealist style, ''Lost Highway'' is a foreboding mystery that arguably leads to a dead end, although it is signposted throughout with some of the director's most haunting images yet."<ref name="Rotten Tomatoes"/> On [[Metacritic]], the film holds a score of 53 out of 100 based on 21 reviews.<ref name="Metacritic"/> ''Lost Highway'' was ranked at No. 323 on the 2012 ''[[Sight & Sound]]'' critics' poll of The Greatest Films of All Time.<ref name="BFI Greatest"/>

As a [[cult film]],<ref name="DV review"/> ''Lost Highway'' was included in ''[[The A.V. Club]]''{{'}}s "The New Cult Canon" section.<ref name="AV Club cult"/> Editor Scott Tobias viewed it as "more cohesive than it might appear at first blush" and argued that Lynch "goes digging for truths that people don't know or won't acknowledge about themselves—within dreams, within the subconscious, within those impossibly dark hallways where we fear to tread".<ref name="AV Club cult"/> Lucia Bozzola of the [[AllMovie]] online database claimed that ''Lost Highway'' retrospectively remains "a sound/image tour de force".<ref name="AllMovie review"/> Jeremiah Kipp of ''[[Slant Magazine]]'' claimed that the film was not an artistic failure but rather "in many ways, it's Lynch at his most daring, emotional, and personal" and described it as "a fitting companion piece to, and inversion of, ''[[Mulholland Drive (film)|Mulholland Drive]]''".<ref name="Slant DVD"/> Writing for ''[[Little White Lies (magazine)|Little White Lies]]'', William Carroll also connected the film to ''Mulholland Drive'' due to "Los Angeles' iconic topography", and felt that it deserved to be regarded as one of Lynch's best works.<ref name="Lost Highway at 20"/> ''[[Daily Vanguard]]'' editor Victoria Castellanos remarked that the film "serves as a wonderful companion to ''Mulholland Drive'' and ''[[Inland Empire (film)|Inland Empire]]'', and in many ways is more surreal and emotional than some of Lynch's other films".<ref name="DV review"/>

Lynch's filmography inspired the ''[[Silent Hill]]'' video game series, with ''Lost Highway'' specifically serving as an inspiration for ''[[Silent Hill 2]]'' (2001).<ref>{{Cite web |last=Fukuyama |first=Koji |date=2023-01-16 |title=Silent Hill 2 Remake: World Exclusive Deep Dive Interview |url=https://www.ign.com/articles/silent-hill-2-remake-world-exclusive-deep-dive-interview |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230116120552/https://www.ign.com/articles/silent-hill-2-remake-world-exclusive-deep-dive-interview |archive-date=January 16, 2023 |access-date=2024-10-24 |website=IGN |language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |author1=Dustin Bailey |date=2024-08-23 |title=Silent Hill 2 veteran says the survival horror classic would have featured David Lynch-inspired multiple protagonists if it didn't make the story "too complicated" |url=https://www.gamesradar.com/games/silent-hill/silent-hill-2-veteran-says-the-survival-horror-classic-would-have-featured-david-lynch-inspired-multiple-protagonists-if-it-didnt-make-the-story-too-complicated/ |access-date=2024-08-25 |website=GamesRadar+ |language=en}}</ref>
