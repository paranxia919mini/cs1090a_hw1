'''''The Thomas Crown Affair''''' is a 1968 American [[heist film]] directed by [[Norman Jewison]] and written by [[Alan Trustman|Alan R. Trustman]]. Starring [[Steve McQueen]], [[Faye Dunaway]], [[Paul Burke (actor)|Paul Burke]], and [[Jack Weston]], the film follows Vicki Anderson (Dunaway), who is hired to investigate the culprits of a multi-million dollar bank heist orchestrated by Thomas Crown (McQueen).

The film was nominated for two [[Academy Awards]], winning [[Academy Award for Best Original Song|Best Original Song]] for [[Michel Legrand]]'s "[[The Windmills of Your Mind]]". [[The Thomas Crown Affair (1999 film)|A remake]] was released in 1999, and [[The Thomas Crown Affair (2027 film)|a second remake]] is set to be released in 2027. A Hong Kong version of the movie was released in 1987 under the title ''[[:zh:通天大盜|Easy Money]]'', starring [[Michelle Yeoh]] as Thomas Crown's female version.

==Plot==
Multi-millionaire businessman Thomas Crown is a handsome, dashing, cultured adrenaline junkie. Out of boredom, he masterminds a five-man heist of $2.66 million from a Boston bank, with the getaway driver dumping the money in a quiet cemetery trash can. None of the men ever meet Crown face to face, nor know or meet each other before the robbery. Crown retrieves the money after secretly trailing the drop. He deposits it into a numbered bank account in Geneva over several trips to avoid suspicion. 

Independent investigator Vicki Anderson is contracted by the bank's insurance company to investigate the heist; she will receive 10% of the stolen money if she recovers it. Speculating that whoever planned the robbery was familiar with the bank's routines because he had an account there, and that the stolen money has been spirited to Switzerland, Vicki and the police draw up a list of bank customers who have made several recent trips to Geneva. When she sees Crown's profile on the list, she intuitively recognizes him as capable of orchestrating the robbery, and shortly thereafter guesses the cellular method that he used to organize the robbers.

Growing closer to her quarry, Vicki makes it clear to Crown that she knows he is the thief and that she intends to prove it. A game of cat and mouse ensues, which spurs both a physical and emotional attraction that soon evolves into an affair. This complicates Vicki's vow to find the money and help detective friend Eddy Malone bring the perpetrator to justice.

A reward offer entices the wife of the getaway driver, Erwin Weaver, to [[informant|inform]] on him for $25,000. Vicki finds out that he was hired by a man he never saw but whose voice he heard (via a microphone). She tries placing Erwin in the same room as Crown, but there is no hint of recognition on either's part.

While Vicki is clearly closing in on Crown, even using the [[Internal Revenue Service]] (IRS) as leverage against his liquid assets, he forces her to realize the extent of her conflicted emotions. When she seemingly persuades him to negotiate an end, Malone stubbornly refuses to make any deal, leaving her torn between the men, her convictions, and her heart.

Crown organizes another robbery exactly like the first with different accomplices and tells Vicki where the "drop" will be, because he has to know for sure that she is on his side. The robbery goes off like clockwork.

Choosing her loyalty, Vicki and the police stake out the cemetery, where they watch one of the robbers make the drop, then lie in wait to ambush Crown. However, when his [[Rolls-Royce Silver Shadow|Rolls-Royce]] arrives, he has sent a messenger in his place, with a [[telegram]] inviting her to either bring the money and join him or to keep the car as a [[consolation prize]]. Reflexively smiling at his eluding her trap, she soon realizes she had revealed her hand yet gained nothing in return. Tearing the telegram to bits, she strews the pieces to the wind, tearfully staring at the sky while Crown escapes unruffled overhead.

==Production==
A pioneering [[split screen (film)|split screen]] photography technique used to show simultaneous actions was inspired by the breakthrough [[Expo 67]] films ''[[In the Labyrinth (film)|In the Labyrinth]]'' and ''[[A Place to Stand (film)|A Place to Stand]]''. The latter debuted the use of Christopher Chapman's "[[multi-dynamic image technique]]" of images shifting on moving panes.<ref>{{cite news|title=When camera and gun collide|last=Atherton|first=Tony|newspaper=[[Ottawa Citizen]]|pages=D7|date=2000-07-10}}</ref><ref name="Scrivener">{{cite news | url=https://www.thestar.com/article/205867 | newspaper=[[Toronto Star]] | first=Leslie | last=Scrivener | title=Forty years on, a song retains its standing | date=2007-04-22 | access-date=September 16, 2017 | archive-date=September 25, 2012 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120925203428/http://www.thestar.com/article/205867 | url-status=dead}}</ref> Steve McQueen was on hand for an advance screening of ''A Place to Stand'' in Hollywood, and personally told Chapman he was highly impressed; the following year, Norman Jewison incorporated the technique into the film by inserting split screen scenes into the already finished product.<ref name="Scrivener"/>

The film also features a [[chess]] scene, with McQueen and Dunaway playing a game of chess, silently [[flirting]] with each other.<ref>{{cite book |title=One Hundred Sex Scenes That Changed Cinema |first=Neil|last=Fulwood |page=32 |isbn=978-0-7134-8858-6 |year=2003|publisher=Batsford}}</ref> The game depicted is based on a game played in Vienna in 1898 between Gustav Zeissl and Walter von Walthoffen.<ref>{{cite web|first=Bill|last=Wall|url=https://www.chess.com/article/view/the-thomas-crown-affair|title=The Thomas Crown Affair|website=[[Chess.com]]|date=October 29, 2009 |access-date=June 23, 2017|archive-date=April 28, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180428093455/https://www.chess.com/article/view/the-thomas-crown-affair|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1242897|title=Zeissl-Walthoffen|website=chessgames.com|access-date=June 23, 2017|archive-date=October 25, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171025073816/http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1242897|url-status=live}}</ref> The game was played on a Cantonese Export 1820-1850 "Burmese" Pattern chess set.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.chess-collection.de/newpage6|title=Cantonese Export "Burmese" Ivory Chess Set, ca. 1820-1850}}</ref>

McQueen performed his own stunts, which include playing polo and driving a dune buggy at high speed along the Massachusetts coastline.<ref name=machines>{{cite book|last=Stone|first=Matt|title=McQueen's Machines: The Cars and Bikes of a Hollywood Icon|year=2007|publisher=MBI Publishing Company|location=Minneapolis, Minnesota|isbn=978-0-7603-3895-7|pages=82–83}}</ref>

Vicki Anderson's car, referred to as "one of those red Italian things",<ref name=rat>[https://www.roadandtrack.com/car-culture/news/a28989/a-ferrari-spider-goes-for-a-grand-tour/ A Ferrari Spider Goes For A Grand Tour], Road and Track, Blake Z. Rong,: April 30, 2016</ref> is the first of only ten [[Ferrari 275#275 GTB/4S NART Spider|Ferrari 275 GTB/4S NART Spiders]] built.<ref name=machines/> Today, this model is one of the most valuable Ferrari road cars made.<ref name=rat/> McQueen liked the car very much, and eventually managed to acquire one for himself.<ref name=machines/> The [[dune buggy]] was a [[Meyers Manx]].{{citation needed|date=March 2024}} McQueen owned one.{{citation needed|date=March 2024}} Crown's two-door [[Rolls-Royce Silver Shadow#Two-door versions|Rolls-Royce Silver Shadow]] carried the Massachusetts vanity license plate "TC 100" for the film.

[[Sean Connery]] had been the original choice for the title role, but turned it down—a decision he later regretted.<ref>{{cite news|last=Jaccarino|first=Mike|title='Thomas Crown Affair' screenwriter Alan Trustman talks films, working with Steve McQueen|url=http://www.nydailynews.com/entertainment/tv-movies/thomas-crown-affair-screenwriter-alan-trustman-talks-films-working-steve-mcqueen-article-1.950830|access-date=21 July 2013|newspaper=NY Daily News|date=28 August 2011|archive-date=December 28, 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131228064227/http://www.nydailynews.com/entertainment/tv-movies/thomas-crown-affair-screenwriter-alan-trustman-talks-films-working-steve-mcqueen-article-1.950830|url-status=live}}</ref> [[Johnny Carson]] also turned down the role.<ref>{{cite magazine | url=https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1978/02/20/fifteen-years-salto-mortale | title=The World of Johnny Carson | magazine=The New Yorker | date=February 20, 1978 | last1=Tynan | first1=Kenneth}}</ref>

==Release==
''The Thomas Crown Affair'' had its world premiere in [[Boston]] on June 19, 1968, with openings in Los Angeles and New York on June 26, 1968, and a nationwide release in August 1968.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/19845|title=The Thomas Crown Affair (1968)|website=[[AFI Catalog of Feature Films]]|access-date=January 3, 2020|archive-date=June 6, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190606155046/https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/19845|url-status=live}}</ref>

This film's release introduced [[United Artists]]' new logo that shows the iconic [[Transamerica Corporation|Transamerica]] "T" and the byline, "Entertainment from Transamerica Corporation".{{citation needed|date=December 2023}}

''The Thomas Crown Affair'' made its American television premiere on ''[[NBC Saturday Night at the Movies]]'' in September 1972.

==Reception==
===Box office===
''The Thomas Crown Affair'' grossed $14&nbsp;million on a $4.3&nbsp;million budget.<ref name="numbers">{{Cite web|url=https://www.the-numbers.com/movies/1968/0TCFF.php|website=[[The Numbers (website)|The Numbers]]|title=The Thomas Crown Affair, Box Office Information|access-date=May 23, 2012|archive-date=June 27, 2012|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120627002208/http://www.the-numbers.com/movies/1968/0TCFF.php|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Critical response===
Reviews at the time were mixed. Critics praised the chemistry between McQueen and Dunaway and Norman Jewison's stylish direction, but considered the plotting and writing rather thin. [[Roger Ebert]] gave it two-and-a-half stars out of four, calling it "possibly the most under-plotted, underwritten, over-photographed film of the year. Which is not to say it isn't great to look at. It is."<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/thomas-crown-affair-1968|title=Thomas Crown Affair|access-date=2020-10-04|last=Ebert|first=Roger|date=1968-08-27|newspaper=[[Chicago Sun-Times]]|via=[[RogerEbert.com]]|archive-date=September 26, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200926101925/https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/thomas-crown-affair-1968|url-status=live}}</ref> 

Film critic of the time, [[Pauline Kael]], called it "pretty good trash" and likened the film to enjoyable entertainment not to be confused with art.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Kael |first=Pauline |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=tkShTL84MrcC |title=The Age of Movies: Selected Writings of Pauline Kael: A Library of America Special Publication |date=2011-10-27 |publisher=Library of America |isbn=978-1-59853-171-8 |pages=221 |language=en}}</ref>

On the [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film holds an approval rating of 70% based on 40 reviews, with an average rating of 6.5/10. The website's critics consensus reads, "Steve McQueen settles into the role with ease and aplomb, in a film that whisks viewers into an exotic world with style and sex appeal."<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/thomas_crown_affair |title=The Thomas Crown Affair (1968) |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |access-date=February 10, 2024}}</ref> [[Metacritic]], which uses a [[Weighted arithmetic mean|weighted average]], assigned the {{#switch:  | episode = episode | series = series | season = season | film }} a score of 66 out of 100, based on 7 critics, indicating "generally favorable" reviews.<ref name="Metacritic">{{cite web |title=''The Thomas Crown Affair'' |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-thomas-crown-affair-1968 |website=[[Metacritic]] |publisher=[[Fandom (website)#Fandom, Inc.|Fandom, Inc.]] |access-date= |archive-url= |archive-date= |url-status=}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |title=The Thomas Crown Affair |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-thomas-crown-affair-1968/ |access-date=20 September 2025 |website=Metacritic}}</ref>

The music was composed and conducted by [[Michel Legrand]], scoring his first major American film. Director Norman Jewison had hoped to hire [[Henry Mancini]] for the project, but he was unavailable and recommended Legrand; he wrote his music as long pieces rather than specifically to scene timings, with the film later edited to the music by Legrand, Jewison and editor [[Hal Ashby]]. In addition, Legrand also had to prepare an original song to replace "[[Strawberry Fields Forever]]", used as the temporary track for the glider scene. Taking [[Quincy Jones]]' advice, Legrand worked with the [[Alan and Marilyn Bergman|Bergmans]] to compose "[[The Windmills of Your Mind]]" and a second song, "His Eyes, Her Eyes"; [[Noel Harrison]] recorded "The Windmills of Your Mind" after Jewison failed to get his friend [[Andy Williams]] to do it, while Legrand performed "His Eyes, Her Eyes". Although the film's score was recorded in Hollywood, featuring [[Vincent DeRosa]], [[Bud Shank]], [[Carol Kaye]], [[Emil Richards]], [[Ray Brown (musician)|Ray Brown]] and [[Shelly Manne]], the album re-recording issued by [[United Artists Records]] on [[LP record|LP]] was done in France under the composer's baton; Jewison said it was the favorite score for any of his films.<ref>Stéphane Lerouge, "Michel Legrand: The Windmills of His Mind," liner notes, expanded MGM motion picture soundtrack, Quartet QR 158</ref>

The original album was later reissued by [[Rykodisc]] in 1998 on [[Compact disc|CD]], with five dialogue excerpts and the inclusion of "Moments of Love" and "Doubting Thomas". [[Varèse Sarabande]] re-released the album in 2004 (without the dialogue excerpts). In 2014, Quartet Records issued a limited edition CD featuring the previously released album tracks (1–13 below) and the premiere release of the film version.

==In popular culture==
* The film's famous kissing scene, which is depicted on the film's poster, was used in [[Hal Ashby]]'s [[satirical film]] ''[[Being There]]'' (1979). Ashby was ''The Thomas Crown Affair'''s associate producer and editor. 
* The music video for [[Madonna]]'s 1998 single "[[The Power of Good-Bye]]" is based on the chess scene from the film.
* The chess scene is parodied in the 1999 film ''[[Austin Powers: The Spy Who Shagged Me]]''.

==Remakes==
===1999 film===
{{Main|The Thomas Crown Affair (1999 film)}}

The 1999 remake was released starring [[Pierce Brosnan]] as Crown, [[Rene Russo]] as the insurance investigator, and [[Denis Leary]] as the detective. The original film's co-star Faye Dunaway also appears as Crown's therapist. This adaptation is different from the original in that it is set in New York rather than Boston, and the robbery is of a priceless painting, a ''[[San Giorgio Maggiore at Dusk|Monet]]'', instead of cash, among other story line differences, including the complete lack of violence in Thomas Crown's crimes.<ref name="Comparison_SC">{{cite web|url=https://spectrumculture.com/2013/05/12/remakeremodel-the-thomas-crown-affair-1968-vs-the-thomas-crown-affair-1999/|website=Spectrum Culture|title=Remake/Remodel: The Thomas Crown Affair (1968) vs. The Thomas Crown Affair (1999)|author-last1=Hassannia|author-first1=Tina|date=May 12, 2013|accessdate=April 15, 2023}}</ref>
