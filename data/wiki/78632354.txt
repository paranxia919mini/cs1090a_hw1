'''Communism in Brazil''' has existed at least since the 1920s.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/> The movement has given rise to various [[leftist]] factions and uprisings. It has been embodied in social movements, various political parties, and in the intellectual works of various [[Marxist]] authors.<ref name="dm-importancia">{{Cite web | first=Yuri | last=Brandão |date=2017-04-03 |title=A importância do Partido Comunista Brasileiro para a nossa história |url=https://www.dm.com.br/opiniao/2017/04/a-importancia-do-partido-comunista-brasileiro-para-a-nossa-historia |access-date=2024-12-31 |website={{ill|Diário da Manhã (Goiânia)|pt|lt=Diário da Manhã}} |language=pt-br}}</ref>

Currently, there are seven officially registered [[Political party|political parties]] in Brazil that claim to be [[Communism|communist]] or communist-adjacent: [[Brazilian Communist Party]] (PCB),<ref name="pcb-ideology"/> [[Communist Party of Brazil]] (PCdoB),<ref name="pcdob-ideology"/> [[Workers' Cause Party]] (PCO),<ref name="pco-ideology1"/><ref name="pco-ideology2"/> [[Socialism and [[Popular Unity (Brazil)|Popular Unity]] (UP).<ref name="up-ideology"/> Additionally, several communist parties in Brazil have their own [[youth wing]]s: for example, PCB's Young Communist Union == History ==
=== Late 19th and early 20th century ===
Worker organizations in Brazil are known to have existed since the 19th century. The first known strike by salaried employees happened in 1858, though slave revolts related to working conditions had been happening prior — slavery was only [[Lei Áurea|abolished in Brazil]] in 1888. Though information is lacking due to the societal treatment of slaves at the time, it is known that many such revolts ended with police repression.<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://riomemorias.com.br/memoria/greve-de-escravizados/ | title=Greve de Escravizados | language=pt-br | website=Associação Rio Memórias | date=20 March 2023 | access-date=4 November 2024 }}</ref>

[[File:Jornal dos Typographos n.1 (1858-01-10) p.1.jpg|thumb|Front page of the first issue of ''Jornal dos Typographos'', from 10 January 1858. The leftmost column contains the typographers' reasoning for the strike and their motivation for starting this new bulletin]]
On 8 January 1858, a total of 80 [[typographer]]s from 3 daily publications (''[[Jornal do Commercio]]'', ''{{ill|Correio Mercantil|pt}}'' and ''{{ill|Diário do Rio de Janeiro|pt}}'') refused to work simultaneously.<ref name="vitorino2010">{{ cite journal  | url=https://ojs.ifch.unicamp.br/index.php/ael/article/view/2479 | title=Escravismo, proletários e a greve dos compositores tipográficos de 1858 no Rio de Janeiro | language=pt-br | first=Artur José Renda | last=Vitorino | journal=Sociedades Operárias e Mutualismo | pages=69–107 | publisher=Cadernos [[Arquivo Edgard Leuenroth]] | volume=6 | issue=10/11 | date=8 October 2010 | access-date=4 November 2024 }}</ref> The workers published a bulletin, titled ''Jornal dos Typographos'', stating their demands for better pay in light of increasing food prices. They further explained that the strike was a last resort after being denied a raise on multiple occasions, as they had not seen an increase in pay since 1855.<ref name="vitorino2010"/> The result of the typographers' strike is not known; it lasted until at least 12 March 1858, the date of the last issue of ''Jornal dos Typographos'' before it was sold.<ref name="vitorino2010"/>

In 1890, the first self-declared [[Socialism|socialist]] political party of Brazil was established in [[Rio Grande do Sul]], namely the ''Partido Operário do Rio Grande do Sul''.<ref name="may1st">{{ cite web | url=https://vermelho.org.br/2020/05/01/o-1o-de-maio-e-a-luta-historica-dos-trabalhadores-pelos-seus-direitos/ | title=O 1º de Maio e a luta histórica dos trabalhadores pelos seus direitos | language=pt-br | first=Raúl | last=Carrion | website=O Vermelho | date=1 May 2020 | access-date=4 November 2024 }}</ref> It called for, among other things, [[women's rights]] and the end of [[inheritance]] rights.<ref name="may1st"/>

The early 1900s were rife with strikes, usually in the interest of better pay and shorter work days.<ref name="teoriaecultura">{{ cite journal | url=https://periodicos.ufjf.br/index.php/TeoriaeCultura/article/view/12383 | doi=10.34019/2318-101X.2017.v12.12383 | title=A Revolução Russa e o Brasil | language=pt-br | trans-title=The Russian Revolution and Brazil | first1=André Moysés | last1=Gaio | first2=Daniel Jorge Salles de | last2=Freitas | journal=Teoria e Cultura | issn=1809-5968 | eissn=2318-101X | publisher=[[Federal University of Juiz de Fora]] | volume=12 | issue=2 | date=30 November 2017 | access-date=5 November 2024 | doi-access=free }}</ref> For example, the 8-hour workday was among the proposals of the [[First Brazilian Workers' Congress]] held in April 1906. In October, workers inspired by that proposal participated in the [[1906 Porto Alegre general strike|21-day strike]] in [[Porto Alegre]] achieving a partial success by reducing their workday from 11 to 9 hours. However, strikes were more commonly met with police repression and, even when successfully leading to new agreements, company owners could simply decide not to honor them or revert any of the changes after some time.<ref name="teoriaecultura"/><ref name="bbc-strike"/><ref name="scielo1917"/>

After the abolition of slavery in Brazil in 1888, coffee farmers saw immigration as a source of cheap labor to meet production demands.<ref name="alice-gordo-leis">{{ cite web | url=https://cpdoc.fgv.br/sites/default/files/verbetes/primeira-republica/LEIS%20ADOLFO%20GORDO.pdf | title=Leis Adolfo Gordo | language=pt-br | first=Alice Beatriz da Silva Gordo | last=Lang | publisher=[[Fundação Getulio Vargas]] | year=2013 | access-date=5 November 2024 }}</ref><ref name="clarté-brazil">{{
cite journal | title=The Clarté Group in Brazil | url=https://www.jstor.org/stable/3778017 | doi=10.2307/3778017 | first1=Michael M. | last1=Hall | first2=Paulo Sérgio | last2=Pinheiro | year=1980 | journal=[[Le Mouvement social]] | issn=0027-2671 | eissn=1961-8646 | number=111 | pages=217–234 | jstor=3778017 | url-access=limited }}</ref> However, immigrants brought with them ideals of anarchism and socialism, which the landowners considered undesirable. In 1907, federal deputy [[Adolfo Gordo]] authored Decree N.1,641, which became known as the [[Adolfo Gordo Law]] or the first "Foreigner Expulsion Law", allowing for the immigrants' expulsion from the country for [[vagrancy]] or simply "compromising public tranquility".<ref name="teoriaecultura"/><ref name="alice-gordo-leis"/><ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www2.camara.leg.br/legin/fed/decret/1900-1909/decreto-1641-7-janeiro-1907-582166-publicacaooriginal-104906-pl.html | title=Decreto Nº 1.641, de 7 de Janeiro de 1907 | language=pt-br | trans-title=Decree N.1,641, of 7 January 1907 | first=Afonso | last=Pena | authorlink=Afonso Pena | date=7 January 1907 | access-date=5 November 2024 }}</ref> This and other laws authored by Gordo became yet another tool for capitalists to suppress strikes and workers' movements.<ref name="alice-gordo-leis"/>

Ideologically, not every revolutionary worker self-identified as a [[Communism|communist]] or a socialist; many were deemed [[Anarchism|anarchists]], who rejected the idea of political parties, and instead preferred direct action or organization through [[Trade union|unions]].<ref name="teoriaecultura"/> Political theory and literature were not always accessible in Brazil, either because they were not translated into [[Brazilian Portuguese|Portuguese]], or because the Portuguese text was not accommodating to workers with little to no [[Education in Brazil|formal education]].<ref name="teoriaecultura"/> As such, many at the time were simply [[Anti-capitalism|anti-capitalists]], supporting a revolution without fully considering what would come after any revolutionary action.<ref name="teoriaecultura"/>

=== World War I and the Russian Revolution ===
[[File:São Paulo (Greve de 1917).jpg|thumb|300px|Workers carrying red flags at a demonstration in São Paulo during the 1917 [[general strike]]]]

In July 1914, [[World War I]] began. While Brazil did not see direct conflict, the effects of the war were quickly felt by its citizens. Brazil's economy was (as it had been for decades) reliant on [[Brazilian coffee cycle|exporting coffee]]; in 1914 the nation controlled around 80% of the world's coffee exports.<ref name="bbc-ww1">{{ cite web | url=https://www.bbc.com/portuguese/noticias/2014/11/141110_brasil_guerra_fd | title=Participação do Brasil na 1ª Guerra trouxe ganhos modestos e baque econômico | language=pt-br | trans-title=Brazil's participation in World War I brought modest gains and economical impact | first=Fernando | last=Duarte | website=[[BBC News]] | date=11 November 2014 | access-date=5 November 2024 }}</ref> With the advent of the war, demand for coffee fell drastically. Additionally, [[Blockade of Germany (1914–1919)|Britain's blockade]], aimed at preventing aid to the [[Central Powers]] from neutral countries such as Brazil, successfully hindered trade with a big part of Europe, worsening Brazil's coffee exports.<ref name="bbc-ww1"/>

In contrast, in certain industries such as [[Textile industry|textiles]], profits skyrocketed. With the war, products that would previously be imported now had to be produced in Brazil, and were even exported in some cases.<ref name="bbc-strike">{{ cite web | url=https://www.bbc.com/portuguese/brasil-39740614 | title=1ª greve geral do país, em 1917, foi iniciada por mulheres e durou 30 dias | language=pt-br | trans-title=First general strike in the country, in 1917, was started by women and lasted for 30 days | first=Camilla | last=Costa | website=[[BBC News]] | date=28 April 2017 | access-date=5 November 2024 }}</ref> However, workers did not see those profits; on the contrary, higher demand meant longer workdays of up to 16 hours, without increase in pay.<ref name="bbc-strike"/> Moreover, with growing demand for food in [[Triple Entente|belligerent countries]], Brazil began heavily exporting crops and meat which, in turn, caused shortages and a massive price increase locally.<ref name="principios-pcdob">{{ cite journal | url=https://revistaprincipios.emnuvens.com.br/principios/article/view/172 | doi=10.4322/principios.2675-6609.2022.163.002 | title=A fundação do Partido Comunista do Brasil | first=Raul K. M. | last=Carrion | volume=41 | number=163 | journal=Princípios | series=Modernismo e revolução nos anos 1920 | pages=9–62 | issn=1415-7888 | eissn=2675-6609 | date=7 February 2022 | access-date=6 November 2024  | doi-access=free }}</ref> Between 1914 and 1919, prices almost tripled, growing by 185%.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/>

In this context, with worse working conditions, a higher cost of living and diminishing [[purchasing power]], workers were at a historical low point.<ref name="bbc-ww1"/><ref name="dw-rusrev">{{ cite web | url=https://www.dw.com/pt-br/os-ecos-da-revolu%C3%A7%C3%A3o-russa-no-brasil/a-40791959 | title=Os ecos da Revolução Russa no Brasil | language=pt-br | trans-title=The echoes of the Russian Revolution in Brazil | first=Clarissa | last=Neher | website=[[Deutsche Welle]] | date=25 October 2017 | access-date=5 November 2024 }}</ref><ref name="scielo1917"/> This culminated in the [[1917–1919 Brazil strike movement|general strike of 1917]], starting in the [[Mooca (district of São Paulo)|Mooca]] district of [[São Paulo]] in early June and spreading throughout Brazil in the following days.<ref name="scielo1917">{{ Cite journal | doi=10.1590/S2178-14942017000200011 | title=Um ano extraordinário: greves, revoltas e circulação de ideias no Brasil em 1917 | language=pt-br | trans-title=An extraordinary year: strikes, uprisings and circulation of ideas in Brazil in 1917 | volume=30 | number=61 | issn=0103-2186 | journal=Estudos Históricos (Rio de Janeiro) | publisher=[[Centro de Pesquisa e Documentação de História Contemporânea do Brasil|CPDOC]] | first=Edilene | last=Toledo | date=May 2017 | pages=497–518 | doi-access=free }}</ref> At its peak, it saw almost 44 thousand workers striking simultaneously.<ref name="scielo1917"/> [[Protest|Demonstrations]] occurred almost daily, protesting low wages, [[child labor]], high rent, and food prices, among other issues.{{efn|The full list of demands of strikers in São Paulo, as reported in the newspaper ''A Plebe'' on 21 July 1917, is as follows:<ref name="scielo1917"/>
* The release of all arrested strikers;
* The "most absolute" respect to the [[freedom of association]] of workers;
* No lay-offs for participation in the strike;
* The abolition of work for children under the age of 14;
* The abolition of night shifts for women and children under the age of 18;
* A 35% pay increase for salaries up to 5$000 [[Brazilian real (old)|Réis]], and of 25% for salaries above that limit;
* The punctual payment of salaries every 2 weeks;
* A guarantee of permanent work;
* An 8-hour work day;
* A 5½-day work week and a 50% pay increase during [[overtime]];
* The immediate cheapening of [[necessity good]]s, blocking the action of hoarders;
* The request, when necessary, of foodstuff for feeding the public;
* Measures to prevent adulteration and falsification of food products;
* The decrease of rent prices.
}}{{efn|This revolutionary sentiment wasn't unique to Brazil; in part due to the effects of WWI, and in part due to news of the success of the Russian Revolution, worker revolts had been happening all across the world – see [[Revolutions of 1917–1923]].}} They were organized by workers themselves, with support from union, anarchist and socialist leaderships.<ref name="scielo1917"/> Additionally, women played a significant role in organizing the strikes. Beyond being workers themselves, they were also usually in charge of home finances. Thus they saw firsthand the ever-growing prices of goods.<ref name="bbc-strike"/><ref name="scielo1917"/>

Brazilian workers, including anarchists, looked to the recent [[Russian Revolution]] with enthusiasm. In the [[Alagoas]] newspaper ''A Semana Social'', in March 1917, {{ill|Antônio Canellas|pt}} wrote: "if the evil temper of the [[bourgeoisie]] doesn't prevent it", then Brazil would also see "the branching of the generous tree that just emerged in Russia — the tree of liberty".<ref name="scielo1917"/> In November 1918, this inspiration was put to action, in a failed [[1918 Rio de Janeiro anarchist insurrection|anarchist insurrection]].<ref name="principios-pcdob"/>

It is also in this context that [[anti-communism|anti-communist]] sentiment was born. Developed from a fear by the elites of revolutionary action by the [[proletariat]], it led Brazilian newspapers at the time to denounce[[Vladimir Lenin]] as an outlaw and crook.<ref name="scielo1917"/> [[Astrojildo Pereira]], one of the bigger enthusiasts of the Russian Revolution in Brazil, would write to newspapers under [[pseudonym]]s to combat the hostile framing of the event.<ref name="scielo1917"/>

=== First communist parties ===
On 2 March 1919, the [[Communist International]] was founded, and [[Twenty-one Conditions|21 conditions]] were given for admission to it. Among them, that "any party seeking affiliation must call itself the Communist Party of the country in question".<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1920/jul/x01.htm | title=Terms of Admission into Communist International | translator-first=Julius | translator-last=Katzer | editor-first1=David | editor-last1=Walters | editor-first2=Robert | editor-last2=Cymbala | website=[[Marxists Internet Archive]] | date=July 1920 | access-date=6 November 2024 }}</ref>

A few days later, news broke of the foundation of Brazil's first self-declared Communist Party (''Partido Comunista do Brasil'', PCdoB) on 9 March 1919, open to "anarchists, socialists and all of those who accepted social communism".<ref name="principios-pcdob"/><ref>{{ cite web | url=https://congressoemfoco.uol.com.br/reportagem/1919-%E2%80%93-o-primeiro-partido-comunista-do-brasil-e-a-revolucao-russa/ | title=1919 – O primeiro Partido Comunista do Brasil e a Revolução Russa | language=pt-br | trans-title=1919 – The first Communist Party of Brazil and the Russian Revolution | first=Augusto | last=Buonicore | website={{ill|Congresso em Foco|pt}} | date=18 March 2017 | access-date=6 November 2024 }}</ref> Founded by anarchist [[José Oiticica]] and delegates from [[Rio de Janeiro (state)|Rio de Janeiro]], [[Rio Grande do Sul]], [[São Paulo (state)|São Paulo]] and [[Alagoas]], the party had major [[Libertarianism|libertarian]] influences; anarchists at the time may not have fully realized their ideological differences from the [[Bolsheviks]], and the Russian Revolution itself may have been seen as an anarchist movement.<ref name="scielo1917"/><ref name="principios-pcdob"/> This first party was short-lived, marking the start of the split between communists and anarchists.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/>

Many strikes had continued to be planned, executed, and severely repressed since 1917, led by anarchists and [[Anarcho-syndicalism|anarcho-syndicalists]].<ref name="principios-pcdob"/> By 1921 workers were wholly defeated and anarchism, which had been against political parties and was seen to lack discipline, received the blame.<ref name="clarté-brazil"/><ref name="principios-pcdob"/> Additionally, due to WWI, the recent push for industrialization had made Brazil a worker-dense country, so conditions were fertile for the growth of communism in Brazil.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/>

In 1918, [[Abílio de Nequete]], Francisco Merino and Otávio Hengist had founded the ''União Maximalista de Porto Alegre'' ([[Maximum programme|Maximalist]] Union of [[Porto Alegre]]), renamed ''Grupo Comunista de Porto Alegre'' (Communist Group of Porto Alegre) in 1921.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/> In the aftermath of the general strikes, the country saw the formation of multiple self-proclaimed communist groups; in 1919, the ''Grupo Comunista Brasileiro Zumbi'', and ''Núcleo Comunista de Pelotas''; in 1921, the ''Grupo Clarté'', inspired by the French group of the same name organized by [[Henri Barbusse]], [[Raymond Lefebvre]], [[Paul Vaillant-Couturier]] and others;<ref name="clarté-brazil"/> and in the same year, the ''Grupo Comunista do Rio de Janeiro''.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/>

''Grupo Clarté'' was founded by [[Everardo Dias]], [[Afonso Schmidt]], [[Lima Barreto]], [[Francisco Cavalcanti Pontes de Miranda|Pontes de Miranda]], {{ill|Cristiano Cordeiro (politician)|pt|Cristiano Cordeiro|lt=Cristiano Cordeiro}}, {{ill|Joaquim Pimenta|pt}}, {{ill|Evaristo de Morais|pt}}, Agripino Nazareth, Antônio Figueiredo, Leônidas Resende, {{ill|Maurício Paiva de Lacerda|pt}} and his brother, Paulo de Lacerda.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/> Though some called themselves socialists, the group tended towards moderate reformism, in the context of a weakened, post-repression [[Left-wing politics|Left]]. Pimenta described his own politics as the "pragmatic method – to wrest from situation and events whatever they can provide on behalf of the working class".<ref name="clarté-brazil"/> The group was largely sympathetic to the Russian Revolution, frequently reprinting documents on events in the Soviet Union, though not organizing any meaningful action towards any revolutionary goal in Brazil.<ref name="clarté-brazil"/>

The ''Grupo Comunista do Rio de Janeiro'' was founded by [[Astrojildo Pereira]] and 11 others. Immediately after formation, the group started contacting other workers' groups throughout the country to share the 21 conditions and recommend their implementation. In response, several other communist groups started to form, in [[Recife]], [[Juiz de Fora]], [[Santos, São Paulo|Santos]] and [[Cruzeiro, São Paulo|Cruzeiro]].<ref name="principios-pcdob"/> In January 1922, the group launched the ''Movimento Comunista'' magazine to spread the agenda of the Communist International.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/>

Between 25 and 27 March 1922, the various communist groups made the decision to start the ''[[Brazilian Communist Party (1922)|Partido Comunista do Brasil]]'' (Communist Party of Brazil; dubbed the "''Partido Communista – Secção Brasileira da Internacional Communista''", or "Communist Party – Brazilian Section of the Communist International"), the first "truly" communist party of Brazil.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/> Soon after its inception, the party would adopt the abbreviation PCB.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/> The party, created through the meeting of 9 delegates representing 73 affiliates throughout the country, was hurried in light of the soon-approaching [[4th World Congress of the Communist International]], for which Brazil had been lacking representation.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/> Due to the lack of political party-specific legislation at the time, the party was registered as a [[civil society]].<ref name="principios-pcdob"/>

Knowledge of [[Marxism]] was still in its infancy in Brazil at the time. As such, PCB was under educated in Marxist theory in its inception.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/>

Parallel to the PCB's foundation on 1 March 1922, Brazil held a [[1922 Brazilian presidential election|presidential election]].<ref name="senado-1922-elections">{{ cite web | url=https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/especiais/arquivo-s/em-1922-eleicao-teve-fake-news-e-resultado-questionado | title=Em 1922, eleição presidencial teve fake news e resultado questionado | language=pt-br | first=Ricardo | last=Westin | website=[[Federal Senate (Brazil)|Agência Senado]] | date=2022-07-01 | access-date=2025-02-28 }}</ref> It was won by [[Artur Bernardes]] of the [[Mineiro Republican Party]], who was up against [[Nilo Peçanha]].<ref name="senado-1922-elections"/> Bernardes' candidacy was part of the [[milk coffee politics]] of the time, a scheme which ensured only candidates from the two wealthiest states, [[Minas Gerais (state)|Minas Gerais]] and [[São Paulo (state)|São Paulo]], would occupy the presidency.<ref name="senado-1922-elections"/> The opposition contested the election results and, over the following months, a military conspiracy emerged across the country to remove the still-in-office [[Epitácio Pessoa]] and prevent Bernardes' inauguration.<ref name="senado-1922-elections"/> This culminated in the [[Copacabana Fort revolt]], which began on 4 July 1922.<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www.jota.info/artigos/centenario-da-revolta-dos-18-do-forte-de-copacabana | title=Centenário da Revolta dos 18 do Forte de Copacabana | language=pt-br | trans-title=Centenary of the Copacabana Fort Revolt | first=Paulo Fernando Pinheiro | last=Machado | website=JOTA | date=2022-07-01 | access-date=2025-03-06 }}</ref><ref>{{ cite web | url=https://cpdoc.fgv.br/sites/default/files/verbetes/primeira-republica/REVOLTA%20DE%205%20DE%20JULHO%20DE%201922.pdf | title=Revolta de 5 de Julho de 1922 | language=pt-br | trans-title=Revolt of 5 July 1922 | first=Alzira Alves | last=de Abreu | website=[[CPDOC]] | date=2013-05-27 | access-date=2025-03-06 }}</ref>

On 5 July 1922, Epitácio Pessoa declared a [[State of emergency in Brazil (1922–1927)|state of emergency]] in response to the revolt,<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www2.camara.leg.br/legin/fed/decret/1920-1929/decreto-4549-5-julho-1922-568200-publicacaooriginal-91593-pl.html | title=Decreto Nº 4.549, de 5 de Julho de 1922 | language=pt-br | trans-title=Decree N.4,549, of 5 July 1922 | first=Epitacio | last=Pessôa | author-link=Epitácio Pessoa | website=[[Chamber of Deputies (Brazil)|Câmara dos Deputados]] | date=5 July 1922 | access-date=10 November 2024 }}</ref> which would end up lasting for years and multiple presidencies. Among other repercussions, the PCB's operation would soon be declared illegal a mere three months after its foundation.<ref name="principios-pcdob"/><!-- When exactly was it made illegal? ==== Communist uprising of 1935 ====
{{Main|Brazilian communist uprising of 1935}}
[[File:Intentona Comunista de 1935 - 3º Regimento de Infantaria em chamas.jpg|thumb|The 3rd Infantry Regiment barracks in Rio de Janeiro on fire after the communist insurrection]]
In 1935, a communist uprising ({{Langx|pt|Intentona Comunista}}) was carried out by the [[National Liberation Alliance (Brazil)|ANL]] (National Liberation Alliance) with the support of the PCB as well as the [[Communist International]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=2015-12-22 |title=A revolta comunista de 1935 {{!}} CPDOC |url=http://cpdoc.fgv.br/producao/dossies/FatosImagens/RevoltaComunista |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151222135755/http://cpdoc.fgv.br/producao/dossies/FatosImagens/RevoltaComunista |archive-date=22 December 2015 |access-date=2025-01-04}}</ref> Under the leadership of Luis Carlos Prestes, a prominent communist figure, the Comintern provided funding, propaganda, and youth programs, which helped the movement grow rapidly.<ref>{{cite book |last=Pinheiro |first=Paulo Sérgio |title=Estratégias da ilusão: a revolução mundial e o Brasil, 1922–1935 |date=1992 |publisher=Companhia das Letras |isbn=85-7164-165-X |oclc=26067080}}</ref> Revolts broke out in [[Natal, Rio Grande do Norte|Natal]] and [[Recife]] where citizens were provided weapons to fight alongside the revolutionaries.<ref>{{cite book |last=Sa Motta |first=Rodrigo |title=On Guard Against the Red Menace |year=2020}}</ref> In Natal, the revolt broke out on the 23rd of November due to a fake telegram by the counter-intelligence services which claimed that the date of the revolt was pushed forward from the 27th, and was contained within 4 days. In Recife on the 24th, the revolt begun and was dominated within 2 days. In [[Rio de Janeiro]] on the 27th, unaware of the status of the revolts in the north, the revolt begun and by 1:30, the rebels surrendered.<ref>{{cite web |title=The Communist Intentona: what is a fact and what is a rumor |url=http://www.pitoresco.com/historia/republ207.htm |url-status=usurped |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160303230554/http://www.pitoresco.com/historia/republ207.htm |archive-date=2016-03-03}}</ref> After November 1935, the National Congress of Brazil approved a series of laws that restricted its own power, while the executive gained almost unlimited powers of repression. This process culminated in the coup of November 10th 1937, which closed the National Congress of Brazil, canceled the upcoming 1938 presidential elections, and installed [[Getúlio Vargas]] as a dictator. The PCB faced significant repression during Getúlio Vargas's government following the failure of the insurgency.<ref>{{Cite journal |date=2022-04-11 |title=Parques e outras Unidades de Conservação da Região Metropolitana de São Paulo |doi=10.55881/cem.db.par001 |doi-access=free }}</ref>

==== World War II and onwards ====
Despite being driven underground, the PCB clandestinely supported [[Brazil in World War II|Brazilian involvement in World War II]],<ref>(Prestes, 2010: 51-52)</ref> due to which its membership increased to 82,000 and its leaders were released.<ref name=":0">{{cite web |title=John Newsinger: The Brazilian Communist Party (March 1975) |url=https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/isj/1975/no076/newsinger.htm |access-date=10 August 2021 |website=www.marxists.org}}</ref> It later underwent progressive and gradual reconstruction.<ref>Tribuna Popular, RJ, 27/6/1946, p. 1.</ref><ref>(Prestes, 2001: cap.IX)</ref>

=== Fourth Brazilian Republic ===
The PCB was banned once again during [[Eurico Gaspar Dutra]]'s government in 1947, as it maintained a significant presence in both rural and urban [[syndicalism]] and administration, and also due to Dutra's close ties to the [[United States|American]] government.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Welch |first=Clifford Andrew |title=Keeping Communism Down on the Farm: The Brazilian Rural Labor Movement during the Cold War |url=https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249693259 |journal=Latin American Perspectives |volume=33 |issue=3 |pages=28–50 |doi=10.1177/0094582X06287337 |via=ResearchGate}}</ref> During [[Jânio Quadros]]'s government in 1961, the party became legal once again, as it preferred not use violence to achieve its political goals.<ref name="Não_nomeado-xd4R-2">[https://blogdaboitempo.com.br/2016/11/18/cabo-dias-o-revolucionario-de-1935/ Cabo Dias, o revolucionário de 1935]</ref> In 1962, the PCB split due to the emergence of [[Communist Party of Brazil|a Maoist faction]]<ref>Later this faction began armed struggle during the military regime, see: (Marighella, 1979: 49, 58, 63, 104)</ref> which later adopted Marxist–Leninist principles.<ref name="Estatuto">[http://www.justicaeleitoral.jus.br/arquivos/tse-estatuto-do-partido-comunista-do-brasil.2011 TSE] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170216051000/http://www.justicaeleitoral.jus.br/arquivos/tse-estatuto-do-partido-comunista-do-brasil.2011|date=16 February 2017}} Estatuto do Partido Comunista do Brasil</ref> This faction opposed the PCB's strategy of prioritizing an urban-bourgeois revolution.<ref>{{Cite thesis |last=Vinicius Bandeira de Menezes |first=Marcus |title=Estrategias e taticas da revolução brasileira |date=2002 |publisher=Universidade Estadual de Campinas |doi=10.47749/t/unicamp.2002.229764|doi-access=free }}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |last=Prestes |first=Anita Leocádia |date=2011-05-30 |title=Da Declaração de Março de 1958 à renúncia de Jânio Quadros |journal=Crítica Marxista |volume=18 |issue=32 |pages=147–174 |doi=10.53000/cma.v18i32.19363 |issn=2966-0165|doi-access=free }}</ref>

During João Goulart's administration in Brazil, the PCB adopted distinct strategies across three phases. During the parliamentary phase, the PCB distanced itself from Goulart; in the early presidential phase, the communists continued to oppose him for supporting the [[Social Democratic Party (Brazil, 1945–1965)|PSD]] and proposed a "Single Front" of the left composed of the PCB, the Popular Mobilization Front (FMP), the General Workers' Command (CGT) and Miguel Arraes' faction. Goulart accepted the proposal and gained the PCB's support in late 1963.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Ferreira |first=Jorge |date= |title=The Brazilian Communist Party and João Goulart's Administration |url=https://www.scielo.br/j/rbh/a/VzG6xG7GSg4cWSBB7qSHCKQ/?lang=en |journal=Revista Brasileira de História |language=en |volume=33 |pages=113–134 |doi=10.1590/S0102-01882013000200007 |issn=0102-0188|doi-access=free }}</ref>

=== Coup of 1964 and onwards ===

==== Uprisings in the Brazilian military dictatorship, 1968-1972 ====
{{Main|Armed struggle against the Brazilian military dictatorship}}
During the [[Military dictatorship in Brazil|Brazilian military dictatorship]], following the enactment of [[Institutional Act Number Five|Institutional Act No. 5]] (AI-5), the Brazilian Communist Party (PCB), aligned with the [[Soviet Union]], belatedly and without proper preparation,<ref>(Moraes, 1989: 198)</ref> organized a congress in 1967. At this congress, the PCB resolved to support the coordination of demonstrations against martial law while the Communist Party of Brazil (PCdoB) pursued armed [[Guerrilla warfare|guerrilla]] warfare.{{citation needed|date=March 2025}}

An armed struggle occurred against the Brazilian military dictatorship by different left-wing groups between 1968 and 1972, the most severe phase of the regime. Despite its resistance aspect, the majority of the groups that participated in the armed struggle aimed to achieve a [[Communist revolution|socialist revolution]] in Brazil, inspired by the [[Chinese Communist Revolution|Chinese]] and [[Cuban Revolution|Cuban]] revolutions. The confrontation deepened after the enactment of [[Institutional Act Number Five]] (AI-5) in 1968.<ref name=":22">{{Cite web |title=ATO INSTITUCIONAL Nº 5, DE 13 DE DEZEMBRO DE 1968 |url=https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/ait/ait-05-68.htm |access-date=2024-02-13 |website=Federal Government of Brazil}}</ref>

Revolutionary organizations initially aimed to incite rural guerrilla warfare but became more notable for urban operations, which included fundraising activities and actions designed to support rural guerrilla campaigns and sustain clandestine infrastructure. Despite some initial successes, these groups became socially isolated as the regime's repression intensified, accompanied by a disinformation campaign designed to undermine public support for the rebels. Paramilitary groups linked to the government carried out [[false flag]] operations to justify further crackdowns and deepen authoritarianism. The armed actions in the cities were short-lived.<ref name=":20">{{Cite journal |last=Angelo |first=Vitor Amorim |year=2011 |title=Ditadura militar, esquerda armada e memória social no Brasil |url=https://repositorio.ufscar.br/bitstream/handle/ufscar/1420/3959.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y |journal=UFSCar}}</ref>

The dismantling of the [[Araguaia Guerrilla War|Araguaia guerrillas]], supported by the PCdoB, in 1974 marked the collapse of the armed struggle. The conflict resulted in numerous casualties, as well as many cases of exile, imprisonment, and enforced disappearances during the dictatorship.<ref name=":20" />

==== After 1972 ====
[[File:Bundesarchiv Bild 183-69234-0003, Luis Carlos Prestes.jpg|thumb|Luis Carlos Prestes, the leader of the PCB from 1943 to 1980]]
Between 1974 and 1976, during the height of the military government's repression, nearly 700 infiltrated militants were arrested, and more than 20 high-ranking communist leaders were killed. This wave of arrests forced the PCB into deeper clandestinity and exile. Consequently, [[Luís Carlos Prestes]] stepped down from the party's leadership,<ref>ALMEIDA, Antônio (pseudonym of Prestes), original typewritten document, untitled, 23 pgs, 08/04/1969, (Author's private file); copy in Luiz Carlos Prestes Collection, Edgard Leuenroth Archive/UNICAMP, folder 009.</ref> transferring it to Giocondo Dias. Despite President [[João Figueiredo]]'s political liberalization, communist activists traveling to the Soviet Union continued to face arrests upon their return.<ref name="Não_nomeado-xd4R-2" />

Brazilian cultural production—particularly in dramaturgy, soap operas, and cinema—was notably influenced by communist militant authors.<ref>PRESTES, Luiz Carlos. "Letter to Aloyzio Neiva", Rio, 16/01/1983, 3 pgs.; original document, typed. (Author's private file)

</ref> However, their efforts were hampered by inexperience, police surveillance during periods of persecution, and the detachment of exiles from the country's evolving social and political landscape.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Carone |first=Edgar |title=O PCB-1964-1982 |year=1982 |location=Sao Paulo |language=pt}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book |last=Gaspari |first=E. |title=A Ditadura Derrotada |publisher=Companhia da Letras |year=2003 |location=Sao Paulo |language=pt}}</ref> Despite severe repression, communist influence remained strong in labor movements, notably within the [[Volkswagen]] union, which represented workers in the country's largest company at the time.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Costa |first=E. |title=A Política Salarial no Brasil |publisher=Boitempo |year=1998 |location=Sao Paulo |language=pt}}</ref>

During the Diretas Já campaign for direct elections in the early 1980s,<ref>{{Cite web |title=Arquivos do Dops dizem que Lula barrou a entrada de Luís Carlos Prestes no PT – Brasil – R7 |url=http://noticias.r7.com/brasil/noticias/arquivos-do-dops-dizem-que-lula-barrou-a-entrada-de-luis-carlos-prestes-no-pt-20111004.html |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170731004817/http://noticias.r7.com/brasil/noticias/arquivos-do-dops-dizem-que-lula-barrou-a-entrada-de-luis-carlos-prestes-no-pt-20111004.html |archive-date=2017-07-31 |access-date=2025-01-04 |website=noticias.r7.com |language=pt-br}}</ref> the PCB actively supported the movement.<ref>{{Cite book |last=MAZZEO |first=A. C. |title=As tarefas históricas da esquerda brasileira e o Partido dos Trabalhadores |year=2004 |location=Sao Paulo}}</ref> However, during the [[1978–1980 ABC Paulista strikes]], the [[Workers' Party (Brazil)|Workers' Party]] (PT) rapidly gained prominence, taking advantage of the PCB's inability to adapt to new political realities.<ref>PRESTES, Luiz Carlos, "Declarations" (unrevised transcription) in "Political Resolutions of the 3rd State Meeting of the Gaucho Communists Who Are Guided by the Letter to the Communists of Comrade Luiz Carlos Prestes" (January/1984), typewritten document (xerox copy), 28 pgs.</ref> The PCB's decline was compounded by inexperienced leadership and a lack of deep political and methodological reflection on Brazil's changing context.<ref>{{Cite book |last=ANTUNES |first=R |title=A Rebelião no Trabalho |publisher=Editora Unicamp |year=1992 |location=Sao Paulo |language=pt}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book |title=O Sindicalismo Brasileiro nos anos 80 |collaboration=Boito Jr |publisher=Paz e Terra |year=1991 |location=Sao Paulo |language=pt}}</ref>

As the PT rose to power in state and municipal governments throughout the 1990s, it ceded itself to the tools of traditional politics and disillusioned its former remaining communist members who broke away from it,<ref>{{Cite book |title=Resoluções de Encontro e Congressos: 1979–1998 |publisher=Fundação Perseu Abramo |year=2000 |location=Sao Paulo |language=pt}}</ref> thus creating a new social-democratic tradition aimed at countries dependent on the [[First World]].<ref>{{Cite book |last=DOS SANTOS |first=T. |title=Teoria da Dependência, Balanço e Perspectiva |publisher=Civilização Brasileira |year=2000 |location=Rio de Janeiro |language=pt}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book |last=MARINI |first=R. M. |title=Dialética de la Mercancia e Teoria del Valor |publisher=Editorial Universitária Centroamericana |year=1982 |location=Mexico |language=pt}}</ref> In the late 1990s, an attempt was made by some PCB leaders to dissolve the party through a vote by non-affiliated members, but the effort ultimately failed.<ref name="dm-importancia"/>

=== Modern day ===
[[File:Fora Bolsonaro em Campinas 29.05.2021 33.jpg|thumb|In March 2021, protests calling for former president [[Jair Bolsonaro]]'s impeachment for mishandling the pandemic break out in over 200 Brazilian cities as [[COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil|COVID-19]] deaths surpass 460 thousand<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www.theguardian.com/world/2021/may/29/tens-of-thousands-of-brazilians-march-to-demand-bolsonaros-impeachment | title=Tens of thousands of Brazilians march to demand Bolsonaro's impeachment | first=Tom | last=Phillips | location=[[Rio de Janeiro]] | website=[[The Guardian]] | date=29 May 2021 | access-date=4 November 2024 }}</ref>]]

Communist parties such as the PCB and the PCdoB were part of [[Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva|Lula]]'s coalition governments in 2002 and 2006.<ref name=":6">{{Citation |last=Bethell |first=Leslie |chapter=The failure of the Left in Brazil |date=2018 |title=Brazil: Essays on History and Politics |pages=195–222 |chapter-url=https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv51309x.11 |access-date=2025-01-07 |publisher=University of London Press |jstor=j.ctv51309x.11 |isbn=978-1-908857-54-5}}</ref> In 2006, [[Aldo Rebelo]], a PCdoB member and [[President of the Chamber of Deputies (Brazil)|President of the Chamber of Deputies]], served briefly as acting [[President of Brazil]] when both the President and Vice President underwent medical procedures. During the 2006 elections, PCdoB secured its first executive office in a state capital by winning the mayoral race in [[Aracaju]], the capital of [[Sergipe]].<ref name=":1">{{Cite web |last=Britt |first=M. P. |date=2022-04-06 |title=Communist Party of Brazil celebrates 100 years of struggle for socialism |url=https://www.cpusa.org/article/communist-party-of-brazil-celebrates-100-years-of-struggle-for-socialism/ |access-date=2025-01-07 |website=Communist Party USA |language=en-US}}</ref> [[Manuela d'Ávila]], as a member of PCdoB, was the running mate for [[Fernando Haddad]] for the [[2018 Brazilian general election]] against [[Jair Bolsonaro]].<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/colunas/monicabergamo/2018/08/confirmacao-da-chapa-haddad-manuela-davila-deixa-psol-preocupado.shtml | title=Confirmação da chapa Haddad-Manuela D'Ávila deixa PSOL preocupado | language=pt-br | trans-title=Confirmation of Haddad–Manuela D'Ávila ticket leaves PSOL worried | first=Mônica | last=Bergamo | website=[[Folha de S.Paulo]] | date=2018-08-08 | access-date=2025-03-06 }}</ref>

Currently, PCdoB remains a member of [[Brazil of Hope]], a coalition that includes Lula's party, the [[Worker's Party (Brazil)|Worker's Party]] (PT).<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www.tse.jus.br/partidos/federacoes-registradas-no-tse/brasil-da-esperanca-fe-brasil | title=Federação Brasil da Esperança (FE BRASIL) | language=pt-br | trans-title=Brazil of Hope Federation | website=[[Superior Electoral Court]] | access-date=2025-03-06 }}</ref> Additionally, it is the second-largest non-ruling communist party in the world.<ref name=":1"/>

The PCB underwent a major crisis in 2023, after controversial expulsions of prominent party figures, and the opposition of its youth wing, the Union of Communist Youth (UJC). The party's leadership thus faced internal divisions and accusations of undemocratic practices.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2023-08-21 |title=Letter: Crisis in the Brazilian Communist Party |url=https://cosmonautmag.com/2023/08/letter-crisis-in-the-brazilian-communist-party/ |access-date=2025-01-18 |website=Cosmonaut Magazine|language=en-US}}</ref> In July 2023, [[Ivan Pinheiro]] was one of those who were expelled from the PCB.<ref name=":5">{{Cite web |date=2023-07-31 |title=PCB expulsa Jones Manoel e mais quatro membros |url=https://www.cartacapital.com.br/politica/pcb-expulsa-jones-manoel-e-mais-quatro-membros/ |access-date=2024-12-27 |website=CartaCapital |language=pt-BR}}</ref> He subsequently founded his own party, the Brazilian Communist Party – Revolutionary Reconstruction (PCB-RR).<ref name=":2">{{Cite web |last=Marxism |first=In Defence of |date=2024-06-10 |title=Historic leader of Brazilian Communist Party sends warm greetings to RCI founding conference |url=https://marxist.com/historic-leader-of-brazilian-communist-party-sends-warm-greetings-sent-to-rci-founding-conference.htm |access-date=2024-12-19 |website=In Defence of Marxism |language=en-gb}}</ref>

== Anti-communist sentiment ==
{{Main|Anti-communism}}<!-- Send to [[Anti-communism in Brazil]] if that ever gets made; currently a redirect (Nov.2024) -->
[[File:07 09 2021 Comemoração do dia da Independência (51435073416) (cropped).jpg|thumb|[[Independence Day (Brazil)|Independence Day]] demonstrations in 2021, in support of then-president [[Jair Bolsonaro]], with signs saying "out communists" and "no to communism"]]

Brazil still faces somewhat strong {{Interlanguage link|Communist threat in Brazil|lt=anti-communist|pt|Ameaça comunista no Brasil}} sentiment, even decades after the end of the multiple military dictatorships that sought to curb it.<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www.cartacapital.com.br/politica/esquerda-volver-o-que-une-e-divide-up-pco-pstu-e-pcb/ | title=Esquerda, volver! O que une (e separa) UP, PCO, PSTU e PCB | language=pt-br | first=Victor | last=Ohana | website=[[CartaCapital]] | date=2019-12-18 | access-date=2025-02-28 }}</ref> Near the end of [[Dilma Rousseff]]'s presidency and the [[2015–2016 protests in Brazil|mass protests]] from late 2014 through 2016, the expression ''{{ill|Nossa bandeira jamais será vermelha|pt}}'' ("[[Flag of Brazil|Our flag]] will never be [[Red flag (politics)|red]]") became a popular anti-communist motto.<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://brasil.elpais.com/brasil/2014/11/16/politica/1416094049_851005.html | title=Movimento contra Dilma cresce ao mesmo tempo que fica mais radical | language=pt-br | trans-title=Movement against Dilma grows while becoming more radical | first=Afonso | last=Benites | website=[[El País]] | date=15 November 2014 | access-date=16 November 2024 }}</ref><ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www.bbc.com/portuguese/noticias/2015/03/150315_cartazes_protestos_pai | title=15 frases dos protestos pró e anti-Dilma | language=pt-br | first1=Camilla | last1=Costa | first2=Luis Guilherme | last2=Barrucho | first3=Jefferson | last3=Puff | website=[[BBC News]] | date=15 March 2015 | access-date=16 November 2024 }}</ref>

That same motto's popularity was later revitalized during [[Jair Bolsonaro]]'s presidency.<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://valor.globo.com/politica/noticia/2019/01/01/nossa-bandeira-jamais-sera-vermelha-afirma-bolsonaro-na-posse.ghtml | title='Nossa bandeira jamais será vermelha', afirma Bolsonaro na posse | language=pt-br | first1=Maria Cristina | last1=Fernandes | first2=Carla | last2=Araújo | first3=Cristiane | last3=Agostine | first4=Malu | last4=Filgueiras | website=[[Valor Econômico]] | date=1 January 2019 | access-date=16 November 2024 }}</ref><ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www.poder360.com.br/governo/nossa-bandeira-jamais-sera-vermelha-diz-michelle-bolsonaro/ | title="Nossa bandeira jamais será vermelha", diz Michelle Bolsonaro | language=pt-br | first=Vinícius | last=Nunes | website={{ill|Poder360|pt}} | date=7 September 2022 | access-date=16 November 2024 }}</ref> Bolsonaro, a staunch anti-communist, had previously stated that Brazil "could become like [[North Korea]] if the Workers' Party was not stopped".<ref name="theatlantic-bolsonaro-anticommunism">{{ cite web | url=https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2020/09/how-anti-communist-conspiracies-haunt-brazil/614665/ | title=How Anti-Communist Conspiracies Haunt Brazil | first=Vincent | last=Bevins | others=Illustrated by Eren Su Kibele Yarman | website=[[The Atlantic]] | date=16 September 2020
 | access-date=16 November 2024 }}</ref> His presidential campaign in 2018 was filled with fake news aiming at a red scare, such as alleging his opponent, [[Fernando Haddad]], was a [[Marxism|Marxist]].<ref name="theatlantic-bolsonaro-anticommunism"/>

In a survey in 2023, after [[Lula]]'s win over Bolsonaro in the [[2022 Brazilian general election|2022 election]], around 70% of Bolsonaro voters believed the country was at risk of becoming communist.<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/internacional/en/brazil/2023/07/52-thinks-brazil-is-at-risk-of-becoming-communist-says-datafolha.shtml | title=52% thinks Brazil is at risk of becoming communist, says Datafolha | first=Igor | last=Gielow | translator-first=Cassy | translator-last=Dias | website=[[Folha de S.Paulo]] | date=3 July 2023 | access-date=16 November 2024  }}</ref> This is in stark contrast with Lula's actual political positioning, which is closer to center-left.<ref>{{ cite web | url=https://www.dw.com/en/brazils-lula-da-silva-the-communist-who-wasnt/a-64240797 | title=Brazil's Lula da Silva: the communist who wasn't | first=Oliver | last=Pieper | website=[[Deutsche Welle]] | date=29 December 2022 | access-date=16 November 2024 }}</ref>
