'''''New York, New York''''' is a 1977 American [[Musical film|musical]] [[romantic comedy]] film directed by [[Martin Scorsese]] from a screenplay by [[Earl Mac Rauch]] and [[Mardik Martin]], based on a story by Rauch. [[John Kander]] and [[Fred Ebb]] wrote several songs for the film, including "[[Theme from New York, New York|New York, New York]]", which became a worldwide hit and [[Standard (music)|standard]]. A tribute to Scorsese's hometown of [[New York City]], the film stars [[Liza Minnelli]] and [[Robert De Niro]] as a pair of musicians and lovers.

==Plot==
On [[V-J Day]] in 1945, a celebration in a New York City nightclub is underway, with music provided by the [[Tommy Dorsey Orchestra]]. Selfish and smooth-talking [[saxophone]] player Jimmy Doyle pesters small-time [[USO]] singer Francine Evans for her phone number, but she wants nothing to do with him.

The next morning, Francine and Jimmy end up sharing a cab and, against her will, Francine accompanies Jimmy to an audition, at which he argues with the club owner. In an attempt to get the audition back on track, Francine begins to sing the old standard "[[You Brought a New Kind of Love to Me]]". Jimmy joins in on his sax. The club owner is impressed and, to Francine's astonishment, they are both offered a job as a boy–girl act in a club. 

But the next morning Jimmy receives a message from Francine telling him that she's left to join a traveling orchestra. He catches up with her and joins the orchestra as well, which he eventually takes over as his own when the conductor retires.

From that moment, Jimmy and Francine's relationship deepens into a mix of obsession and love. But there are problems—mainly, Jimmy's tendency to fight with his co-workers, his overly dramatic behavior, and his increasingly violent arguments with Francine, who becomes pregnant with their child. An especially harsh shouting match between them results in Francine going into labor. Jimmy rushes her to the hospital, where she delivers a boy. However, Jimmy is not ready to be a father or a good husband, so he abandons his wife and declines to see his newborn son as he leaves the hospital.

Several years later, in a recording studio, Francine records "But the World Goes Round," a powerful anthem that makes the charts and helps her become a popular entertainment figure. In the years that follow, Jimmy and Francine both find success in the music industry; he becomes a renowned [[jazz musician]] and club owner, while she becomes a successful singer and film actress.

Jimmy records a saxophone version of a self-written song, "New York, New York," and it tops the [[jazz]] charts. One night, he goes to see Francine perform in the nightclub where they had first met, years earlier; Francine's performance of "New York, New York," for which she has provided lyrics, is received by a wildly appreciative audience. After the show, Jimmy and Francine have a brief, awkward reunion, and then Jimmy leaves, spending a few moments with his and Francine's young son on the way out of the club. Shortly thereafter Jimmy telephones his ex-wife from a pay phone in the street, suggesting she join him for dinner. Francine says yes, but as she heads toward the stage-door exit, she changes her mind and leaves the club via another route. Jimmy, waiting on the sidewalk, realizes that he has been stood up and walks down the street.

==Production==
[[Irwin Winkler]]'s purchase of the screenplay for ''New York, New York'' caught Martin Scorsese's attention before he began filming ''[[Taxi Driver]]''.<ref name=SoS/> Winkler and [[Robert Chartoff]] had agreed to a four-year contract with [[United Artists]], at which they would release a minimum of 12 films, and ''New York, New York'' was the first that was produced under the deal. They announced Scorsese as the director in April 1975, with a budget of $3.5 million.<ref>Tusher, Will. "Exclusive UA Contract Lands Chartoff, Winkler", ''The Hollywood Reporter''. April 23, 1975. 1.</ref> [[Lynn Stalmaster]] was the [[casting director]].<ref>"Stallmaster Casting On 'New York' Film", ''The Hollywood Reporter''. April 30, 1976. 23.</ref> [[Principal photography|Filming]] began on June 14, 1976.<ref>"Film Production." ''[[The Hollywood Reporter]]'' vol. 243, no. 40. October 29, 1976. 10.</ref>

Scorsese wanted to marry the [[movie musical]] of his parents' generation with a [[documentary]] style. He hoped that the [[juxtaposition]] would emphasize the continuity between human relationships through history.<ref>Flatley, Guy. "[https://timesmachine.nytimes.com/timesmachine/1977/06/24/356222932.html?zoom=16 At the Movies]", ''[[New York Times]]''. June 24, 1977.</ref> The actors would improvise on the script in front of the massive, unrealistic sets that fit the musical style. Having recently won the [[Palme d'Or]], Scorsese felt that he could improve the script during filming, but his excesses led to mistakes, such as the opening V-J Day sequence being overly long. The initial cut of the film was four and a half hours.<ref name=SoS>Scorses, Martin. ''[https://archive.org/details/scorseseonscorse0000scor/page/68/mode/1up Scorsese on Scorsese]''. Faber, 1996. 68–73.</ref>

Scorsese's [[cocaine]] addiction made matters worse, and according to [[Peter Biskind]], the director was also taking [[lithium]] to control his anger. Scorsese lamented, "I was just too drugged out to resolve the structure."<ref>Biskind, Peter. ''[[Easy Riders, Raging Bulls]]''. Bloomsbury, 1999. 339.</ref> He stopped press interviews one day short because, as he explained, he had run out of cocaine.<ref name=Power>Power, Ed. "A Cocaine-Fuelled Folly: Why New York, New York almost Killed Martin Scorsese." Telegraph.co.uk. May 8, 2023.</ref> He concluded, "It's a miracle that the film makes any kind of sense."<ref name=SoS/>

The director had an affair with Liza Minnelli during filming, and his second wife [[Julia Cameron]] was often on set in an attempt to catch them. The improvisation of dialogue was an additional strain on Minnelli, who was not used to [[method acting]]. The stress is sometimes visible in her scenes with De Niro.<ref name=Power/> Before their relationship ended, Scorsese directed Minnelli in ''[[The Act (musical)|The Act]]'', a [[Kander and Ebb]] musical that some saw as a [[Spinoff (media)|spinoff]] of this film.<ref>Kroll, Jack. "[https://archive.org/details/newsweek90novnewy/page/n241/mode/2up At Long Last, Liza]", ''Newsweek''. November 14, 1977. 99.</ref>

Robert De Niro studied the saxophone with [[Georgie Auld]], a veteran of [[Swing music|swing]] giants [[Artie Shaw]] and [[Benny Goodman]]'s bands. Auld also played bandleader Frankie Hart in the film. De Niro was so demanding of Auld's time that the musician felt like a "slave", and his wife worried that the actor would be joining them in bed with the instrument.<ref>Kroll, Jack. "[https://archive.org/details/newsweek89maynewy/page/n288/mode/1up De Niro: A Star for the '70s]", ''[[Newsweek]]''. May 16, 1977. 80–6.</ref> Auld recorded the saxophone parts in the film, and De Niro mimed to them on set. Auld stood off-camera and would make a slashing motion if De Niro made a fingering or breathing mistake.<ref>Tait, R. Colin. "Robert De Niro's 'Raging Bull': The History Of A Performance And A Performance Of History." ''Revue Canadienne d'Études Cinématographiques/Canadian Journal of Film Studies'', vol. 20, no. 1, 2011. 38.</ref>

The film cost $9 million, which was $2 million over the original budget, and was a much larger amount than the budgets for Scorsese's previous films. [[George Lucas]] predicted that the [[box office|gross]] would improve by $10 million if Scorsese would change the ending to a happy one.<ref name=SoS/>

Scorsese considered having a [[cameo appearance]] in the film, but declined because he would have had to shave his beard.{{sfn|Wilson|2011|p=73}}

==Releases==
The film was released on June 21, 1977, with a running time of 155 minutes. The [[Box-office bomb|box-office failure]] of the film prompted [[United Artists]] to shorten the film to 136 minutes for [[Europe]].

In 1981, the film was re-released with a runtime of 163 minutes. Scorsese had spent $350,000 of the budget on filming a musical-within-a-musical called "Happy Endings", which depicts Francine Evans as a movie star. The twelve-minute sequence was [[choreographed]] by [[Ron Field]].<ref>Rathbun, Keith. "'New York, New York' See it! See it!", ''[[Cleveland Scene|Scene]]''. July 26-August 3, 1977. 11.</ref> The scene was restored in the 1981 version, and the expanded film earned praise for its ironic look at Hollywood musicals.<ref name=SoS/>

==Music==
===Theme song===
[[Fred Ebb]] and [[John Kander]]'s initial submission for the theme song was deemed so bad that Robert De Niro rejected it outright. The lyrics began, "They always say it's a nice place to visit, but I wouldn't want to live here", and the melody was completely different.<ref name=JL>Leve, James. ''Kander and Ebb''. Yale University Press, 2009.</ref>{{rp|310–1}} The eventual song, "[[Theme from New York, New York|Theme from ''New York, New York'']]", begins with one of Kander's famous vamps, derived from the [[ragtime]] practice of putting the melody underneath a repeated note.<ref name=JL/>{{rp|25–6}}

The song was released as a single from the [[soundtrack album]], and peaked at #104 on the [[Billboard (magazine)|''Billboard'']] chart.<ref>{{cite book|title=The Billboard Book of Top 40 Hits|last=Whitburn |first=Joel |author-link=Joel Whitburn |year=2000 |publisher=[[Billboard Books]] |location=United States |isbn=9780823076901 |page=576 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=drgq4g0rmVsC&q=bibliogroup:%22BILLBOARD+BOOK+OF+TOP+FORTY+HITS%22|quote=Theme From New York, New York" introduced in the movie musical ''New York, New York'' by Liza Minnelli (her version "Bubbled Under" at #104 in 1977).|accessdate=August 18, 2012 }}</ref> Two years later, [[Frank Sinatra]] recorded a [[cover version]] for his [[triple album]] ''[[Trilogy: Past Present Future]]''. On June 14, 1980, the single reached #32 on the [[Billboard Hot 100|''Billboard'' Hot 100]] and was Sinatra's last [[Top 40]] hit.<ref>{{Cite magazine |date=June 14, 1980 |title=Billboard Hot 100 |url=https://www.billboard.com/charts/hot-100/1980-06-14/ |access-date=2024-06-06 |magazine=Billboard}}</ref> Both Sinatra's and Minnelli's versions have become closely associated with Manhattan in New York City. Sinatra performed the number at nearly all of his concerts until his retirement in 1995, and Minnelli continues to perform it at nearly all of hers.{{Update inline|date=May 2025|?=yes}}

==Reception==
===Box office===
The film [[Box-office bomb|underperformed to expectations at the box office]], grossing $16.4 million against a $9 million budget. The disappointment [[Depression (mood)|depressed]] Scorsese and worsened his drug addiction.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.scorsesefilms.com/article15.htm| title=Liza Minelli [sic] on New York New York| date=July 10, 2006| archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20060710091117/http://www.scorsesefilms.com/article15.htm| archive-date=July 10, 2006|url-status=usurped| website=Scorsese Films}}</ref> Certain that ''New York, New York'' would be a hit, United Artists structured the film's box office as a bulwark against the flop that they had expected in ''[[Rocky]]''. The two productions pooled their profits, but ''Rocky'' ended up covering the losses of Scorsese's movie.<ref>{{cite magazine| title=EW: The Right Hook: How Rocky Nabbed Best Picture| url=https://ew.com/article/2002/02/19/how-rocky-nabbed-best-picture/| magazine=[[Entertainment Weekly]]| first=Chris| last=Nashawaty| date=2002-02-19| df=mdy}}</ref>

In his introduction to the film's [[DVD]] release, Scorsese explains that he intended for the film, which he saw as an [[Homage (arts)|homage]] to the [[musical film|musicals]] of [[Classical Hollywood cinema]], to break from the gritty realism for which he had become famous, hence the deliberately artificial sets and storyline. He acknowledges that the experiment did not please everyone.<ref>Chartoff, Robert, Irwin Winkler, Martin Scorsese, Earl Mac Rauch, Mardik Martin, Liza Minnelli, Robert De Niro, et al., dirs. 2007. ''New York, New York''. Distributed by Twentieth Century Fox Home Entertainment.</ref>

===Critical response===

The film has an overall critical score of 57% with a mean of 6.8 of a possible 10 on [[Rotten Tomatoes]], based on 54 reviews; the site's consensus explains, "Martin Scorsese's technical virtuosity and Liza Minnelli's magnetic presence are on full display in ''New York, New York'', although this ambitious musical's blend of swooning style and hard-bitten realism makes for a queasy mixture."<ref>{{rotten-tomatoes|new_york_new_york|New York, New York}}</ref>

[[Christopher Porterfield]] wrote in ''[[Time (magazine)|Time]]'', "If this movie were a big-band arrangement, it would be a duet for a sax man and a girl singer, but with the soloists in a different key from the band."<ref>{{cite magazine| last1=Porterfield| first1=Christopher| title=Cinema: Dissonant Duet| url=http://content.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,915094,00.html| magazine=Time| date=27 June 1977| url-access=subscription}}</ref>

[[Vincent Canby]] of ''[[The New York Times]]'' wondered, "Why should a man of Mr. Scorsese's talent be giving us what amounts to no more than a film buff's essay on a pop-film form that was never, at any point in film history, of the first freshness?"<ref name="nytimes">{{cite news| date=June 23, 1977| first=Vincent| last=Canby| title=Film: 'New York' In a Tuneful Era| url=https://www.nytimes.com/1977/06/23/archives/film-new-york-in-a-tuneful-era.html| newspaper=New York Times| url-access=subscription}}</ref>

[[Roger Ebert]] of the ''[[Chicago Sun-Times]]'' lamented, "Scorsese's New York, New York never pulls itself together into a coherent whole, but if we forgive the movie its confusions we're left with a good time."<ref>{{cite news| date=January 1, 1977| last1=Ebert| first1=Roger| title=New York, New York Movie Review (1977)| url=https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/new-york-new-york-1977| newspaper=Chicago Sun-Times| via=RogerEbert.com}}</ref>

In the ''[[Chicago Reader]]'', [[Dave Kehr]] concluded, "Scorsese created a very handsome and dynamic film, but the spectacular set pieces don't add up to much."<ref>Kehr, Dave. "[https://chicagoreader.com/film/new-york-new-york-2/ New York, New York]", ''Chicago Reader''. July 5, 1985.</ref>

''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' raved, "A final burst from Old Hollywood, Minnelli tears into the title song and it's a wowser."<ref>{{cite news| date=1 January 1977| title=New York New York| url= https://variety.com/1976/film/reviews/new-york-new-york-1200423960/| magazine=Variety}}</ref>

''[[Time Out (magazine)|Time Out's]]'' [[Geoff Andrew]] enthused, "Scorsese's tribute/parody/critique of the MGM musical is a razor-sharp dissection of the conventions of both meeting-cute romances and rags-to-riches biopics.<ref>{{cite news| url=https://www.timeout.com/film/reviews/63278/new_york_new_york.html| title=New York, New York| date=September 10, 2012| magazine=Time Out| archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121107132714/https://www.timeout.com/film/reviews/63278/new_york_new_york.html| archive-date=November 7, 2012}}</ref>

Independent reviewer [[Gene Shalit]] said that the argument between Francine and Jimmy in the parked car was the most realistic he had ever seen on film.<ref name="MKC">Connelly, Marie K. ''The Films of Martin Scorsese: A Critical Study''. [[Case Western Reserve University]]. 1991. 103.</ref>

In ''[[Cinéaste (magazine)|Cinéaste]]'', Leonard and Barbara Quart called the film "an interesting and at sometimes exciting failure..." They pointed out the self-conscious parallels with the work of Liza's mother in ''[[A Star Is Born (1954 film)|A Star Is Born]]'', and praised Scorsese's "stylized settings (gold tinsel snowfalls, claustrophobic reddish interiors, and spotlit, dream-like musical solos)", but felt that they were "too calculated and without purpose".<ref>Quart, Leonard, and Barbara Quart. ''Cinéaste'', vol. 8, no. 2, 1977, pp. 44–45.</ref> William Harding heaped blame on De Niro's performance: "[He] zooms in on the role as if he were playing [[Hamlet]]. His hard work backfires...The character of Jimmy Doyle is completely obscured by the spectacle of DeNiro attempting to come to grips with an impossibly one-note role."<ref name="NGL">Harding, William. "Screenings" ''New Gay Life'' 1, no. 5 (August 1, 1977).</ref>

==Stage musicals==
===Brazilian musical===
The film was adapted into a [[stage musical]] in Brazil. It premiered on April 14, 2011, in [[Teatro Bradesco]] in [[São Paulo]], with direction by José Possi Neto. The songs were not translated, instead featuring subtitles projected on a digital panel.<ref>{{cite news| url=https://www.broadwayworld.com/brazil/article/-Scorseses-NEW-YORK-NEW-YORK-among-the-shows-opening-in-Brazil-in-2011-20110122| title=Scorsese's NEW YORK NEW YORK among the shows opening in Brazil in 2011!| first=Paulo Afonso| last=DeLima| website=[[BroadwayWorld]]| access-date=5 July 2024}}</ref>

===Broadway musical===
{{Main|New York, New York (musical)}}
The film was adapted into a stage musical in the United States. It opened on [[Broadway theater|Broadway]] on April 26, 2023, at the [[St. James Theater]], following [[Preview (theatre)|previews]] that began on March 24.<ref>{{cite news| url=https://deadline.com/2022/08/lin-manuel-miranda-new-york-new-york-musical-john-kander-1235099489/| title=Lin-Manuel Miranda Teams With John Kander On New Broadway Musical Loosely Based On Martin Scorsese's 'New York, New York'; Susan Stroman Directing| first=Baz| last=Bamigboye| date=August 25, 2022}}</ref><ref>{{cite news| url=https://deadline.com/2022/10/kander-ebb-broadway-musical-york-new-york-sets-opening-date-1235155150/| title=New Kander & Ebb Musical 'New York, New York' Sets Spring Broadway Opening, Venue|first=Greg| last=Evans| date=October 26, 2022}}</ref>

The production received mixed reviews, with ''[[Entertainment Weekly]]'' saying that the musical was "deeply flawed, but deeply entertaining", and [[Elisabeth Vincentelli]] of ''The New York Times'' saying that the show was "The Big Apple, Without Bite".<ref>{{cite news| title=New York, New York review: Start spreadin' the news... there's a new Phantom on Broadway| url=https://ew.com/theater/theater-reviews/new-york-new-york-broadway-review/| first=Lester Fabian| last=Brathwaite| magazine=Entertainment Weekly| date=April 26, 2023| access-date=5 July 2024}}</ref><ref>{{cite news| title='New York, New York Review: The Big Apple Without Bite| url=https://www.nytimes.com/2023/04/26/theater/new-york-new-york-review-broadway.html| first=Elisabeth| last=Vincintelli| newspaper=The New York Times| date=April 26, 2023| access-date=5 July 2024| url-access=subscription}}</ref>

The ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' said that "the film is not good. The new Broadway musical is worse."<ref>{{cite news| title='New York, New York' the film is not good. The new Broadway musical is worse| url=https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/story/2023-04-26/new-york-new-york-broadway-musical-review| first=Charles| last=McNulty| date=April 26, 2023| newspaper=[[Los Angeles Times]]| access-date=5 July 2024}}</ref> Due to the poor reviews, lack of [[76th Tony Awards|Tony Award wins]], and high production costs, producers posted a hasty announcement on July 23, 2023, stating that the musical would close on July 30.<ref>{{cite news| title=New York, New York to Close on Broadway| url=https://playbill.com/article/new-york-new-york-to-close-on-broadway| first=Diep| last=Tran| date=July 23, 2023| magazine=[[Playbill]]| access-date=5 July 2024}}</ref>
