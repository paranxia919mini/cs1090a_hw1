'''''Lost in Translation''''' is a 2003 [[romantic comedy drama]] film{{refn|The film has been noted for its resistance to any single genre classification. While it has been labeled in terms such as "[[romantic comedy]]", the film has been identified for integrating elements from multiple genres, including romance, comedy, and drama. For one discussion of ''Lost in Translation''{{'}}s position between genres, see {{harvnb|King|2010|pp=60–75}}.|group="note"}} written and directed by [[Sofia Coppola]]. [[Bill Murray]] stars as Bob Harris, a fading American movie star who is having a [[midlife crisis]] when he travels to [[Tokyo]] to promote [[Suntory]] whiskey. He befriends another disillusioned American, Charlotte ([[Scarlett Johansson]]), a recent college graduate who has been married for two years. The film explores themes of alienation and disconnection against a backdrop of [[Culture shock|cultural displacement]] in [[Japan]]. It does not use mainstream narrative conventions and is atypical in its depiction of romance.<ref name="haslem"/>

Coppola started writing the film after spending time in Tokyo and becoming fond of the city. She began forming a story about two characters experiencing a "romantic melancholy"<ref name="olsen" /> in the [[Park Hyatt Tokyo]], where she stayed while promoting her first feature film, the 1999 drama ''[[The Virgin Suicides (film)|The Virgin Suicides]]''. Coppola envisioned Murray playing the role of Bob from the beginning and tried to recruit him for months. While Murray eventually agreed to play the part, he did not sign a contract. Coppola spent a quarter of the film's $4 million budget without knowing if he would arrive.

[[Principal photography]] began on September 29, 2002, and lasted 27 days. Coppola kept a flexible schedule during filming with a small crew and minimal equipment. The screenplay was short and Coppola allowed significant improvisation during filming. The [[director of photography]], [[Lance Acord]], used [[available light]] where possible, and many Japanese places of business and public areas were used as locations. After 10 weeks of editing, Coppola sold distribution rights for the United States and Canada to [[Focus Features]], and the company promoted the film through [[Word-of-mouth marketing|word of mouth]].

''Lost in Translation'' premiered on August 29, 2003, at the [[Telluride Film Festival]], and was distributed to American theatres on September 12, 2003. At the [[76th Academy Awards]], ''Lost in Translation'' won Coppola [[Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay|Best Original Screenplay]], and the film was nominated for [[Academy Award for Best Picture|Best Picture]], [[Academy Award for Best Director|Best Director]] and [[Academy Award for Best Actor|Best Actor]] (Murray). Other accolades won include three [[British Academy Film Awards|BAFTAs]] and three [[Golden Globes]]. It has since been named one of the [[List of films voted the best|greatest films]] of the 2000s and 21st century. The film received acclaim for its writing, direction, and performances, and grossed $119 million worldwide.

''Lost in Translation'' drew criticism for its depiction of [[Japanese people]]; critics and scholars in both the United States and Japan accused the film of racism, [[Orientalism]] and [[Stereotypes of East Asians in the United States|anti-Asian stereotyping]]. This backlash led to organized political pushback as an [[Asian American movement]] during the 2003–2004 film awards season, which included a nationwide boycott and petition campaign launched by an [[Asian Pacific Americans|Asian American and Pacific Islander]] (AAPI) organization against major motion picture industry groups at the 76th Academy Awards.{{sfn|King|2010|p=132}} The retrospective prompted a critical re-evaluation of the film's legacy, renewing media coverage and public debate regarding its controversial themes.<!-- NOTE: This statement is properly supported by multiple reliable sources in "Legacy and impact". ==Plot==
 <!-- Per WP:FILMPLOT, plot summary should be between 400 to 700 words. 395-->
Bob Harris is a [[Occupational burnout|burnt out]] American movie star who arrives in [[Tokyo]] to appear in lucrative advertisements for [[Suntory]]'s [[Hibiki (whiskey)|''Hibiki'' whiskey]]. He stays at the  [[Park Hyatt Tokyo]] and is miserable due to problems within his twenty-five-year marriage and a [[midlife crisis]].

Charlotte, another American staying at the hotel, is a young [[Yale]] graduate in philosophy who is accompanying her husband John while he works as a celebrity photographer. She is feeling similarly disenchanted as she questions her marriage and is anxious about her future. They both struggle additionally with bouts of [[jet lag]] and [[culture shock]] in Tokyo and pass the time loitering around the hotel, Charlotte also trying ''[[ikebana]]'' by chance.

Charlotte is repelled by a vacuous Hollywood actress named Kelly, who is also at the hotel promoting a film. Kelly bumps into Charlotte and John, gushing over photography sessions she has previously done with him. Bob and Charlotte frequently cross paths in the hotel and eventually start to connect in the hotel bar.

After several encounters, when John is on assignment outside Tokyo, Charlotte invites Bob into the city to meet some local friends. They bond over an evening in Tokyo, where they experience the city nightlife together and end up singing at a [[karaoke box]]. In the days that follow, Bob and Charlotte spend more time together, and their friendship strengthens. One night while watching television and drinking ''[[sake]]'', while neither can sleep, the two share an intimate conversation about Charlotte's personal uncertainties and their married lives.

Bob has a cold conversation with his wife, then spends the night with a jazz singer from the hotel bar. Charlotte hears the woman singing in Bob's room the next morning, leading to tension between Bob and Charlotte during a ''[[shabu-shabu]]'' lunch together. The pair encounter each other in the evening and Bob reveals that he will be leaving Tokyo the following day.

Bob and Charlotte reconcile and express how they will miss each other, making a final visit to the hotel bar. The next morning, when Bob is leaving the hotel, he and Charlotte share sincere but unsatisfactory goodbyes. On Bob's taxi ride to the airport, he sees Charlotte on a crowded street, stops the car, and walks to her. He then embraces her and whispers something in her ear. The two share a kiss and say goodbye before Bob departs.

Coppola did not initially write the screenplay in traditional script form, citing the difficulty of mapping out a full plot.<ref name="olsen" /> Instead, she opted to write "little paragraphs" largely based on disparate impressions and experiences of her life in Tokyo, which she then adapted to a script.<ref name="olsen" /> Among the first images she included was of her friend Fumihiro Hayashi performing a karaoke rendition of the [[Sex Pistols]]' "[[God Save the Queen (Sex Pistols song)|God Save the Queen]]", which Coppola saw him perform during the time she worked in Tokyo.<ref name="diaconescu" /> After writing the first 20 pages with help from her brother, [[Roman Coppola]], she returned to Tokyo for further inspiration.<ref name="thompson" /> There, she videotaped anything she could use as a further writing aid.<ref name="focusinterview">{{cite web|url=http://lost-in-translation.com/qaPopup.html |title=Lost In Translation |publisher=[[Focus Features]] |access-date=May 3, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20031001195147/http://lost-in-translation.com/qaPopup.html |archive-date=October 1, 2003}}</ref>

Coppola envisioned Murray playing the role of Bob from the beginning, wanting to show off "his more sensitive side"<ref name="focusinterview" /> and feeling amused by the image of him dressed in a [[kimono]].<ref name="mitchell" /> She described her mental pictures of Murray as a significant source of inspiration for the story.<ref name="allen">{{cite web |last1=Allen |first1=Greg |title=Mafia Princess: An Interview with Sofia Coppola |date=August 31, 2003 |website=Greg.org |url=http://www.greg.org/sofia_coppola_interview.html |access-date=May 3, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20031012025250/http://www.greg.org/sofia_coppola_interview.html |archive-date=October 12, 2003}}</ref> For the character of Charlotte, Coppola drew from her own feelings of early-twenties disorientation, citing the strain in her relationship with her then-husband [[Spike Jonze]] as an influence for the relationship between Charlotte and John.<ref name="peretz">{{cite magazine |last1=Peretz |first1=Evgenia |title=Something About Sofia |magazine=[[Vanity Fair (magazine)|Vanity Fair]] |url=https://archive.vanityfair.com/article/2006/9/something-about-sofia |date=September 2006 |access-date=May 3, 2020}}</ref> She also drew inspiration from [[J. D. Salinger]]'s character Franny Glass in ''[[Franny and Zooey]]'', finding appeal in "the idea of a preppy girl having a breakdown".<ref name="meyer" />

As she developed the relationship between Bob and Charlotte, Coppola was compelled by the juxtaposition of the characters having similar internal crises at different stages of their lives.<ref name="betts">{{cite magazine |last1=Betts |first1=Kate |title=Sofia's Choice |url=http://content.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,483314,00.html |magazine=[[Time (magazine)|Time]] |date=September 8, 2003 |access-date=May 3, 2020}}</ref> She cited the dynamic between [[Philip Marlowe]] ([[Humphrey Bogart]]) and Vivian Sternwood Rutledge ([[Lauren Bacall]]) in ''[[The Big Sleep (1946 film)|The Big Sleep]]'' as a source of inspiration for their relationship.<ref name="thompson" /> Coppola reported doing little re-writing of the script,<ref name="chumo" /> which took six months to complete<ref name="thompson" /> and culminated in 75 pages, much shorter than the average feature film script.<ref name="harris">{{cite news |last1=Harris |first1=Dana |title=New film legends of the fall |url=https://variety.com/2003/film/news/new-film-legends-of-the-fall-1117893767/ |access-date=May 3, 2020 |work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] |date=October 12, 2003 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190403204309/https://variety.com/2003/film/news/new-film-legends-of-the-fall-1117893767/ |archive-date=April 3, 2019 |url-status=live }}</ref>{{refn|A feature film script is typically 90–120 pages.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Hilliard |first1=Robert L. |title=Writing for Television, Radio, and New Media |date=August 10, 2011 |publisher=[[Cengage|Cengage Learning]] |edition=10th |isbn=978-1439082713 |page=403}}</ref>|group="note"}} Despite worrying that the screenplay was too short and "indulgent" for including assortments of her personal experiences, she resolved to begin production of the film.<ref name="meyer" />

Coppola maintained that she would not have made ''Lost in Translation'' without Murray.<ref name="stern" /> Murray had an [[Toll-free telephone number|800 number]] for prospective clients interested in casting him, but he had a reputation as a recluse who was difficult to contact.<ref name="brownfield">{{cite news |last1=Brownfield |first1=Paul |date=February 29, 2004 |title=What about Bill? |url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2004-feb-29-ca-brownfield29-story.html |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201028030346/https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2004-feb-29-ca-brownfield29-story.html |archive-date=October 28, 2020 |access-date=May 3, 2020 |work=[[Los Angeles Times]]}}</ref> Coppola relentlessly pursued him and sent telephone messages and letters for months.<ref name="betts" /> She also sought people in her professional network that might help her make contact.<ref name="hirschberg" /> She recruited screenwriter [[Mitch Glazer]], who was a longtime friend of Murray's, to accept an early version of the script and try to persuade him.<ref name="peretz" /> Glazer was impressed with the story and said he called Murray frequently, telling him, "You need to read this",<ref name="brownfield" /> but he would not provide an answer.<ref name="peretz" /> After up to a year of cajoling, Murray finally agreed to meet with Coppola at a restaurant to discuss the film.<ref name="peretz" /> He then accepted the role, saying "she spent a lot of time getting me to be the guy. In the end, I felt I couldn't let her down."<ref name="hirschberg">{{cite magazine |last1=Hirschberg |first1=Lynn |title=The Coppola Smart Mob |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2003/08/31/magazine/the-coppola-smart-mob.html |access-date=May 3, 2020 |magazine=[[The New York Times Magazine]] |date=August 31, 2003 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170722164515/http://www.nytimes.com/2003/08/31/magazine/the-coppola-smart-mob.html |archive-date=July 22, 2017 |url-status=live }}</ref>

Despite Murray's agreement, Coppola had to take him at his word, as he did not sign a formal contract.<ref name="thompson" /> She described this as "nerve-wracking", wondering if he would show up for filming in Tokyo.<ref name="thompson" /> She discussed the issue with director [[Wes Anderson]], who had previously worked with Murray and encouraged her, saying, "If he says he's going to do it, he'll show up."<ref name="thompson" /> For Murray's co-star, Coppola liked Johansson's performance in ''[[Manny & Lo]]'', remembering her "as a cute little girl with that husky voice".<ref name="mitchell" /> She then invited Johansson to a restaurant to discuss the role.<ref name="vernon" /> Initially worried that the 17-year-old Johansson might be too young to play a character in her twenties, Coppola concluded that she appeared older and could convincingly play the part.<ref name="clarkj">{{cite news |last1=Clark |first1=John |date=November 9, 2003 |title=Young heart and old soul |url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2003-nov-09-ca-clark9-story.html |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201028030311/https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2003-nov-09-ca-clark9-story.html |archive-date=October 28, 2020 |access-date=May 3, 2020 |work=[[Los Angeles Times]]}}</ref> Coppola offered Johansson the role without an audition, which she accepted.<ref name="vernon">{{cite news |last1=Vernon |first1=Polly |title=Scarlett fever |url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2003/dec/28/features.magazine |access-date=May 3, 2020 |work=[[The Guardian]] |date=December 28, 2003 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190711034047/https://www.theguardian.com/film/2003/dec/28/features.magazine |archive-date=July 11, 2019 |url-status=live }}</ref>

Feeling a sense of personal investment in the project, Coppola wanted to maintain [[final cut privilege]] and feared that a distribution deal with a North American studio would threaten her influence.<ref name="thompson" /> It was also unlikely that a studio would provide such backing, given the short length of the screenplay and Murray's lack of formal involvement.{{sfn|King|2010|p=8}} Instead, she and her agent opted to sell foreign distribution rights to an assortment of companies to fund production costs of $4 million.<ref name="thompson" /> She struck a deal first with Japan's [[Tohokushinsha Film]], then with [[Pathé]] in France and Mikado Film in Italy, and finally with the international arm of [[Focus Features]] for the remaining foreign market.<ref name="thompson" /> By piecing together the funding from multiple distributors, Coppola reduced the influence of any single financier.{{sfn|King|2010|p=8}} Still not knowing if Murray would show up in Tokyo, Coppola spent $1 million of the budget,<ref name="thompson" /> knowing that his absence would doom the production. When he finally arrived, days before filming, she expressed significant relief.<ref name="elle">{{cite web |title=Our Own Private Hollywood |url=https://www.elle.com/culture/celebrities/g2263/our-own-private-hollywood-509892/?slide=24 |website=[[Elle (magazine)|Elle]] |date=October 7, 2010 |access-date=May 3, 2020}}</ref>

The idea for Bill Murray's character doing an advertising campaign for Suntory came from Coppola's father [[Francis Ford Coppola]] and director [[Akira Kurosawa]]'s Suntory Whiskey advertisement from the 1970s. In 2023 for Suntory's 100th anniversary, and 20 years after the appearance of ''Lost in Translation'', she was asked to direct the company's anniversary tribute video starring [[Keanu Reeves]], with a mix that included scenes from her film along with footage of her father and Kurosawa's original advertisement.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Matheson |first=Abbey |date=2023-08-10 |title=The True Story Behind Bill Murray's Whiskey Ad in 'Lost in Translation' |url=https://collider.com/lost-in-translation-bill-murray-whiskey-commercial-true-story/ |access-date=2024-03-06 |website=Collider |language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite magazine |date=2023-05-24 |title=Sofia Coppola on Tokyo, Suntory Whisky, and 20 Years of "Lost in Translation" |url=https://www.harpersbazaar.com/culture/film-tv/a43977665/sofia-coppola-suntory-whiskey-tribute-interview/ |access-date=2024-03-06 |magazine=Harper's BAZAAR |language=en-US}}</ref>

===Filming===
[[File:Early summer night Shibuya.jpg|thumb|upright=1.25|right|alt=See caption.|The production used bystanders as extras in public areas such as [[Shibuya Crossing]] (photograph taken in summer of 2002, with the walking dinosaur advertisement visible on the QFRONT (Tsutaya) building<ref name="focusfeatures.com">{{cite web | url=https://www.focusfeatures.com/article/film-locations_japan_lost-in-translation | title=Finding Japan in Lost in Translation }}</ref>).]]
[[Principal photography]] began on September 29, 2002,<ref name="dvdextra">{{cite video |date=February 3, 2004 |title='Lost' on Location |type=''Lost in Translation'' DVD Extra |publisher=[[Focus Features]]}}</ref> and lasted 27 days.<ref name="mitchell" /> With a tight schedule and a limited $4 million budget, filming was done six days per week, without permits,{{sfn|Ferriss|2023|pp=17–18}} marked by a "run-and-gun" approach: Coppola was keen to stay mobile with a small crew and minimal equipment.<ref name="focusinterview" /> She conducted few rehearsals and kept a flexible schedule, sometimes scrapping filming plans to shoot something she noticed on location if she thought it better served the story.<ref name="grove">{{cite news |last1=Grove |first1=Martin A. |title=Word is terrific for Coppola's 'Translation' |work=[[The Hollywood Reporter]] |date=August 15, 2003}}</ref>{{refn|One example includes the sequence featuring Charlotte walking through [[Shibuya Crossing]]. When Coppola noticed that rain had made the area look hazy and atmospheric, she scrapped filming plans in a nearby arcade to shoot the sequence.<ref name="thompson" />|group="note"}} Since the screenplay was sparse, missing details were often addressed during shooting, and Coppola allowed a significant amount of improvisation in dialogue, especially from Murray.<ref name="chumo" /> One example includes the scene in which Bob is being photographed for Suntory whisky, wherein Coppola encouraged Murray to react to the photographer spontaneously as she whispered names for the man to repeat to Murray as unrehearsed dialogue, such as "[[Roger Moore]]".<ref name="allen" /><ref name="chumo" />{{refn|Other examples of significant improvisation during shooting include the scenes that occur in the karaoke box<ref name="thompson" /> and sushi restaurant.<ref name="mitchell" />|group="note"}}

While key crew members were Americans that Coppola invited to Tokyo, most of the crew was hired locally.<ref name="grove" /> This proved to be challenging for the production, as most of the Japanese crew could not communicate with Coppola in English, so both sides relied on translations by a bilingual [[assistant director]] and a [[Gaffer (filmmaking)|gaffer]].<ref name="focusinterview" /> The production encountered frequent delays while translations took place and suffered from occasional cultural misunderstandings, such as one example when Coppola described a shoot in a restaurant that ran 10–15 minutes late, something she said was normal on an American shoot,<ref name="mitchell" /> but it prompted the restaurant owner to feel disrespected; he subsequently disconnected the crew's lights and the film's Japanese [[location manager]] resigned.<ref name="thompson" /> Despite this, Coppola said she worked to adapt to a Japanese style of filmmaking, not wanting to impose an approach that her crew was not used to.<ref name="focusinterview" />

Coppola worked closely to visualize the film with her [[director of photography]], [[Lance Acord]]. She showed him and other key crew members a book of photographs she created that represented the visual style she wanted to convey in the film.<ref name="chumo" /> To evoke a sense of isolation in Bob, Coppola and Acord used stationary shots in the hotel and avoided conspicuous camera movements.{{sfn|Acord|2004a|p=22}} They also had numerous discussions about shooting on [[Digital cinematography|video]], but they ultimately decided that film better suited the romantic undertones of the story.<ref name="focusinterview" /> Coppola remarked, "Film gives a little bit of a distance, which feels more like a memory to me. Video is more present tense".<ref name="focusinterview" /> Acord believed that new film stocks would reduce the need for excessive lighting, ultimately using [[List of motion picture film stocks#Vision color negative (ECN-2 process 1996–2002)|Kodak Vision]] 500T 5263 [[35 mm movie film|35 mm stock]] for night exteriors and Kodak Vision 320T 5277 stock in daylight.{{sfn|Acord|2004a|p=21}} Most of the film was shot with an [[Aaton Digital|Aaton 35-III]] camera. For some confined locations where the Aaton would have been too noisy, a [[Moviecam Compact]] was used.{{sfn|Acord|2004a|p=21}}

With high-speed film stocks, Acord chose to utilize [[available light]] as often as possible,<ref name="acord" /> only supplementing with artificial lights when necessary.{{sfn|Acord|2004a|p=21}} He reported "never really" rigging lights for night exteriors, relying on the natural light on Tokyo's city streets.{{sfn|Acord|2004a|p=21}} For interior sequences in the Park Hyatt Tokyo, he relied mostly on the hotel's practical lighting sources, shooting at a wide open [[f-stop]] and heavily [[Flag (lighting)|cutting]] the light to eliminate reflections in the hotel window.{{sfn|Acord|2004a|p=23}} Acord said he heard objections about lighting from some of the Japanese electricians, who were unaccustomed to relying so much on available light and were concerned that the exposure would not be sufficient.{{sfn|Acord|2004a|p=19}} Acord, assured that the film stocks would hold up against lower lighting, ultimately shot much of the film two stops underexposed.<ref name="acord">{{cite magazine |last=Acord |first=Lance |title=Channeling Tokyo for 'Lost in Translation' |magazine=[[American Cinematographer]] |date=January 2004b |volume=85 |issue=1 |pages=123–124}}</ref>

Many of the shooting locations were local places of business and public areas at the time of filming, including rooms, bar and swimming pool in the Park Hyatt Tokyo<ref>{{Cite web |title=detail｜Search List｜TOKYO LOCATION GUIDE｜TOKYO LOCATION BOX OFFICIAL SITE |url=https://www.locationbox.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/english/locatabi_eng/list/detail.html?pdid=544 |access-date=2024-03-06 |website=東京ロケーションボックス |language=ja-JP}}</ref> and [[Shibuya Crossing]] in Tokyo.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Zohar |first=Erica Wertheim |title=Park Hyatt Tokyo: 20 Years After Lost In Translation, This Iconic Property Looks Ahead To Its Sequel |url=https://www.forbes.com/sites/ericawertheimzohar/2023/09/07/park-hyatt-tokyo-20-years-after-lost-in-translation-this-iconic-property-looks-ahead-to-its-sequel/ |access-date=2024-03-06 |website=Forbes |language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |title=Park Hyatt Tokyo - The Hotel From 'Lost In Translation' Movie |url=https://uniqhotels.com/park-hyatt-tokyo/ |access-date=2024-03-06 |website=uniqhotels.com |language=en}}</ref> Filming also took place at [[Jōgan-ji (Nakano, Tokyo)]].<ref name="blog.gaijinpot.com">{{cite web | url=https://blog.gaijinpot.com/guide-best-lost-translation-spots-tokyo/ | title=A Guide to the Best 'Lost in Translation' Spots in Tokyo | date=January 16, 2018 }}</ref><ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.ultimatetravelclub.com/2022/08/03/visit-the-film-locations-of-lost-in-translation-tokyo-japan/ | title=Visit the Film Locations of Lost in Translation: Tokyo, Japan | work=Ultimate Travel Club | date=August 3, 2022 | last1=Wilson | first1=Laura }}</ref> The opening scene was done at Yasukuni-dōri in [[Kabuki-chō]].<ref>{{Cite web |last= |first= |date=2021-09-18 |title=Lost in Translation Filming Locations: Kabuki-cho (Shinjuku) |url=https://black-harpoon.medium.com/lost-in-translation-filming-locations-kabuki-cho-shinjuku-6f3765f96161 |access-date=2024-03-06 |website=Medium |language=en}}</ref> Charlotte's ride at the subway was taken at [[Omote-sando Station]] at the Hanzōmon and Ginza lines platform.<ref>{{Citation |title=Lost in Translation (2003) - On public streets and subways, the production did not secure filming permits<ref name="stern">{{cite web |last1=Stern |first1=Marlow |title=Sofia Coppola Discusses 'Lost in Translation' on Its 10th Anniversary |url=https://www.thedailybeast.com/sofia-coppola-discusses-lost-in-translation-on-its-10th-anniversary |website=[[The Daily Beast]] |access-date=May 7, 2020 |date=September 12, 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200114021809/https://www.thedailybeast.com/sofia-coppola-discusses-lost-in-translation-on-its-10th-anniversary |archive-date=January 14, 2020 |url-status=live }}</ref> and relied on city bystanders as [[Extra (acting)|extra]]s.<ref name="focusinterview" /> Coppola described the shooting as "documentary-style"<ref name="mitchell" /> and was worried at times about getting stopped by police, so she kept a minimal crew.<ref name="focusinterview" /> In the hotel, the production was not allowed to shoot in public areas until 1 or 2 a.m. to avoid disturbing guests.{{sfn|Acord|2004a|p=24}} In the film's concluding sequence in which Bob and Charlotte make their final goodbyes, Coppola reported being unhappy with the dialogue she had scripted, so Murray improvised the whisper in Johansson's ear.<ref name="chumo" /> Too quiet to be understandable, Coppola considered [[Dubbing (filmmaking)|dubbing]] audio in the scene, but she ultimately decided it was better that it "stays between the two of them".<ref name="chumo" /> After production concluded, Coppola supervised 10 weeks of editing by [[Sarah Flack]] in [[New York City]].<ref name="crabtree">{{cite news |last1=Crabtree |first1=Sheigh |title=Editor Flack in Fashion for Coppola's 'Lost' Pic |work=[[The Hollywood Reporter]] |date=September 10, 2003}}</ref>

==Soundtrack==
{{main|Lost in Translation (soundtrack)}}

The film's soundtrack was released by [[Emperor Norton Records]] on September 9, 2003.<ref name="metacriticost">{{cite web |title=Lost in Translation OST |url=https://www.metacritic.com/music/lost-in-translation-ost |website=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=May 20, 2020}}</ref> It contains 15 tracks, largely from the [[Shoegazing|shoegaze]] and dream pop genres of [[Indie rock|indie]] and [[alternative rock]]. The soundtrack was supervised by Brian Reitzell and contains songs from artists and groups including [[Death in Vegas]], [[Phoenix (French band)|Phoenix]], [[Squarepusher]], [[Sébastien Tellier]], and [[Happy End (band)|Happy End]]. [[The Jesus and Mary Chain]]'s song "[[Just Like Honey]]" and "[[Sometimes (My Bloody Valentine song)|Sometimes]]" by [[My Bloody Valentine (band)|My Bloody Valentine]] featured, and four original tracks were written for ''Lost in Translation'' by the latter band's frontman, [[Kevin Shields]].<ref name="dansby">{{cite magazine |last1=Dansby |first1=Andrew |title=Kevin Shields Found on 'Lost' |url=https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/kevin-shields-found-on-lost-243814/ |access-date=May 20, 2020 |magazine=[[Rolling Stone]] |date=September 24, 2003}}</ref> Other tracks produced for the film include two co-written by Reitzell and [[Roger Joseph Manning Jr.]], and one by [[Air (French band)|Air]].<ref name="dansby" /> Songs featured in the film that are not in the soundtrack include karaoke performances of [[Elvis Costello]]'s cover of "[[(What's So Funny 'Bout) Peace, Love, and Understanding]]" and [[the Pretenders]]' "[[Brass in Pocket]]". A further performance by Murray of [[Roxy Music]]'s "[[More Than This (Roxy Music song)|More Than This]]" is included as a bonus track.{{refn|"[[(What's So Funny 'Bout) Peace, Love, and Understanding]]" was chosen for Bob to highlight his position in an older generation, "[[Brass in Pocket]]" was chosen for Charlotte to show her playful side, and "[[More Than This (Roxy Music song)|More Than This]]" was chosen extemporaneously by Coppola and Murray during shooting, after the two discovered they had a mutual affinity for [[Roxy Music]].<ref name="chumo" />|group="note"}}

During the screenwriting stage, Coppola spoke to Reitzell about the "moody" and "melancholic" qualities she wanted the music to convey in the film, as well as what Reitzell understood to be the "strange, floating, jet-lagged weirdness" that would define the central characters.<ref name="hundley" /> Coppola said she wanted the soundtrack "to be less like a score" and more like the dream-pop mixes Reitzell made to assist her writing of the film.<ref name="mitchell" /> While Shields had released little music since the release of ''[[Loveless (album)|Loveless]]'' in 1991,<ref name="white">{{cite magazine |last1=White |first1=Michael |title=My Bloody Valentine's Kevin Shields: An Enigma Emerges |url=http://exclaim.ca/film/article/lost_in_translation-sofia_coppolas_journey_into_culture |access-date=May 20, 2020 |magazine=[[Exclaim!]] |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190916102334/http://exclaim.ca/film/article/lost_in_translation-sofia_coppolas_journey_into_culture |archive-date=September 16, 2019 |url-status=live }}</ref> at Reitzell's suggestion, he and Coppola enlisted him to help write original music for the film;<ref name="keast">{{cite magazine |last1=Keast |first1=James |title=Lost In Translation: Sofia Coppola's Journey Into Culture Clash |date=October 1, 2003 |url=http://exclaim.ca/film/article/lost_in_translation-sofia_coppolas_journey_into_culture |magazine=[[Exclaim!]] |access-date=May 20, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190916102334/http://exclaim.ca/film/article/lost_in_translation-sofia_coppolas_journey_into_culture |archive-date=September 16, 2019 |url-status=live }}</ref> Reitzell believed Shields "could capture that droning, swaying, beautiful kind of feeling that we wanted."<ref name="hundley" /> He then joined Shields in [[London]] for some two months<ref name="npr">{{cite news |title=From My Bloody Valentine to 'Lost in Translation' |url=https://www.npr.org/2003/09/15/1431220/from-my-bloody-valentine-to-lost-in-translation |access-date=May 20, 2020 |work=[[NPR]] |type=Audio |date=September 15, 2003 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200204175551/https://www.npr.org/2003/09/15/1431220/from-my-bloody-valentine-to-lost-in-translation |archive-date=February 4, 2020 |url-status=live }}</ref> of overnight recording sessions, and they used the screenplay and [[dailies]] from production as inspiration while they worked on songs for the film.<ref name="hundley" /> Shields commented on the challenge he felt in songwriting for a film, saying "I was barely aware of the language of music that's not essentially just for your ears.&nbsp;... In the end, just the physical movement of the film, that was a delicacy. And I suppose that's why I ended up doing stuff that was so delicate."<ref name="dansby" />

King argues that music often plays the most significant role in setting mood and tone in the film, writing that it is substantial "in evoking the dreamy, narcotised, semi-detached impressions of jet-lag" as well as broader feelings of alienation and disconnection, "making what is probably the largest single contribution to the widespread understanding of the film as a 'mood piece'."{{sfn|King|2010|p=115}} He points to the use of "Girls" by Death in Vegas, featured in the early sequence in which Bob is driven from the airport to the hotel, arguing that it "plays a role equal to if not dominating that of the visuals{{nbsp}}..., creating a drifting, ethereal and somewhat dreamy quality that precisely captures the impressions of temporal and spatial disjunction".{{sfn|King|2010|pp=115–116}} He also points to the use of "cool and distant" tracks like "Tommib", used in the extended sequence featuring Charlotte observing Tokyo while seated in her hotel room window, as playing a significant role in establishing feelings of isolation and disorientation in the character.{{sfn|King|2010|pp=116–119}} In King's view, some sequences feature combinations of music and visuals so as to function as "audio-visual [[set piece]]s", which offer distinct points of appeal in the film for its target audience.{{sfn|King|2010|p=117}}

==Release==

===Marketing===
Coppola did not sell distribution rights for the United States and Canada until she and Flack finished editing the film.<ref name="harris" /> In February 2003, the director showed the film to top executives at the domestic arm of Focus Features,<ref name="harris" /> the company to which it had already sold most of the foreign distribution. The prior contract proved to be significant for Focus, as it received privileged access to the film while competing buyers complained that they were restricted to the viewing of a three-minute trailer in the Focus offices at the [[American Film Market]].<ref>{{cite news |last1=Harris |first1=Dana |last2=Dunkley |first2=Cathy |date=February 25, 2003 |title=Focus frames 'Lost' deal at finish of AFM |url=https://variety.com/2003/film/news/focus-frames-lost-deal-at-finish-of-afm-1117881191/?jwsource=cl |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201028030236/https://variety.com/2003/film/news/focus-frames-lost-deal-at-finish-of-afm-1117881191/?jwsource=cl |archive-date=October 28, 2020 |access-date=May 9, 2020 |work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]}}</ref> Coppola initially offered the domestic distribution rights for $5 million, but she decided to sell them to Focus for $4 million, citing her appreciation for the international deals the company had secured for the film.<ref name="harris" />

Once Focus was involved, it began promoting the film by employing a conventional "[[Independent film|indie]]-style" marketing campaign.{{sfn|King|2010|p=16}} The strategy involved generating positive word of mouth for the film well before its September 2003 release.<ref name="grove2" /> The distributor arranged advance press screenings throughout the summer of 2003 and combined this with a magazine publicity campaign.<ref name="grove2">{{cite news |last1=Grove |first1=Martin A. |title=Focus Heads Focus on 'Translation' Success |work=[[The Hollywood Reporter]] |date=October 10, 2003}}</ref>{{refn|For one example of a magazine article cited by Focus as being part of this campaign, see {{harvnb|Hirschberg|2003}}.<ref name="grove2" />|group="note"}} Posters and trailers emphasized the recognizable star presence of Murray, highlighting his performance in the film's comic sequences, which favored wider audience appeal.{{sfn|King|2010|pp=24–25}} Immediately prior to its release, Focus placed ''Lost in Translation'' in [[film festival]]s and hosted "intimate media screenings" that included question-and-answer panels with Coppola and Murray.<ref name="harris" /> Many of these marketing tacks were designed to promote the film at minimal cost, a departure from more costly strategies often employed in the Hollywood mainstream, such as major television advertising.{{sfn|King|2010|p=16}}

===Theatrical run===
''Lost in Translation'' had its premiere on August 29, 2003, at the [[Telluride Film Festival]] in the United States.<ref name="afi" /> Over the next week, it appeared at the [[60th Venice International Film Festival]] and the [[2003 Toronto International Film Festival]].<ref name="afi" /> It opened to the public in [[limited release]] on September 12, 2003, at 23 theaters in major cities in the United States.<ref name="brooks">{{cite news |last1=Brooks |first1=Brian |title=The Land of the Rising Box Office Shines Bright for 'Lost in Translation' |url=https://www.indiewire.com/2003/09/the-land-of-the-rising-box-office-shines-bright-for-lost-in-translation-79462/ |access-date=May 10, 2020 |work=[[IndieWire]] |date=September 16, 2003 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180528014459/http://www.indiewire.com/2003/09/the-land-of-the-rising-box-office-shines-bright-for-lost-in-translation-79462/ |archive-date=May 28, 2018 |url-status=live }}</ref> The film had already generated speculation about [[Academy Awards|Oscar]] contention from advance screenings and was noted for opening several weeks earlier than expected for an indie vying for awards—a risk being that opening too early might cause the film to be forgotten by the time nominations were made for major prizes like the Academy Awards.<ref name="grove2" />{{sfn|King|2010|pp=16–17}} Focus Features co-presidents [[James Schamus]] and [[David Linde]] commented that the company chose an early release date on the basis of factors including the film's quality and early marketing campaign, as well as a lack of competition from other films.<ref name="grove2" /> The strategy was intended to give ''Lost in Translation'' more time to command the marketplace.{{sfn|King|2010|p=17}}

[[File:Lost in Translation Theater Count.png|upright=1.45|thumb|alt=The number starts at 23 on September 12, 2003, rises to 882 on October 10, and declines to a low of 123 on January 2, 2004. It then rises to 632 on January 30 and then declines to 51 on March 25.|Graph showing the estimated number of theaters in which ''Lost in Translation'' played in the United States, Canada, and Puerto Rico in 2003–2004.<ref name="bom" /> Focus Features expanded its theater presence in January after it received nominations for the 76th Academy Awards.]]
The film grossed $925,000 in its opening weekend and was expanded the next week from 23 theaters to 183<ref name="grove2" /> in the top 25 markets of the country.<ref name="brooks" /> There, it grossed more than $2.62 million over the weekend<ref name="brooks2">{{cite news |last1=Brooks |first1=Brian |title='Scarface' Lashes the B.O.; Docs Continue Strong |url=https://www.indiewire.com/2003/09/scarface-lashes-the-b-o-docs-continue-strong-79447/ |access-date=May 10, 2020 |work=[[IndieWire]] |date=September 23, 2003}}</ref> and nearly paid off the total budget of the film. It entered [[wide release]] on October 3,{{refn|"Wide release" is defined here as crossing the 600-theater threshold.|group="note"}} its fourth weekend, peaking at a rank of seven in the box office chart;<ref name="bom">{{cite web |title=Lost in Translation |website=[[Box Office Mojo]] |url=https://www.boxofficemojo.com/release/rl2388755969/weekend/ |access-date=July 4, 2020}}</ref> a week later, it expanded to an estimated 882 theaters, the film's highest theater count over its run.<ref name="bom" /> ''Lost in Translation'' grossed an estimated to-date total of $18.5 million through October 13<ref name="bom" /> and was noted by ''[[The Hollywood Reporter]]'' to have been performing well even "in smaller and medium-sized markets where audiences don't always respond to this type of upscale material".<ref name="grove2" /> Following this performance, ''Lost in Translation'' saw a gradual decline in theater presence progressing into the new year,<ref name="bom" /> though it was expanded again after the film received nominations for the [[76th Academy Awards]].{{sfn|King|2010|p=22}} The film was widened from a late December low of 117 theaters to an estimated 632 at the end of January, ultimately ending its run in the United States and Canada on March 25 and earning $44.6 million.<ref name="bom" /> Its international release earned $74.1 million, for a worldwide total of $119 million.<ref name="bom" />

===Home media===
The DVD of ''Lost in Translation'' was released on February 3, 2004 by [[Universal Studios Home Video]],<ref name="metacritic2">{{cite web |title=Lost in Translation Details and Credits |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/lost-in-translation/details |website=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=May 10, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180813001028/http://www.metacritic.com/movie/lost-in-translation/details |archive-date=August 13, 2018 |url-status=live }}</ref> and includes deleted scenes, a behind-the-scenes featurette, a conversation about the film featuring Murray and Coppola, and a music video for "[[City Girl (song)|City Girl]]",<ref>{{cite AV media |title=Lost in Translation |type=DVD |date=February 3, 2004 |publisher=[[Focus Features]]}}</ref> one of the original songs composed for the film by [[Kevin Shields]]. Wanting to capitalize on the publicity surrounding ''Lost in Translation''{{'}}s presence at the Academy Awards, Focus Features made the unusual move of releasing the film on home media while it was still screening in theaters, immediately after its Oscar nominations were announced.{{sfn|King|2010|p=22}} The strategy was seen as risky, as the industry was generally concerned that theatrical revenues could be harmed by early home video release.{{sfn|King|2010|p=22}} ''Lost in Translation'' ultimately earned nearly $5 million from its first five days of video rentals and sold one million retail copies during its first week of release.<ref name="saccone">{{cite magazine |last1=Saccone |first1=Melinda |title=Coppola's Oscar-Lauded Film Translates Well to Video |magazine=[[Home Media Magazine|Video Store Magazine]] |date=February 15, 2004 |volume=26 |issue=7 |page=18}}</ref> Early returns showed it was the second-best selling DVD during this period<ref name="saccone" /> while the film screened in 600 theaters and box office revenues dropped 19% from the previous week, which ''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' described as "relatively modest".<ref name="hettrick">{{cite news |last1=Hettrick |first1=Scott |title='Lost' translates to DVD, bigscreen |url=https://variety.com/2004/digital/features/lost-translates-to-dvd-bigscreen-1117900355/ |access-date=May 10, 2020 |work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] |date=February 17, 2004}}</ref> Focus credited the performance to positive word of mouth and cited the marketing for the film on both media as helpful for whichever platform consumers chose.<ref name="hettrick" />

==Criticism and boycott calls==<!--Please don't change this section to "controversy" per WP:CSECTION.-->

===Accusations of racism===
Critics have argued that ''Lost in Translation'' is [[Racism|racist]] and perpetuates [[Orientalism]], and that its tropes and visual framing rely on [[Ethnic joke|ethnic humor]], [[Stereotypes of East Asians in the United States|stereotypes of East Asians]], Orientalist imagery, and the [[dehumanization]] of Japanese characters.<ref name="wright">{{cite news |last1=Wright |first1=George |title=Hit film gets lost in racism row |url=https://www.theguardian.com/world/2004/feb/27/oscars2004.usa |access-date=May 25, 2020 |work=[[The Guardian]] |date=February 27, 2004 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190816210556/https://www.theguardian.com/world/2004/feb/27/oscars2004.usa |archive-date=August 16, 2019 |url-status=live }}</ref>{{sfn|King|2010|p=132}}<ref name="day">{{cite news |last1=Day |first1=Kiku |title=Totally lost in translation |url=https://www.theguardian.com/world/2004/jan/24/japan.film |access-date=May 25, 2020 |work=[[The Guardian]] |date=January 23, 2004 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200702104422/https://www.theguardian.com/world/2004/jan/24/japan.film |archive-date=July 2, 2020 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="paik">{{cite magazine |last1=Paik |first1=E. Koohan |title=Is 'Lost in Translation' Racist? |magazine=[[ColorLines|ColorLines RaceWire]] |date=November 2003 |access-date=May 25, 2020 |url=http://www.arc.org/racewire/031112e_paik.html |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20050418214717/http://www.arc.org/racewire/031112e_paik.html |archive-date=April 18, 2005}}</ref><ref name=":0" />

On January 31, 2004, the Asian American and Pacific Islander (AAPI) organization Asia Media Watch sponsored and launched a nationwide campaign to petition motion picture industry groups, including the [[Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences]], [[Directors Guild of America]], [[Screen Actors Guild]], and [[Writers Guild of America]], to vote against the film ''Lost In Translation'' in all awards categories at the [[76th Academy Awards]].<ref name="lost-in-racism">{{Cite web |title=lost-in-racism.org {{!}} Campaign - No Votes for Lost In Translation |url=https://lost-in-racism.org/|access-date=2026-05-09 |website=lost-in-racism.org}}</ref><ref name="wright" /> The group, along with its campaign volunteers and supporters, distributed flyers and lobbied voters at film screenings in Los Angeles, New York, and San Francisco, while also conducting email campaigns and contacting the press.<ref name="lost-in-racism" /> The initiative ultimately had little impact on the Academy Awards due to the dominant sweep of ''[[The Lord of the Rings: The Return of the King]]'', which won eleven awards, including [[Academy Award for Best Picture|Best Picture]] and [[Academy Award for Best Director|Best Director]].<ref>{{cite news |last=Morales |first=Tatiana |title=Peter Jackson, Lord Of The Oscars |work=[[CBS News]] |date=February 13, 2009 |url=https://www.cbsnews.com/news/peter-jackson-lord-of-the-oscars/ |access-date=July 24, 2013}}</ref> However, spokesperson Tom Roman suggested that because some voters who were lobbied agreed to return their promotional screening DVDs for refunds, the campaign may have influenced voting at the [[10th Screen Actors Guild Awards]], where ''Lost in Translation'' star Bill Murray, who was previously considered the favorite for [[Outstanding Performance by a Male Actor in a Leading Role]], was defeated by [[Johnny Depp]] for his role in ''[[Pirates of the Caribbean: The Curse of the Black Pearl]]''.<ref name="wright" />

The organization insisted that the film "dehumanizes the Japanese people by portraying them as a collection of shallow stereotypes who are treated with disregard and disdain," claiming that such portrayals and lack of [[cultural sensitivity]] led to discrimination against [[Asian Americans]] in the United States, where a significant minority of the population already holds [[Anti-Asian sentiment in the United States|anti-Asian sentiment]].<ref name="lost-in-racism" /> The group further criticized the film for perpetuating [[perpetual foreigner]] stereotypes, claiming that they also reinforce discrimination and stating: "This film is indicative of a level of mainstream tolerance and acceptance of Asian American discrimination that would otherwise be unacceptable if directed towards [[African Americans|African]] and [[Hispanic and Latino Americans|Hispanic Americans]]."<ref name="lost-in-racism" /> In a released statement, the group asserted: <blockquote>The humor and lampooning of the Japanese in the film has a distinctly racial element … The main characters prey on the Japanese and their inability to understand English. Particularly offensive are the hackneyed stereotypical jokes such as the overdone juxtaposition of L's and R's, mocking them because they bow and are short, and references to their disgusting food. The main characters visibly express disdain, and make insulting remarks and jokes in the direct presence of Japanese characters. There are no redeeming Japanese roles in the film, nor is there any significant dialogue between the main characters and the Japanese characters. They merely serve as buffoons for the main characters to ridicule.<ref name="lost-in-racism" /></blockquote>
Another Asian American and Pacific Islander (AAPI) organization at the time, the Asian American Movement Ezine,<ref>{{Cite web |last=School |first=Stanford Law |title=APILSA Resources |url=https://law.stanford.edu/apilsa/apilsa-resources/ |access-date=2026-05-09 |website=Stanford Law School |language=en}}</ref> criticized the film, comparing its tropes to the history of [[Asian-American representation in Hollywood|Asian American representation in Hollywood]] and [[racism in early American film]]: "To show the culture clash between American and Japanese culture, the film relies on stale stereotypes of the Japanese for laughs: They’re short! They’re wacky! [[Perception of English /r/ and /l/ by Japanese speakers|They can’t pronounce their r’s]]!  The film is replete with racial gags that draw from the same old Hollywood stereotypes, from [[Mickey Rooney]]’s [[I. Y. Yunioshi|Mr. Yunioshi]] in [[Breakfast at Tiffany's (film)|''Breakfast at Tiffany’s'']], to the Japanese car makers in 1986’s [[Gung Ho (film)|''Gung Ho'']], or even [[Sixteen Candles|''Sixteen Candle''s]] infamous [[Long Duk Dong]]."<ref>{{Cite web |title="Lost in Translation" is the Same Old Story |url=http://www.aamovement.net/art_culture/filmreviews/losttrans1.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20031110084717/http://www.aamovement.net/art_culture/filmreviews/losttrans1.html |archive-date=10 Nov 2003 |access-date=9 May 2026 |website=Asian American Movement Ezine}}</ref><ref name="lost-in-racism-2">{{Cite web |title=asianmediawatch.net {{!}} Review of Lost In Translation |url=https://lost-in-racism.org/lost.html#reviews |access-date=2026-05-27 |website=lost-in-racism.org}}</ref>

Filmmaker [[Koohan Paik-Mander|E. Koohan Paik]] argued: "The Japanese are presented not as people, but as clowns ... the hilarity is rooted entirely in the '[[Other (philosophy)|otherness]]' of the Japanese people. We laugh at them, not with them. This is why the film is accused of being racist." Paik wrote that it is "the shirking of responsibility to depict them as full human beings, either negative or positive, which constitutes discrimination, or racism."<ref name="paik" /><ref name="lost-in-racism-2" /> Paik also criticized the film's racial undertones within its [[cinematography]]:<blockquote>Even [[Cinéma vérité|verité-style footage]] of authentic locals focuses on the Japanese as a sorry lot, preoccupied with cheesifying all things Western ... These scenes are occasionally 'balanced' by appropriately reverent, but equally inscrutable, shrine-and-temple sequences. Moreover, the film is simultaneously scornful and smug in the knowledge that imitation, no matter how tacky, is the sincerest form of flattery. This sentiment is actually articulated in the dialogue, by Charlotte's husband, galled by a rock-band photo shoot: "Let them be who they are! They're trying to make them [[Keith Richards]] when they're just skinny and nerdy." The subtext here is, when Westerners ape the [[The Rolling Stones|Rolling Stones]], it's normal; but when Asian kids fall prey to the same media hype, they're pathetic wannabees. They should be meditating in a [[dojo]] somewhere, not playing [[rock and roll]].<ref name="paik" /></blockquote>Critics argued that the film portrays Japanese people through exaggerated stereotypes, particularly in scenes involving [[language barrier]]s.<ref name=":1" /><ref name=":0" /> Several scenes attracted criticism for using Japanese accents and English pronunciation as sources of humor; opponents of the film argued that such scenes encourage audiences to laugh at [[Japanese language|Japanese speech patterns]] and mannerisms rather than at the protagonists’ discomfort.<ref name="rich" /><ref name="paik" /><ref>{{Cite web |title=Pop culture lost in Asian stereotypes - The Arizona State Press |url=https://www.statepress.com/article/2004/03/pop-culture-lost-in-asian-stereotypes |access-date=2026-05-09 |website=www.statepress.com |language=en-US}}</ref> In ''[[National Post|The National Post]],'' [[Robert Fulford (journalist)|Robert Fulford]] criticized the film: “''Lost in Translation'' expresses a distasteful racism through romantic comedy. But they’re ''our'' yucks, at ''their'' expense. It’s bad enough to make fun of Japanese for their English in [[English-speaking world|English-speaking countries]]. But in Japan? In ''[[The Guardian]],'' ethnomusicologist Kiku Day wrote: "There is only one type of humor in the film that I could see: [[Anti-Japanese sentiment|anti-Japanese racism]], which is its very spine … There is no scene where the Japanese are afforded a shred of dignity. Day wrote:<blockquote>The Japanese are one-dimensional and dehumanized in the movie, serving as an exotic background for Bob and Charlotte's story, like dirty wallpaper in a cheap hotel. How funny is it to put the 6ft-plus Bill Murray in an elevator with a number of overly small Japanese? To manufacture a joke, the film has Murray contorting himself to have a shower because its head isn't high enough for him - although he is supposed to be staying in a five-star hotel. It's made up simply to give western audiences another stereotype to laugh at. Why can't these funny little people understand me?"<ref>{{Cite web |date=2004-01-09 |title=Lost in Translation (15) |url=https://www.the-independent.com/arts-entertainment/films/reviews/lost-in-translation-15-72637.html |access-date=2026-07-20 |website=The Independent |language=en}}</ref> Film director [[Robert Allan Ackerman]] accused the film of stereotyping and racism, stating that he "thought that it had nothing to do with Japan; they could have been on the moon, and the only reason to have Japanese people was to make fun of them."<ref>{{Cite web |last=Hadley-garcia |first=George |date=2009-01-23 |title=Broth in translation |url=https://www.japantimes.co.jp/culture/2009/01/23/films/broth-in-translation-2/ |access-date=2026-07-14 |website=The Japan Times |language=en}}</ref> Film critic [[Mike D'Angelo]] argued that viewers are never encouraged to identify with anyone other than the two white American protagonists, he wrote that he felt "constantly being nudged to find the Japanese hilariously wacky." He cited this persistent "nudging" as a primary reason for his surprise at the film's widespread acclaim.<ref>{{Cite web |last=D'Angelo |first=Mike |title=Lost in Translation 2003 |url=https://letterboxd.com/gemko/film/lost-in-translation/ |access-date=2026-07-20 |website=letterboxd.com |language=en}}</ref>

Homay King, professor of History of Art at [[Bryn Mawr College]] accused the film of Orientalism stating that, "''Lost in Translation'' makes but minimal efforts to rewrite the myths of Asia that Hollywood film has been recycling for nearly a century: the [[Orient]] as primitive, feminized, and eroticized; Asian citizens as alternately silly or perilous, enigmatic and cloaked in artifice."{{sfn|King|2005|p=46}} She also noted that the film fails to establish the perspective from which Japanese representations are made, writing that "the film [does not] sufficiently clarify that its real subject is not Tokyo itself, but Western perceptions of Tokyo{{nbsp}}... When Japan appears superficial, inappropriately erotic, or unintelligible, we are never completely sure whether this vision belongs to Coppola, to her characters, or simply to a Hollywood cinematic imaginary."{{sfn|King|2005|pp=45–46}} Kendra Marston, a [[film studies]] lecturer at [[Massey University]], argues that the narrative primarily centers on [[Whiteness studies|whiteness]], "the soul searching of the white American characters," contrasting this focus with what she characterizes as a "heavily stereotyped depiction" of local Japanese characters. According to Marston, "the 'unknowability' of the Tokyo environment aids in perpetuating a cinematic mood characterized by sustained periods of white melancholia."<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Marston |first=Kendra |title=Postfeminist Whiteness: Problematising Melancholic Burden in Contemporary Hollywood |url=https://www.jstor.org/content/oa_chapter_monograph/10.3366/j.ctt1pwt3hk.11?searchText=Sofia+coppola&searchUri=%2Faction%2FdoBasicSearch%3FQuery%3DSofia%2Bcoppola%26so%3Drel&ab_segments=0%2Fbasic_search_gsv2%2Fcontrol&refreqid=fastly-default%3A90baacae9c33bf0e126d871c4d43f57b&seq=16 |journal=[[Edinburgh University Press]] |jstor=10.3366/j.ctt1pwt3hk.11 |via=JSTOR}}</ref> Marston further identifies the specific emotional undercurrent within the film as a "painful yet pleasurable [[neurasthenia]], experienced only by the white and privileged, that the protagonists are unable to alleviate," writing: "The allure of the exotic Other occupies a source of transient fantasy that remains in the realm of wishful thinking, never promising a lasting transcendence over the melancholic state and yet also importantly working to preserve the text’s primary interest in the mysterious nature and nostalgic idealization of [[White identity|white feminine identity]]."<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Marston |first=Kendra |title=Postfeminist Whiteness: Problematising Melancholic Burden in Contemporary Hollywood |url=https://www.jstor.org/content/oa_chapter_monograph/10.3366/j.ctt1pwt3hk.11?searchText=Sofia+coppola&searchUri=%2Faction%2FdoBasicSearch%3FQuery%3DSofia%2Bcoppola%26so%3Drel&ab_segments=0%2Fbasic_search_gsv2%2Fcontrol&refreqid=fastly-default%3A90baacae9c33bf0e126d871c4d43f57b&seq=19 |journal=[[Edinburgh University Press]] |jstor=10.3366/j.ctt1pwt3hk.11 |via=JSTOR}}</ref>

Critic [[Steve Burgess (author)|Steve Burgess]] argued that the film relies entirely on the audience sympathizing with Bob and Charlotte’s point of view, writing, "The movie would exhibit little appeal for the audience if they disliked the central characters. Clearly, Coppola wants us to sympathize with [them]." Burgess criticized the film for using Japan and its people as a cinematic shorthand for "strangeness," rather than depicting the country with genuine nuance.<ref name="Tyee">{{Cite web |title=Bashing Japan: People Love It! |url=https://thetyee.ca/Entertainment/2004/01/13/Bashing_Japan_People_Love_It/ |access-date=2026-07-02 |website=The Tyee}}</ref> Pointing to specific characterizations, he wrote:<blockquote>Virtually every Japanese person in the film is wacky, creepy, bizarre, or plastic. The worst example is the hooker scene, which even the film’s defenders cannot defend. It’s just over-the-top silly, with the hooker ending up kicking and wailing on the floor of Murray’s hotel room. Those freaky [[Jap]]s. Who can figure ’em?<ref name="Tyee" /></blockquote>

In response to accusations of racism, producer [[Ross Katz]] defended the film, stating he believed "Sofia's love of Japan and love of the people that she's met there is incredibly evident in the film."<ref name="rich" /> Katz added that none of the scenes in the film were "any slight to Japanese people."<ref name="rich" /> 

Director Sofia Coppola stated: "I can see why people might think that, but I know I'm not racist. I think if everything's based on truth you can make fun, have a little laugh, but also be respectful of a culture. I just love Tokyo and I'm not mean-spirited [...] I think that everything you do, people could be offended by—unless you're just trying to be nice about everyone." Coppola further defended the film's humor by noting that specific gags were drawn from her own observations, adding, "Even on our daily call sheets, they would mix up the 'rs' and the 'ls'—all that was from experience, it's not made up. I guess someone has misunderstood my intentions. It bugs me, because I know I'm not racist."<ref name="morrow" /><ref name="wright" />

==Reception==

===Critical response===
''Lost in Translation'' received acclaim, particularly for Murray's performance and for Coppola's direction and screenplay.{{sfn|King|2010|pp=28–29}} On [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film has an approval rating of 95% based on 234 reviews, with an [[weighted arithmetic mean|average]] rating of 8.8/10. The site's critical consensus reads, "Effectively balancing humor and subtle pathos, Sofia Coppola crafts a moving, melancholy story that serves as a showcase for both Bill Murray and Scarlett Johansson."<ref name="rottentomatoes">{{cite web |title=Lost in Translation (2003) |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |access-date=July 10, 2020 |url=http://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/lost_in_translation/ |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200605075150/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/lost_in_translation |archive-date=June 5, 2020 |url-status=live }}</ref> On [[Metacritic]], which assigns a rating to reviews, the film has an average score of 91 out of 100 based on 44 reviews, indicating "universal acclaim".<ref name="metacritic">{{cite web |title=Lost in Translation (2003) |website=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=May 12, 2020 |url=http://www.metacritic.com/movie/lost-in-translation |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200617160815/https://www.metacritic.com/movie/lost-in-translation |archive-date=June 17, 2020 |url-status=live }}</ref>

Critics praised Murray's performance as Bob, commending his handling of a more serious role that was combined with the comic persona for which he was already broadly known. Edelstein argued that it was "the Bill Murray performance we've been waiting for", adding that "his two halves have never come together as they do here, in a way that connects that hilarious detachment with the deep and abiding sense of isolation that must have spawned it".<ref name="edelstein" /> [[Lisa Schwarzbaum]] of ''[[Entertainment Weekly]]'' regarded Murray's performance as Oscar-worthy and lauded it as his "most vulnerable and unmannered" to date; she praised his treatment of a more delicate role as well as his improvisations in the film's comic sequences.<ref |url-status=live }}</ref> [[Roger Ebert]] gave ''Lost in Translation'' four out of four stars and named it the second best film of the year, describing it as "sweet and sad at the same time as it is sardonic and funny".<ref name="ebert">{{cite news | last = Ebert | first = Roger | authorlink= Roger Ebert | title = ''Lost in Translation'' | work = [[Chicago Sun-Times]] | pages = | language = | Coppola received acclaim for her screenplay and direction. [[Kenneth Turan]] of the ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'' commented that ''Lost in Translation'' was "tart and sweet, unmistakably funny and exceptionally well observed—[which] marks{{nbsp}}... Coppola as a mature talent with a distinctive sensibility and the means to express it".<ref name="turan">{{cite news |last1=Turan |first1=Kenneth |title=Bill at his best |url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2003-sep-12-et-turan12-story.html |access-date=May 12, 2020 |work=[[Los Angeles Times]] |date=September 12, 2003 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200403162030/https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2003-sep-12-et-turan12-story.html |archive-date=April 3, 2020 |url-status=live }}</ref> Much of the praise was directed specifically at her attention to qualities of subtlety and atmosphere; David Rooney of ''Variety'' praised the film as "a mood piece", adding that its "deft balance of humor and poignancy makes it both a pleasurable and melancholy experience".<ref name="rooney">{{cite news |last1=Rooney |first1=David |title=Lost in Translation |url=https://variety.com/2003/film/awards/lost-in-translation-6-1200539681/ |access-date=May 12, 2020 |work=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]] |date=August 31, 2003 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180714150754/https://variety.com/2003/film/awards/lost-in-translation-6-1200539681/ |archive-date=July 14, 2018 |url-status=live }}</ref> Likewise, ''[[Salon (website)|Salon]]'' critic [[Stephanie Zacharek]] lauded Coppola as a "stealth dramatist" whose understated narrative style made for an artful depiction of emotion; she praised ''Lost in Translation'' as an intimate story that marks Coppola as an exceptional filmmaker.<ref name="zacharek">{{cite web |last1=Zacharek |first1=Stephanie |title=Lost in Translation |website=[[Salon (website)|Salon]] |date=September 13, 2003 |url=https://www.salon.com/2003/09/12/translation/ |access-date=June 20, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200703170508/https://www.salon.com/2003/09/12/translation/ |archive-date=July 3, 2020 |url-status=live }}</ref>

==Analysis==

===Themes===
{{Quote box|quote=He was trapped.{{nbsp}}... When you go to a foreign country, truly foreign, there is a major shock of consciousness that comes on you when you see that, "Oh God, it's just me here." There's nobody, no neighbors, no friends, no phone calls—just room service.|align=right|width=25em|style=padding:15px|source=—Bill Murray, speaking about Bob Harris<ref>{{cite web |last1=Murray |first1=Rebecca |title=Interview with Bill Murray |url=http://movies.about.com/cs/lostintranslation/a/lostbillint_2.htm |website=[[Dotdash|About]] |access-date=June 1, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20051024011040/http://movies.about.com/cs/lostintranslation/a/lostbillint_2.htm |archive-date=October 24, 2005}}</ref>}}
The film's writer-director, [[Sofia Coppola]], has described ''Lost in Translation'' as a story about "things being disconnected and looking for moments of connection",<ref name="thompson" /> a perspective that has been shared by critics and scholars. In a cultural sense, Bob and Charlotte are disoriented by feelings of jet lag and culture shock as a result of foreign travel to Japan. Bob is bewildered by his interactions with a Japanese commercial director whom he cannot understand, realizing that the meaning of his communication is "lost in translation" by an interpreter.<ref name="rich2">{{cite news |last1=Rich |first1=Motoko |title=What Else Was Lost In Translation |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2003/09/21/style/what-else-was-lost-in-translation.html |access-date=June 1, 2020 |work=[[The New York Times]] |date=September 21, 2003 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200304062812/https://www.nytimes.com/2003/09/21/style/what-else-was-lost-in-translation.html |archive-date=March 4, 2020 |url-status=live }}</ref>{{refn|This scene is an homage to a Suntory commercial Coppola's father, [[Francis Ford Coppola]], shot with [[Akira Kurosawa]] in the 1970s.<ref name="grove" /> Coppola said that like Bob, Murray did not understand what the man playing the director was saying. She remarked that "I like the fact that the American actors don't really know what's going on, just like the characters."<ref name="rich2" />|group="note"}} Moreover, both are sleepless from a change in time zone, choosing to cope with their wakefulness by making late-night visits to the hotel bar. Such feelings provoke a sense of estrangement from their environment, but they also exacerbate deeper experiences of alienation and disconnection in their lives.<ref name="haslem" /> Bob and Charlotte are both in troubled marriages and facing similar crises of identity, as Charlotte is unsure of what to do with her life and questions what role she should embrace in the world, while Bob is invariably reminded of his fading stature as a movie star and feels disassociated from the identity by which he is already defined.{{sfn|Rogers|2018|p=73}}

Such experiences are heightened by the characters' contact with the city environment of Tokyo. Bob feels alienated by seeing his likeness used in an advertisement while he is driven from the airport to his hotel, and the colorful cityscape is rendered as a frenetic environment by which he is overwhelmed.{{sfn|Wong|2009|p=133}}{{refn|Some commentators have described Tokyo itself as a third major character in the film. For examples, see {{harvnb|San Filippo|2003|p=28}}, and {{harvnb|Plate|2004|loc=para. 4}}.|group="note"}} Charlotte feels adrift as she attempts to find meaning while wandering Tokyo,{{sfn|Plate|2004|loc=para. 3}} and feels isolated as she peers over the city from her hotel room window.{{sfn|King|2010|p=104}} The Park Hyatt Tokyo offers hermetic qualities that insulate the characters from the city and is the site Bob chooses to seek refuge from his ails.{{sfn|Cardullo|2004|p=464}} These shared impressions of alienation create common ground for Bob and Charlotte to cultivate a personal connection.<ref name="haslem">{{cite journal |last1=Haslem |first1=Wendy |title=Neon Gothic: ''Lost in Translation'' |journal=[[Senses of Cinema]] |date=April 2004 |issue=31 |url=http://sensesofcinema.com/2004/feature-articles/lost_in_translation/ |access-date=June 1, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190911113236/http://sensesofcinema.com/2004/feature-articles/lost_in_translation/ |archive-date=September 11, 2019 |url-status=live |quote=With Coppola's latest film conventions are revised and consequently the expectations of the audience are challenged. }}</ref> When Charlotte invites Bob to experience the Tokyo nightlife, she reduces his sense of distance from the city<ref name="haslem" /> and the two develop a connection based on small moments together. In the little time they have together, each realize they are not alone in seeking a sense of something deeper in their lives.<ref name="chumo">{{cite magazine |last=Chumo |first=Peter N. II |date=January–February 2004 |title=Honoring the Little Moments: Lost in Translation |magazine=Creative Screenwriting |volume=11 |issue=1 |url=https://creativescreenwriting.com/honouring-the-little-moments-lost-in-translation/ |access-date=May 7, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161013094257/http://creativescreenwriting.com/honouring-the-little-moments-lost-in-translation/ |archive-date=October 13, 2016 |url-status=live }}</ref> Coppola, speaking about the brief nature of their encounter, remarked, "For everyone, there are those moments when you have great days with someone you wouldn't expect to. Then you have to go back to your real lives, but it makes an impression on you. It's what makes it so great and enjoyable."<ref name="mitchell" />

Geoff King, a scholar who wrote a book about the film, comments that the experiences of the central characters are one factor that lends ''Lost in Translation'' to varied interpretations by academics.{{sfn|King|2010|p=127}} [[Todd McGowan]] reads the film from a [[Jacques Lacan|Lacanian]] psychoanalytic perspective, arguing that the film encourages the embrace of "absence" in one's life and relationships.{{sfn|McGowan|2007|p=62}} He describes Coppola's depiction of Tokyo "as a city bubbling over with excess", which offers an empty promise of gratification.{{sfn|McGowan|2007|p=54}} In his view, both Bob and Charlotte recognize that they cannot find meaning in Tokyo's attractions, so they bond over their shared sense of emptiness in them.{{sfn|McGowan|2007|p=54}} Lucy Bolton offers a feminist reading, arguing that ''Lost in Translation'' evokes the thought of feminist philosopher [[Luce Irigaray]] by highlighting issues of young womanhood. She argues that the film provides a complex portrait of Charlotte's female subjectivity and an optimistic rendering of the character's pursuit for individual expression.{{sfn|Bolton|2011|pp=126–127}}

===Narrative===
''Lost in Translation'' has been broadly examined in terms of its narrative structure, with commentators noting that it contains few plot events as compared with films in the Hollywood mainstream. Narrative events are mostly focused on the development of Bob and Charlotte's relationship,{{sfn|King|2010|p=76}} with few "external" obstacles that impact the central characters.{{sfn|King|2010|p=82}} King notes, "More time is taken to evoke the impressions, feelings, and experiences of the central characters",{{sfn|King|2010|p=80}} which represents "a shift in the hierarchical arrangement of [film elements]" that prioritizes character experiences over plot.{{sfn|King|2010|pp=81–82}} The literary critic Steve Vineberg argues that "the links of the story are indeed there, only they're not typical cause-and-effect connections. They're formed by the emotions that gather at the end of one episode and pour into the next".<ref name="vineberg">{{cite magazine |last1=Vineberg |first1=Steve |title=Jet lag |magazine=[[The Christian Century]] |date=October 17, 2003 |volume=120 |issue=21 |page=60 |url=https://www.christiancentury.org/reviews/2011-04/jet-lag |access-date=June 1, 2020}}</ref> King maintains that while the plot does progress according to a basic linear causality,{{sfn|King|2010|p=76}} "If the episodic quality often seems to the fore, this is partly a matter of the pacing of individual sequences that are very often leisurely and dedicated to the establishment or development of mood and atmospherics".{{sfn|King|2010|p=80}} Coppola said she wanted the story to emphasize the qualities of an intimate moment, and she did not want to impose grandiose narrative devices on the characters such as "a war keeping them apart".{{sfn|''Interview''|2003|p=56}}{{refn|Coppola has acknowledged her appreciation for "meandering mood pieces"<ref name="chumo" /> and cited influences for ''Lost in Translation'' from films such as ''[[L'Avventura]]'', ''[[La Dolce Vita]]'',<ref name="allen" /> and ''[[In the Mood for Love]]''.<ref name="crabtree" /> ''La Dolce Vita'' is briefly featured by Coppola in a scene in which Bob and Charlotte are watching the film on television while drinking [[sake]].|group="note"}}

''Lost in Translation'' has also been noted for defying the conventions of mainstream romantic films. Film historian Wendy Haslem writes that the classic romantic comedy assures the audience that the couple has a future, but Coppola defies expectations by refusing to unite the central characters.<ref name="haslem" /> She points to elements such as Bob and Charlotte's lack of sexual consummation as one factor that obscures whether their pairing is more romantic or platonic. Writing about the concluding sequence in which the characters make their final goodbyes, Haslem argues, "Conventionally in mainstream cinema, the kiss{{nbsp}}... signifies resolution by reinforcing the myth of romantic love. But in this new wave of contemporary anti-romance romance, the kiss signifies ambiguity."<ref name="haslem" /> The academic Nicholas Y.B. Wong contends that the film's lack of "heart-melting connections and melodramatic (re)unions between characters" represents a [[Postmodernism|postmodern]] portrait of love, writing that ''Lost in Translation'' is "about non-love, the predominance of affairs and the complexities of intimacy. Characters vacillate between falling in love and out of love. They are neither committed to someone nor emotionally unattached."{{sfn|Wong|2009|p=133}} Coppola said Bob and Charlotte's relationship is "supposed to be romantic but on the edge.{{nbsp}}... [A] little bit more than friends but not an actual romance.{{nbsp}}... To me, it's pretty un-sexual between them—innocent and romantic, and a friendship."<ref name="focusinterview" />

===Opening shot===
[[Image:Lost in Translation Opening Shot.png|thumb|upright=1.3|alt=The backside of Charlotte lying down in a gray sweater and transparent pink panties.|The opening shot{{refn|Johansson was initially apprehensive about wearing sheer panties for the shot, but she conceded after Coppola showed her what they looked like by modeling them personally.<ref name="vernon" /> Johansson said that a male director would not have been able to convince her to wear them.<ref name="hirschberg" />|group="note"}}]]
The film's opening shot, a 36-second take of Charlotte's lower back and buttocks as she lies on a bed wearing [[Transparency and translucency|semi-transparent]] [[panties|pink panties]] and a gray sweater with white T-shirt underneath, as the credits begin to roll, has been another point of discussion among critics and scholars. It is based on the photorealist paintings of [[John Kacere]]<ref name="stern" /> and has often been compared to the initial appearance of [[Brigitte Bardot]]'s character Camille Javal in the 1963 film ''[[Contempt (film)|Contempt]]''.{{refn|For examples, see {{harvnb|Kennedy|2010|pp=46–47}}, {{harvnb|San Filippo|2003|p=26}}, and {{harvnb|Haslem|2004}}.|group="note"}}

''[[Slate (website)|Slate]]'' critic [[David Edelstein]] called the scene the only "head-scratcher" in the film, which he otherwise appreciated:<ref name="edelstein">{{cite magazine |last1=Edelstein |first1=David |date=September 11, 2003 |title=Prisoner of Japan |url=https://slate.com/culture/2003/09/bill-murray-opens-up-in-lost-in-translation.html |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200922092419/https://slate.com/culture/2003/09/bill-murray-opens-up-in-lost-in-translation.html |archive-date=September 22, 2020 |access-date=May 12, 2020 |magazine=[[Slate (magazine)|Slate]]}}</ref>
{{blockquote|The meaning of this image is less transparent than Johansson's attire, but my guess is that Coppola wants us to see the whole film as the vaguely erotic dream of an alienated young woman. She wants to make this woman's detachment from this culture, and from her own body, hypnotically sexy, and to put the longing for human connection into your bloodstream from the first frame. But I was mostly thinking about her butt crack.}}

It has been described as a foreshadowing of a romance between Bob and Charlotte,{{refn|For example, see {{cite magazine |last1=Smith |first1=Paul Julian |title=Tokyo Drifters |magazine=[[Sight & Sound]] |date=2004 |volume=14 |issue=1 |page=13}}|group="note"}} but other writers have a different interpretation.  Correspondingly, the academic [[Maria San Filippo]] maintains that "[Coppola] doesn't seem to be making a statement at all beyond a sort of endorsement of beauty for beauty's sake."{{sfn|San Filippo|2003|p=26}} King notes that the image contains both "subtle" and "obvious" appeal in its combination of aesthetic and erotic qualities, which signifies ''Lost in Translation''{{'}}s position between [[Major film studio|mainstream]] and [[independent film]].{{sfn|King|2010|pp=1–2}}

Film scholar Todd Kennedy interprets it in terms of an invocation and inversion of feminist film theorist [[Laura Mulvey]]'s conception of the [[male gaze]], arguing that the shot "lasts so long as to become awkward—forcing the audience to become aware of (and potentially even question) their participation in the gaze".{{sfn|Kennedy|2010|p=45}} Other critics also see it as inherently subverting the objectification of Charlotte. Haslem argues that "Coppola's intention with this opening shot appears to be to defy taboos and to undermine expectations surrounding what might be considered the 'money shot' in more traditionally exploitative cinema."<ref name="haslem" /> Bolton points out that Charlotte's "state of undress is not designed to be seen by anyone else, as she is alone in the room. Her solitary, meditative state de-sexualizes her appearance by naturalizing her appearance as the state of undress a woman would be likely to adopt if she was on her own."{{sfn|Bolton|2011|pp=109–110}} Fiona Handyside elaborates on Kennedy's notion, that the stillness of the image reduces its voyeuristic appeal and "the very weight of time pulls [Charlotte] from being pure empty iconic spectacle and into the material matter of history itself".<ref name="Fiona Handyside">{{cite book |last1=Handyside |first1=Fiona |title=Sofia Coppola: A Cinema of Girlhood |date=2017 |publisher=[[I.B. Tauris]] |location=London |isbn=9781786721600 |page=Figure 2.7 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=79SLDwAAQBAJ |access-date=February 8, 2024}}</ref>

"I don't have a really good reason for it", Coppola said when asked to explain the shot. "It's just how I wanted to start the movie. I liked having a hint of the character—a sweet, young girl waiting around in her hotel room—and then go into the story."<ref name="diaconescu" /> Suzanne Ferriss, a film scholar who has written extensively about Coppola's work, notes that in the original screenplay, the film opened with Harris's arrival and then went to Charlotte in the hotel. Due to this change, Coppola's comment belies the complexity of the shot. Ferriss observes that the first 10 seconds lead the audience to believe it might be looking at a still, until Charlotte shifts her upper leg:{{sfn|Ferriss|2023|pp=26–27}}
{{blockquote|... [linking] moving and still images, demonstrating an awareness of fine art tradition and a recognition that film is, in fact, a series of still images projected at 24 frames per second. Second, it highlights cinema's unique ability to make meaning through editing ... The film's opening is a bold assertion of cinema's status as an art, like the painting it references, references, or literature, which similarly relies on the audience's imaginative engagement to find meaning...}}

===Ending and the whisper===
The film's concluding scene—in which Bob and Charlotte embrace in a Tokyo street and he whispers inaudibly in her ear—has generated sustained commentary over two decades.<ref name="Nayman">{{cite web |last=Nayman |first=Adam |title=We'll Never Know Everything About 'Lost in Translation' |url=https://www.theringer.com/2023/09/26/movies/lost-in-translation-review-anniversary-sofia-coppola-scarlett-johansson-bill-murray |website=The Ringer |date=September 26, 2023 |access-date=August 24, 2025}}</ref> Coppola said she did not write the line, and that Murray said it was "something that should stay between them".<ref name="Syme">{{cite magazine |last=Syme |first=Rachel |title=Sofia Coppola's Path to Filming Gilded Adolescence |url=https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2024/01/29/sofia-coppola-profile/ |magazine=The New Yorker |date=January 22, 2024 |access-date=August 24, 2025}}</ref>

Viewers have attempted to determine the words, including a 2007 video that claimed to isolate the dialogue by digitally enhancing the soundtrack. ''Entertainment Weekly'' highlighted the clip while cautioning that its accuracy was uncertain.<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Bernardin |first=Marc |date=December 14, 2007 |title=Revealing the 'Lost in Translation' whisper |url=https://ew.com/article/2007/12/14/lost-in-transla/ |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231001231413/https://ew.com/article/2007/12/14/lost-in-transla/ |archive-date=2023-10-01 |access-date=August 24, 2025 |magazine=Entertainment Weekly}}</ref> Neither Coppola nor the actors have endorsed any transcription, and subsequent criticism has emphasized that the unknowability of the line is integral to the ending.<ref name="Syme"/><ref name="Nayman"/>

|date=September 26, 2023 |access-date=August 24, 2025}}</ref><ref name="StanfordDialy-2021">{{Cite web |date=2021-05-19 |title=The egregious racism against Asians in 'Lost in Translation' |url=https://stanforddaily.com/2021/05/19/the-egregious-racism-against-asians-in-lost-in-translation/?ref=stanfordreview.org |access-date=2026-05-09 |language=en-US}}</ref> ''[[Bloomberg News|Bloomberg]]''{{'}}s Gearoid Reidy wrote on the film's 20th anniversary in 2023: "The movie’s obsession with Japanese pronunciation of Rs and Ls, most famously with the sex worker who visits Harris’s room, has aged quite badly — especially as neither of the main characters attempts to learn much Japanese beyond ''[[wiktionary:乾杯#Japanese|kanpai]]'' ... None of the Japanese characters have any agency; all of them are stereotypes."<ref name="Bloomberg" /> In 2024, Morgan Giles wrote for ''[[The Times Literary Supplement]]'' (''TLS''): “On average the film makes a joke about Japanese accents every twelve minutes (and let’s not even mention the jokes about height) ... even in 2003, those jokes just weren’t funny any more.”<ref>{{Cite web |title=Reflecting on Lost in Translation, twenty years on |url=https://www.the-tls.com/regular-features/in-brief/lost-in-translation-suzanne-ferriss-book-review-morgan-giles |access-date=2026-07-14 |website=TLS |language=en-US}}</ref> In a 2024 retrospective for ''[[Tokyo Weekender]]'', Cezary Jan Strusiewicz compared the characters' behavior of "letting Tokyo be their playground" to contemporary issues with [[overtourism]] and disruptive tourists in Japan. Strusiewicz remarked that "there's no way [Coppola] could have predicted that foreigners being annoying in public would become a problem in Japan in 20 years."<ref>{{Cite web |last=Strusiewicz |first=Cezary Jan |date=2024-06-28 |title=Lost in Translation: 20 Years Later |url=https://www.tokyoweekender.com/entertainment/movies-tv/lost-in-translation-20-years-later/ |access-date=2026-07-14 |website=Tokyo Weekender |language=en}}</ref>

The film has remained closely associated with the [[Park Hyatt Tokyo]]—especially its New York Bar—which became a recurring destination for visitors seeking to recreate scenes from the movie. Coverage marking the movie’s anniversary described the hotel as having “been Tokyo’s most famous luxury hotel” since the film and noted that guests still visit for that connection.<ref>{{cite web |last=Zohar |first=Erica Wertheim |title=Park Hyatt Tokyo: 20 Years After Lost In Translation, This Iconic Property Looks Ahead To Its Sequel |url=https://www.forbes.com/sites/ericawertheimzohar/2023/09/07/park-hyatt-tokyo-20-years-after-lost-in-translation-this-iconic-property-looks-ahead-to-its-sequel/ |website=[[Forbes]] |date=September 7, 2023 |access-date=August 24, 2025}}</ref>

The line “For relaxing times, make it Suntory time,” delivered by Murray’s character in a whisky commercial, became a widely quoted catchphrase in U.S. media and helped cement the association between the film and Japanese whisky in the popular imagination.<ref>{{cite news |last=Wilson |first=Jason |title=It's Suntory time |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/lifestyle/food/its-suntory-time/2012/01/11/gIQAjuVy5P_story.html |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |date=January 11, 2012 |access-date=August 24, 2025}}</ref> In 2023, the House of Suntory marked its centennial with an anniversary spot directed by Coppola and starring [[Keanu Reeves]] that explicitly nodded to the film and its “Suntory time” legacy.<ref>{{cite news |last=Springer |first=Jon |title=Suntory channels 'Lost in Translation' in 100th anniversary ad starring Keanu Reeves |url=https://adage.com/article/marketing-news-strategy/suntory-channels-lost-translation-100th-anniversary-ad-starring-keanu-reeves/2496746 |work=[[Ad Age]] |date=May 24, 2023 |access-date=August 24, 2025}}</ref>

Critics and profiles at the time also identified the film as a major career turning point for [[Scarlett Johansson]], signaling her transition to adult roles and mainstream stardom.<ref>{{cite news |last=Vernon |first=Polly |title=Scarlett fever |url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2003/dec/28/features.magazine |work=[[The Guardian]] |date=December 28, 2003 |access-date=August 24, 2025}}</ref>
