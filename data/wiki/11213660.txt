'''''The Natural''''' is a 1984 American [[sports]] [[drama film]] based on [[Bernard Malamud]]'s 1952 [[The Natural|novel of the same name]], directed by [[Barry Levinson]], and starring [[Robert Redford]], [[Robert Duvall]], [[Glenn Close]], [[Kim Basinger]], [[Wilford Brimley]], [[Barbara Hershey]], [[Robert Prosky]] and [[Richard Farnsworth]].<ref name=sivaswrcl>{{cite magazine |url=https://www.si.com/vault/1984/05/07/626594/a-star-with-real-clout |magazine=Sports Illustrated |last=Fimrite |first=Ron |title=A star with real clout |date=May 7, 1984 |page=92 |access-date=September 7, 2018 |archive-date=April 3, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190403101049/https://www.si.com/vault/1984/05/07/626594/a-star-with-real-clout |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name=sivhomyth>{{cite web|url=https://www.si.com/vault/1984/05/21/627371/the-natural-hit-or-myth |magazine=Sports Illustrated |last=Deford |first=Frank |author-link=Frank Deford |agency=(Movies) |title=''The Natural'': hit or myth? |date=May 21, 1984 |page=71 |access-date=September 7, 2018 |archive-date=September 6, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190906042219/https://www.si.com/vault/1984/05/21/627371/the-natural-hit-or-myth |url-status=live }}</ref> Like the novel, the film recounts the experiences of Roy Hobbs, a man with great "natural" [[baseball]] talent, spanning his long but interrupted career in the sport. In direct contrast to the novel, the film ends on a positive tone. It was the first film produced by [[TriStar Pictures|Tri-Star Pictures]].

The film was nominated for four [[57th Academy Awards|Academy Awards]], including [[Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress|Best Supporting Actress]] (Close), and for a [[Golden Globe Award for Best Supporting Actress – Motion Picture|Golden Globe Award for Best Supporting Actress]] (Basinger). Many of the baseball scenes were filmed in 1983 at [[War Memorial Stadium (Buffalo, New York)|War Memorial Stadium]] in [[Buffalo, New York]], built in 1937 and demolished in 1989. [[All-High Stadium]], also in Buffalo, stood in for [[Chicago]]'s [[Wrigley Field]] in a key scene.

==Plot==
In the 1910s [[Midwestern United States|Midwest]], Roy Hobbs learns baseball from his father Ed, who suffers a fatal heart attack. Lightning strikes a nearby tree, from which Roy makes a [[baseball bat|bat]] that he brands with a lightning bolt and the legend "Wonderboy."

In 1923, 19-year-old Roy leaves behind his girlfriend, Iris Gaines, for a tryout with the [[Chicago Cubs]] arranged by [[Scout (sports)|scout]] Sam Simpson. On the train, Sam and Roy are snubbed by "The Whammer," a swaggering batter in the mold of [[Babe Ruth]]<ref>{{cite web |last=Merron |first=Jeff |title=Reel Life: ‘The Natural’ |url=https://www.espn.com/page2/s/closer/020918.html#:~:text=In%20Reel%20Life:%20It's%201923,him%20out%20on%20three%20pitches. |website=ESPN Page 2 |publisher=ESPN Inc. |access-date=30 March 2026}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |last1=Lehman |first1=Bert |last2=Ferrante |first2=Paul |date=15 May 2017 |title=The Natural stands the test of time more than 30 years later |url=https://sportscollectorsdigest.com/news/the-natural-baseball-movie |website=Sports Collectors Digest |publisher=Active Interest Media |access-date=30 March 2026}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |last=Searle |first=Matt |date=28 May 2020 |title=The Natural: The Dark History Behind a Feel-Good Film |url=https://searlebaseball.com/the-natural/#:~:text=of%20Fame%20plaque.-,Searle's%20Final%20Say,is%20currently%20available%20on%20Netflix. |website=Searle Basebell |publisher=Matthew Searle |access-date=30 March 2026}}</ref> and [[Sports journalism|sportswriter]] Max Mercy. While stopped at a carnival, Sam bets Max that Roy can [[strike out]] the Whammer in three pitches. Roy does so, drawing the attention of Harriet Bird, who has a murderous fixation on athletes. Harriet lures Roy to her [[Chicago]] hotel room, shoots him in the stomach, then throws herself out the window. Health complications derail Roy's baseball career.

Despite his age and unknown past, in 1939 the 35-year-old Roy is signed to the New York Knights, [[Major League Baseball]]'s last-place team. The team's manager and part owner, Pop Fisher, nearly demotes Roy to the [[Class B (baseball)|Class B]] league but is impressed by his power hitting during batting practice. During a game against the [[Philadelphia Phillies]], Roy [[pinch hit]]s for star player Bartholomew "Bump" Bailey. Using "Wonderboy," Roy hits a triple that knocks the cover off the ball, making him an overnight sensation.

News of Roy's growing fame reaches Iris, now living in Chicago. In a game against the [[St. Louis Cardinals]], Bump dies after crashing through the outfield wall while trying to make a catch. Roy becomes the starting right fielder and his hitting pulls the Knights up the [[National League (baseball)|National League]] standings. Assistant manager Red Blow reveals that Pop was forced to sell [[controlling interest]] in the team to a new owner, the Judge, but will regain ownership if the Knights win the [[League Championship Series|pennant]]. Unlike Bump, Roy refuses the Judge's bribe to [[Match fixing|throw the season]].

At practice, Max sees Roy pitch a fastball that almost tears through the backstop's netting, then recognizes him from striking out the Whammer. He introduces Roy to [[bookie]] Gus Sands, and Roy begins a romance with Gus's confidante Memo Paris, Pop's niece. Pop believes Memo is bad luck and Roy and the Knights drop into a slump, which continues into a [[Wrigley Field]] game against the Cubs. Spotting Iris in the stands, Roy shatters the scoreboard clock with a game-winning home run. They meet briefly and after another win the following day, Roy walks Iris home, and she reveals she has a teenage son named Ted. Their reunion restores Roy's prowess and the Knights surge into first place. 

At a party, Roy collapses and is taken to a hospital. A doctor discovers Harriet's [[silver bullet]], shows it to Roy, and tells him it may prove fatal if he continues playing. The Knights lose three games in a row, setting up a [[one-game playoff]] against the [[Pittsburgh Pirates]] at Knights Field. The Judge offers Roy $20,000 ({{Inflation|US|20000|1939|fmt=eq|r=-3}}) to throw the game and reveals he has already bribed another player. When Roy refuses, the Judge threatens to reveal photos of Harriet's body and blame Roy for her death. Roy rejects the bribe and is not intimidated by the blackmail.

During the game, Roy deduces that the Knights' pitcher was also bribed but convinces him to play his best. In the dugout, Roy receives a note from Iris, who is attending the game with Ted, and learns that he is Ted's father. With the Knights trailing in the bottom of the ninth inning, Roy hits a [[foul ball]] that splits "Wonderboy." He tells [[batboy]] Bobby Savoy to choose another bat and Bobby retrieves the "Savoy Special", a bat he made with Roy's guidance. Bleeding through his uniform, Roy smashes a home run into the stadium lights, destroying them in a [[short circuit|power surge]] that cascades into a shower of sparks, [[Walk-off home run|winning the game]] and the pennant for the Knights. Back on their farm, Iris watches Roy and Ted play catch in the same field where Roy and Ed used to play.

==Production==

=== Pre-production ===
Malcolm Kahn and Robert Bean acquired the rights to [[Bernard Malamud]]'s 1952 novel ''[[The Natural]]'' in 1976.<ref name=AFI>{{AFI film|67313}}</ref> [[Phil Dusenberry]] wrote the first adaptation.<ref name=AFI/> In October 1981, Roger Towne, a [[Columbia Pictures]] story editor and brother of [[Robert Towne]], quit to produce and write the screenplay, with Bean set to direct and Kahn co-producing.<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=October 7, 1981|page=7|title=Towne Adapts and Produces 'Natural'}}</ref><ref name=AFI/> In 1983, newly formed [[Tri-Star Pictures]] acquired the rights to the film adaptation, its first production.<ref name="AFI" /> It was [[Robert Redford]]'s first acting role in three years.<ref name="AFI" /> The film's producers stated in the DVD extras that the film was not intended to be a literal adaptation of the novel, but was merely "based on" the novel. Malamud's daughter said on one of the DVD extras that her father had seen the film and felt that it had "legitimized him as a writer."<ref name="DVD-lightning-JMS">{{cite video | people=Janna Malamud Smith (daughter of [[Bernard Malamud]]) | date=April 3, 2007 | title=When Lightning Strikes: Creating The Natural | medium=Documentary | publisher=Sony Pictures Entertainment}}</ref>

=== Casting ===
[[Darren McGavin]] was cast late in the process as the gambler Gus Sands and was uncredited in the film. Out of a disagreement, he chose not to be credited, though [[Barry Levinson]] later wanted to credit him and McGavin refused.<ref name=" DVD-lightning-bl"/><ref>{{cite news | last=Heldenfels | first=Rich | title=Mailbag: Why do TV shows run longer than scheduled? | url=http://www.ohio.com/the330entertainment/heldenfels/mailbag-why-do-tv-shows-run-longer-than-scheduled-1.313843 | work=[[Akron Beacon-Journal]] | date=June 14, 2012 | access-date=June 15, 2012 | archive-date=August 8, 2014 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140808194332/http://www.ohio.com/the330entertainment/heldenfels/mailbag-why-do-tv-shows-run-longer-than-scheduled-1.313843 | url-status=live }}</ref> Levinson stated on the DVD extras for the 2007 edition that because there had been too little time during post-production to find a professional announcer willing and able to provide [[voice-over]] services, Levinson had recorded that part of the audio track himself.<ref name="DVD-lightning-bl">{{cite video | people=[[Barry Levinson]] (director) | date=April 3, 2007 | title=When Lightning Strikes: Creating The Natural | medium=Documentary | publisher=Sony Pictures Entertainment}}</ref>

=== Filming ===
Two-thirds of the scenes were filmed in [[Buffalo, New York]], mostly at [[War Memorial Stadium (Buffalo, New York)|War Memorial Stadium]],<ref>{{cite news | title=Film Starring Redford To Be Shot in Buffalo | work=[[The New York Times]] | url=https://query.nytimes.com/gst/fullpage.html?res=9801EFD6153BF93BA25755C0A965948260 | date=June 18, 1983 | access-date=October 31, 2008 | archive-date=July 5, 2023 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230705204747/https://www.nytimes.com/1983/06/18/movies/film-starring-redford-to-be-shot-in-buffalo.html | url-status=live }}</ref> built in 1937 and demolished in 1989. At the time of filming, the stadium was the home of the [[Buffalo Bisons]], a [[Minor League Baseball]] team who were then playing in the [[Double-A (baseball)|Double A]] level [[Eastern League (1938–present)|Eastern League]] and would move up to [[Triple-A (baseball)|Triple A]] and the [[International League]] in 1985 (that team was actually the second Buffalo Bisons to play at the stadium. The [[Buffalo Bisons (1886–1970)|original Bisons]] had actually played in the Triple A International League and were tenants at the stadium from 1961–1970). With a spectator capacity of 46,206, it was the second-largest venue in the Minors in 1983/84 (behind [[Denver]]'s then–75,123 capacity [[Mile High Stadium]]), and the sixth-largest regular Minor League stadium in history.

Although War Memorial Stadium had long been used as a baseball stadium, it was in fact a rectangular venue built primarily for [[American football|football]]. As such, it had both right and right-center field fences between 20 and 30 feet closer to the diamond than those in left field. The film's directors were careful not to include any high shots of the stadium that would show the compromised field dimensions (especially since [[Robert Redford]]'s Roy Hobbs character is a left-handed [[power hitter]], right field being his natural pulling side), while any views of the right-field fence and stands were shot from ground level, no closer than the pitcher's mound, to make the stadium appear to be a [[baseball field]] of regulation size. Balls Roy hits into the stands are shown in slow motion  to exaggerate their distance. Since the diamond was close to the field's northwest corner, the directors were also careful not to show the long distance between the left-field outfield fences and the [[Bleacher|bleacher seats]] at the stadium's eastern end. The home plate was in the northwest corner, placing the diamond on the opposite side of the field from the main grandstand and press box, so that the main stand for football became the center-field bleachers during baseball games.

Buffalo's [[All-High Stadium]], with post-production alterations, stood in for [[Chicago]]'s [[Wrigley Field]] in a key scene in the film.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.buffalonews.com/entertainment/story/305911.html|title=Wrigley Field in Buffalo|access-date=October 5, 2008|publisher=buffalonews.com|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080324195755/http://www.buffalonews.com/entertainment/story/305911.html|archive-date=March 24, 2008}}</ref> Additional filming took place at the [[New York and Lake Erie Railroad]] depot in [[South Dayton, New York]].<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.chautauquatoday.com/news/details.cfm?id=92349#.WEzbL_krLIU|title=South Dayton remembers filming of|work=[[WDOE]]|access-date=2016-12-11|archive-date=2016-12-21|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161221193732/http://www.chautauquatoday.com/news/details.cfm?id=92349#.WEzbL_krLIU|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{Cite magazine |date=December 1983 |title=Railroad News Photos |url=https://www.trains.com/trn/magazine/archive-access/trains-december-1983/ |url-access=limited |access-date=February 12, 2024 |magazine=[[Trains (magazine)|Trains]] |publisher=[[Kalmbach Media|Kalmbach Publishing]] |page=19 |volume=44 |issue=2}}</ref> [[Cece Carlucci]], an [[umpire (baseball)|umpire]] from the [[Pacific Coast League]], made the umpiring gear used in the film.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.newspapers.com/clip/92473841/umpire-batting-one-thousand/ |title=Umpire batting one thousand |work=The Californian |page=B-7 |via=Newspapers.com |date=December 20, 1991 |access-date=January 19, 2022 |archive-date=January 19, 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220119052843/https://www.newspapers.com/clip/92473841/umpire-batting-one-thousand/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

==== Cinematography and Post-Production ====
Director of photography [[Caleb Deschanel]] had a long-running fascination for [[Autochrome Lumière|autochrome photography]], the earliest commercial color process. This photographic process, invented by the [[Lumière brothers]] in 1907, involved light filtered through randomly-aligned grains of potato starch that had been dyed in the primary colors and then used to expose a black and white glass negative. When developed and seen in transmission, like a film slide (the only means of viewing an autochrome), the effect was a somewhat desaturated color [[Monoprinting|monoprint]] made from three, sandwiched glass panes with a [[Dynamic range|tonal range]] reminiscent of watercolors. Deschanel searched for a way to replicate this look for the flashback scenes in ''The Natural''. 

He hit upon an unusual post-production process. Deschanel struck a black and white print of the film and used an [[optical printer]] to combine this footage with a color print of the same material in which the color saturations had been strongly raised. The result was an [[answer print]] with delicate, bloomed colors and a strong black register. Because the effect diminished in generations more distant from the original [[Interpositive|interpositive prints]], Deschanel's intentions for the film are best seen in high-resolution, home media formats such as [[Ultra HD Blu-ray|4K Blu-ray]] releases.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Team Deakins: Caleb Deschanel - Cinematographer |url=https://teamdeakins.libsyn.com/caleb-deschanel-cinematographer |access-date=2026-04-03 |website=teamdeakins.libsyn.com |language=en}}</ref>

==Release==

===Reception===
''[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]'' called it an "impeccably made ... fable about success and failure in America."<ref name="reviews" /> [[James Berardinelli]] praised ''The Natural'' as "[a]rguably the best baseball movie ever made".<ref name="reviews"/> [[ESPN]]'s Page 2 selected it as the sixth best sports movie of all time.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.espn.com/page2/movies/s/top20/fulllist.html|title=Page 2's Top 20 Sports Movies of All-Time|work=ESPN.com|access-date=June 12, 2009|archive-date=February 13, 2010|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100213022513/http://espn.go.com/page2/movies/s/top20/fulllist.html|url-status=live}}</ref> Sports writer [[Bill Simmons]] has argued, "Any 'Best Sports Movies' list that doesn't feature either ''[[Hoosiers (film)|Hoosiers]]'' or ''The Natural'' as the No. 1 pick shouldn't even count."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.espn.com/page2/movies/s/simmons/020830.html|title=Holy trilogy of the 'Karate Kid'|last=Simmons|first=Bill|work=ESPN.com|access-date=June 12, 2009|archive-date=February 10, 2009|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090210190127/http://espn.go.com/page2/movies/s/simmons/020830.html|url-status=live}}</ref> Director Barry Levinson said on [[MLB Network]]'s ''Costas at the Movies'' in 2013 that while the film is based in fantasy, "through the years, these things which are outlandish actually [happen] ... like [[Kirk Gibson]] hitting [[1988 World Series#Game 1|the home run]] and limping around the bases ... [[Curt Schilling]] with the [[2004 American League Championship Series#Game 6|blood on the sock]] in the [[World Series]]."<ref>[http://mlb.mlb.com/video/play.jsp?content_id=25588745&topic_id=7417714 Barry Levinson, ''Costas at the Movies''], ''MLB Network'', February 11, 2013</ref>

[[Leonard Maltin]]'s 18th annual ''[[Leonard Maltin's Movie Guide|Movie Guide]]'' edition called it "too long and inconsistent." Dan Craft, longtime critic for the [[Bloomington, Illinois]] paper, ''[[The Pantagraph]]'',<ref>(May 19, 1984)</ref> wrote, "The storybook ending is so preposterous you don't know whether to cheer or jeer." In ''[[Sports Illustrated]]'', [[Frank Deford]] had [[Damning with faint praise|faint praise]] for it: "''The Natural'' almost manages to be a {{nowrap|swell movie."<ref name=sivhomyth/>}} [[John Simon (critic)|John Simon]] of ''[[National Review]]'' and [[Richard Schickel]] of ''[[Time (magazine)|Time]]'' were disappointed with the adaptation. Simon contrasted Malamud's story about the "failure of American innocence" with Levinson's "fable of success ... [and] the ultimate triumph of semi-doltish purity," declaring "you have, not Malamud's novel, but a sorry illustration of its theme".<ref>{{cite magazine | last = Simon | first = John | title = The Natural | issue = 36 | pages = 51–2 | magazine = [[National Review]] | date = July 13, 1984}}</ref> Schickel lamented that "Malamud's intricate ending (it is a victory that looks like a defeat) is vulgarized (the victory is now an unambiguous triumph, fireworks included)," and that "watching this movie is all too often like reading about ''The Natural'' in the ''College Outline'' {{nowrap|series."<ref>{{cite magazine | last = Schickel | first = Richard | title = The Natural | issue = 123 | page = 91 | magazine = [[Time (magazine)|Time]] | date = May 14, 1984}}</ref>}}

[[Roger Ebert]] called it "idolatry on behalf of Robert Redford."<ref name="ebert">{{cite web |last=Ebert |first=Roger |author-link=Roger Ebert |title=The Natural |publisher=rogerebert.com |date=January 1, 1984 |url=http://rogerebert.suntimes.com/apps/pbcs.dll/article?AID=/19840101/REVIEWS/401010363/1023 |access-date=January 10, 2008 |archive-date=April 21, 2007 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070421151438/http://rogerebert.suntimes.com/apps/pbcs.dll/article?AID=/19840101/REVIEWS/401010363/1023 |url-status=live}}</ref> Ebert's television ''[[At the Movies (1986 TV program)|At the Movies]]'' collaborator [[Gene Siskel]] praised it, giving it four stars, also putting down other critics that he suggested might have just recently read the novel for the {{nowrap|first time.<ref>{{cite news | last = Siskel | first = Gene | title = 'The Natural': Redford scores in an uplifting celebration of the individual | pages = D A1 | newspaper = Chicago Tribune | date = May 11, 1984 }}</ref>}} In a lengthy article on baseball movies in ''[[The New Yorker]]'', [[Roger Angell]] pointed out that Malamud had intentionally treated Roy's story as a baseball version of the [[King Arthur]] legend, which came across in the film as a bit heavy-handed, "portentous and stuffy," and that the book's ending should have been kept. He also cited several excellent visuals and funny bits and noted that  Redford had prepared so carefully for the role, modeling his swing on that of [[Ted Williams]], that "you want to sign {{nowrap|him up."<ref>{{cite magazine|first=Roger|last=Angell|title=No, But I Saw The Game|magazine=The New Yorker|date=July 31, 1989|page=41}}</ref>}}

The film [[review aggregator]] website [[Rotten Tomatoes]] retrospectively compiled reviews from 48 critics to give the film a score of 83%, with an average rating of 7.2/10. The website's consensus reads: "Though heavy with sentiment, ''The Natural'' is an irresistible classic, and a sincere testament to America's {{nowrap|national pastime."<ref name="reviews">{{cite web |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/natural/ |title=The Natural (1984) |work=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |publisher=[[Fandango Media]] |access-date=December 28, 2024 |archive-date=June 24, 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220624034026/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/natural |url-status=live }}</ref>}} The film received a Metacritic score of 61 based on 19 reviews, indicating "generally favorable reviews".<ref>{{cite web| url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-natural| title=The Natural| website=Metacritic| access-date=2021-02-26| archive-date=2020-11-27| archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201127224608/https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-natural| url-status=live}}</ref>

Graham]], [[Bruce Weintraub]]) and [[Academy Award for Best Original Score|Best Original Score]] ([[Randy Newman]]) at the [[57th Academy Awards]].<ref>{{cite web| title =Academy Awards Database: The Natural (57th-1984)| publisher =[[Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences]]| url =http://awardsdatabase.oscars.org/ampas_awards/DisplayMain.jsp?curTime=1200879955474| archive-url =https://archive.today/20120711154212/http://awardsdatabase.oscars.org/ampas_awards/DisplayMain.jsp?curTime=1200879955474| url-status =dead| archive-date =July 11, 2012| access-date =January 20, 2008}}</ref> [[Kim Basinger]] was also nominated for the [[Golden Globe Award for Best Supporting Actress – Motion Picture|Golden Globe Award for Best Supporting Actress]] at the [[42nd Golden Globes]].<ref name="NY Times">{{cite web |url=https://movies.nytimes.com/movie/34607/The-Natural/awards |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100808193613/http://movies.nytimes.com/movie/34607/The-Natural/awards |url-status=dead |archive-date=August 8, 2010 |department=Movies & TV Dept. |work=[[The New York Times]] |date=2010 |title=NY Times: The Natural |access-date=January 1, 2009}}</ref>

==Home media==
The initial DVD edition, with copyright year on the box reading "2001", contained the theatrical version of the film, along with a few specials and commentaries.

The "director's cut" was released on April 3, 2007.<ref name="monstersandcritics1">{{cite web| title =DVD - The Natural (Director's Cut)| publisher =Monsters and Critics.com, WotR Ltd.| url =http://dvd.monstersandcritics.com/archive/dvdarchive.php/The_Natural_%28Director%27s_Cut%29/10359| archive-url =https://archive.today/20130129034150/http://dvd.monstersandcritics.com/archive/dvdarchive.php/The_Natural_(Director's_Cut)/10359| url-status =dead| archive-date =January 29, 2013| access-date =January 20, 2008}}</ref> A two-disc edition, it contains the featurette "The Heart of the Natural," a 44-minute documentary featuring comments from [[Cal Ripken Jr.]] and Levinson; it is the only extra released originally with the 2001 DVD. Sony added a number of other extras, however, including: "When Lightning Strikes: Creating ''The Natural''," a 50-minute documentary discussing the origins of the original novel and the production of the film; "Knights in Shining Armor," which addresses the mythological parallels between ''The Natural'', King Arthur and the ''[[Odyssey]]''; and "A Natural Gunned Down" which tells the story of [[Eddie Waitkus]], a baseball player who was shot by female stalker [[Ruth Ann Steinhagen]] in an incident which some think inspired the fictionalized shooting of Roy Hobbs.<ref name="monstersandcritics2">{{cite web | last = Tezer | first = Adnan | title = DVD Review: The Natural (Director's Cut) | publisher = Monsters and Critics.com, WotR Ltd. | date = April 1, 2007 | url = http://dvd.monstersandcritics.com/reviews/article_1285572.php | access-date = January 20, 2008 | url-status = dead | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20080121153131/http://dvd.monstersandcritics.com/reviews/article_1285572.php | archive-date = January 21, 2008 | df = mdy-all }}</ref><ref>{{cite web|last=Edelman|first=Rob|title=Eddie Waitkus and "The Natural": What is Assumption? What is Fact?|url=https://sabr.org/journal/article/eddie-waitkus-and-the-natural-what-is-assumption-what-is-fact/|access-date=28 March 2026}}</ref>  The film itself has been re-edited, restoring deleted footage to the early chapters of the story. These scenes expand on Roy's sadness, focusing on his visits to his childhood home as an adult and his childhood memories.<ref name="monstersandcritics2" /> The "gift set" version of the release also included some souvenirs: a baseball "signed" by Roy Hobbs; some baseball cards of Hobbs and teammates; and a New York Knights cap.

The 35th anniversary 4k edition was released by Sony Pictures in 2019. The new 4k transfer was made from the original camera negative. The source material was in good condition and required only minimally invasive processing. This 35th anniversary 4k edition is highly praised for outstanding quality of the image and the sound on both the 4k and the Blu-ray Disc.<ref>Blu-ray.com : The Natural 4K Blu-ray 35th anniversary edition[https://www.blu-ray.com/movies/The-Natural-4K-Blu-ray/237391/]</ref>

==Soundtrack==
The [[film score]] of ''The Natural'' was composed and conducted by Randy Newman.<ref>{{cite web | title = The Natural (1984 Film) [SOUNDTRACK] | website = Amazon | year = 1984 | url = https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000002L6A | access-date = January 20, 2008 | archive-date = July 5, 2023 | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20230705204746/https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000002L6A | url-status = live }}</ref> The score has often been compared to the style of [[Aaron Copland]] and sometimes [[Elmer Bernstein]]. Scott Montgomery, writing for ''[[Goldmine (magazine)|Goldmine]]'' music magazine, referenced the influence and [[David Ansen]], reviewing the film for ''[[Newsweek]]'', called the score "Coplandesque".<ref>{{cite magazine|last=Montgomery |first=Scott |author2=Gary Norris |author3=Kevin Walsh |title=The Invisible Randy Newman |volume=21 |issue=18 |magazine=[[Goldmine (magazine)|Goldmine]] |date=September 1, 1995 |url=http://www.randynewman.info/biography/a10.html |access-date=January 20, 2008 |quote=''The Natural'', a 1984 Robert Redford vehicle based on the classic Bernard Malamud novel about a baseball player, features some of Newman's most inspiring movie music — his first score to feature synthesizers prominently in string arrangements. Leaning gently on Copland, Berlin and his uncle Al, the dramatic title theme (which has been heard in virtually every baseball-related film trailer since the movie's release) earned Newman both an Academy Award nomination for best soundtrack and a 1985 Grammy Award for Best Instrumental. |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070817154932/http://www.randynewman.info/biography/a10.html |archive-date=August 17, 2007 }}</ref><ref>{{cite magazine| last = Ansen | first = David | author-link = David Ansen | title = The Natural | magazine = [[Newsweek]] | date = May 28, 1984 }}</ref>  The score also has certain [[Richard Wagner|Wagnerian]] features of orchestration and use of [[leitmotif]]. Adnan Tezer of ''Monsters and Critics'' noted the theme is often played for film and television previews and in "baseball stadiums when introducing home teams and players".<ref name="monstersandcritics2" />

Levinson also described to [[Bob Costas]] in MLB Network's ''Costas at the Movies'' how he heard Newman develop the movie's iconic theme: "We were racing to try to get this movie out in time and we were in one room and then there was a wall and Randy's in the other room. One of the great thrilling moments is I heard him figuring out that theme...You could hear it through the wall as he was working out that theme and I'll never forget that."{{citation needed|date=September 2025}}

The soundtrack album was released May 11, 1984 on the [[Warner Records|Warner Bros.]] label, with the logo for [[TriStar Pictures|Tri-Star Pictures]] also appearing on the album to commemorate this as their first production.
