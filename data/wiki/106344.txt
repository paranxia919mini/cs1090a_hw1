'''''The Elephant Man''''' is a 1980 [[historical drama]] film loosely based on the life of [[Joseph Merrick]] (named "John" in the film), a severely deformed man who lived in [[19th-century London|London in the late 19th century]]. A co-production between the [[Cinema of the United Kingdom|United Kingdom]] and the [[Cinema of the United States|United States]], the film was directed by [[David Lynch]], produced by [[Jonathan Sanger]], and executive produced by [[Mel Brooks]] (who was uncredited, to avoid audiences expecting the film to be like his usual comedic works, although his company Brooksfilms is in the opening credits). It stars [[John Hurt]], [[Anthony Hopkins]], [[Anne Bancroft]], [[John Gielgud]], [[Wendy Hiller]], [[Michael Elphick]], [[Hannah Gordon]], and [[Freddie Jones]].

The screenplay was adapted by Lynch, Christopher De Vore, and [[Eric Bergren]] from [[Sir Frederick Treves, 1st Baronet|Frederick Treves]]' ''The Elephant Man and Other Reminiscences'' (1923) and [[Ashley Montagu]]'s ''The Elephant Man: A Study in Human Dignity'' (1971), incorporating both historical and fictionalized aspects with Lynch's distinctive [[surrealism|surrealist]] sequences, which is highly unusual for a [[biographical film]]. It was shot in [[black-and-white]] by [[Freddie Francis]] and featured make-up work by [[Christopher Tucker]].

''The Elephant Man'' was a critical and commercial success, with critics highlighting Lynch's direction and Hurt's performance. The film [[List of accolades received by The Elephant Man|garnered several accolades]] including three [[BAFTA Awards]] (including [[BAFTA Award for Best Film|Best Film]]), and nominations for eight [[Academy Awards]] and four [[Golden Globe|Golden Globe Awards]]. After receiving widespread criticism for failing to honor the make-up effects, the [[Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences]] was prompted to create a [[Academy Award for Best Makeup|competitive award for Best Makeup]] the following year.

==Plot==
<!-- PER WP:FILMPLOT, PLOT SUMMARIES FOR FEATURE FILM ARTICLES SHOULD BE BETWEEN 400 AND 700 WORDS. -->
Frederick Treves, a [[surgeon]] at the [[Royal London Hospital|London Hospital]], finds John Merrick in a [[Victorian era|Victorian]] [[freak show]] in London's [[East End of London|East End]], where he is kept by Mr. Bytes, the brutish ringmaster. Merrick's head is kept hooded outside of shows, and Bytes, who views him as [[Intellectual disability|intellectually disabled]] due to his muteness, is paid by Treves to have him brought to the hospital for examination.

Treves presents Merrick to his colleagues and highlights his physical abnormalities; his deformed skull forces him to sleep with his head on his knees against a pile of pillows, since if he were to lie down, he would [[asphyxia]]te. On Merrick's return, he is beaten so badly by Bytes that his errand boy has to call Treves for medical help. Treves brings Merrick back to the hospital, where he is tended to by hospital matron Mrs. Mothershead, as the other nurses are too frightened of him. Mr. Carr Gomm, the hospital's Governor, is against housing Merrick, as the hospital does not accept "incurables". Treves encourages Merrick to speak, finding him to be intellectually competent.

To prove that Merrick can make progress, Treves trains him to say a few conversational sentences and part of the [[Psalm 23|23rd Psalm]]. Carr Gomm sees through this ruse but, as he is leaving, Merrick begins to recite the whole of the Psalm. Merrick tells the doctors that he knows how to read and has memorized the 23rd Psalm because it is his favourite. Carr Gomm permits him to stay, and Merrick spends his time building a model of a cathedral he can partially see from his window.

At tea with Treves and his wife, Ann, Merrick views photos of their family and in return shows them his mother's picture. He believes he must have been a disappointment to her, but hopes she would be proud to see him with friends. Merrick begins to take guests in his room, including the actress [[Madge Kendal]], who gives him a copy of ''[[Romeo and Juliet]]''; they play some lines from it and Kendal kisses Merrick.

Merrick quickly becomes an object of curiosity to high society, and Mrs. Mothershead expresses concerns that he is still being put on display as a freak for this audience. Treves begins to question the morality of his own actions. Meanwhile, a night porter named Jim starts selling tickets to locals who come at night to gawk at the "Elephant Man".

The issue of Merrick's residence is challenged at a hospital council meeting, but he is granted permanent residence after the governors' hospitality is commended by [[Queen Victoria]], who sends word with her daughter-in-law [[Alexandra of Denmark|Alexandra]]. However, during one of Jim's raucous late-night showings, Merrick is kidnapped by Bytes. Another night porter reports this to Treves, who confronts Jim about what he has done; Jim is then fired by Mothershead.

Bytes takes Merrick on the road as a circus attraction once again. During a show in Belgium, Merrick, who is weak and dying, collapses, causing a drunken Bytes to lock him in a cage at night and leave him to die. Merrick is released by his fellow freakshow attractions who are escaping Bytes. Upon returning to London, Merrick is harassed through [[Liverpool Street station]] by several young boys and accidentally knocks down a young girl. Merrick is chased, unmasked, and cornered by an angry mob. Before collapsing, he cries "I am not an elephant! I am not an animal! I am a human being! I am a man!"<ref>{{cite web |last1=Soddon |first1=Gem |title=The 25 most depressing movies ever made |url=https://www.gamesradar.com/30-most-depressing-movies/2/ |website=GamesRadar+ |access-date=27 February 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220322002917/https://www.gamesradar.com/30-most-depressing-movies/2/ |archive-date=March 22, 2022 |date=April 1, 2022 |url-status=live}}</ref>

Policemen return Merrick to the hospital. Treves apologises to Merrick for his ordeal, but Merrick says he is happy and his life fulfilled from all Treves has done. Treves and Mothershead take Merrick, accompanying Princess Alexandra, to see a [[pantomime]]. Kendal comes on stage afterwards and dedicates the performance to him, and a proud Merrick receives a standing ovation from the audience.

Back at the hospital, Merrick and Treves bid each other goodnight, and Merrick completes his cathedral model. He lies down on his back in bed, imitating a sleeping child in a picture on his wall, and dies. A vision of his mother quotes [[Alfred, Lord Tennyson|Lord Tennyson]]'s "Nothing Will Die".

==Production==

===Development===
Producer [[Jonathan Sanger]] optioned the script from writers Christopher De Vore and [[Eric Bergren]] after receiving the script from his babysitter.<ref name="AFI" /> Sanger had been working as [[Mel Brooks]]' assistant director on ''[[High Anxiety]]''.<ref name=":0" /> Sanger showed Brooks the script, and Brooks said he "read it through at one sitting and cried. It was the first time something like that had moved me since ''The Miracle Worker''."<ref name="lc">{{cite news|newspaper=The Guardian|date=9 November 1979|page=15|title=Man with anouther kind of high anxiety|first=Linda|last=Christmas}}</ref>

Brooks decided to help finance via Brooksfilms, his new company, which had just made its first film, ''Fatso''. Brooks' personal assistant, [[Stuart Cornfeld]], suggested [[David Lynch]] to Sanger.<ref name=FSR>{{cite web|url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2008/jul/27/42|title= 'How does a guy known for fart jokes make The Elephant Man?'|website= [[The Guardian]]|date= July 26, 2008|accessdate= December 28, 2021}}</ref><ref name=":0">{{cite web|url=https://filmschoolrejects.com/mel-brooks-david-lynch-and-the-elephant-man/|title= A Brief History of Mel Brooks, David Lynch and 'The Elephant Man'|website= Film School Rejects|date= June 28, 2021|accessdate= December 29, 2021}}</ref>

Sanger met Lynch, and they shared scripts they were working on (''The Elephant Man'' and Lynch's unrealized ''[[Ronnie Rocket]]''). Lynch told Sanger that he would love to direct the script after reading it, and Sanger endorsed him after hearing Lynch's ideas. However, Brooks had not heard of Lynch at the time. Sanger and Cornfeld set up an ''[[Eraserhead]]'' viewing at a [[20th Century Studios|20th Century Fox]] screening room; Brooks loved it and enthusiastically agreed for Lynch to direct.<ref name=":0" /> "''Eraserhead'' made me feel he could handle melancholy and freaks and could use black and white film," said Brooks. "We want ''Elephant Man'' in black and white to minimise the horrific deformity and also to help us capture the feel of Victorian England."<ref name="lc"/>

By his own request, Brooks was not credited as executive producer to ensure that audiences would not expect a comedy after seeing his name attached.<ref name="Potton">{{cite news|url=https://www.thetimes.com/life-style/article/the-elephant-man-1980-review-8dj3gzzf0 |title= The Elephant Man (1980) review |date=14 June 2019  |last=Potton |first=Ed |work=The Times |access-date=5 February 2024 |url-access=subscription |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20190614142040/https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/the-elephant-man-1980-review-8dj3gzzf0 |archive-date=June 14, 2019 |url-status=live}}</ref>

A stage play about Merrick called ''[[The Elephant Man (play)|The Elephant Man]]'' by [[Bernard Pomerance]] had debuted in 1977 and enjoyed a successful run on Broadway in 1979. This play was [[The Elephant Man (1982 film)|adapted as a television movie in 1982]]. It was not used as a source for the film.<ref>{{cite news|title=Not so|newspaper=Evening Standard|date=20 August 1979|page=13}}</ref> The producers of the play sued Brooks over the use of the title and he counter sued claiming it was public property.<ref name="lc"/>

===Casting===
[[Dustin Hoffman]] wanted the role of John Merrick, but Sanger rejected the idea, saying "We’re always going to be looking to see where the Elephant Man ends and Dustin Hoffman begins". Lynch considered his friend [[Jack Nance]], who he worked with on ''Eraserhead'', for the role, but he cast John Hurt in the role after seeing ''[[The Naked Civil Servant (film)|The Naked Civil Servant]]''.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://inews.co.uk/culture/the-elephant-man-anniversary-film-tributes-john-merrick-john-hurt-cast-interview-416029 | title='More a mission than a movie': Celebrating the Elephant Man at 40 | date=April 6, 2020 }}</ref> At the time, Hurt was still making ''[[Heaven's Gate (film)|Heaven's Gate]]'' which had fallen badly behind schedule due to director [[Michael Cimino]]'s perfectionism. Hurt spent so long waiting for something to do that he performed the role of Merrick in the interim before returning to ''Heaven's Gate'' to complete shooting.<ref>{{cite web |last=Lambie |first=Ryan |title=10 stories of excess from the production of Heaven's Gate |date=March 27, 2014 |url=https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/10-stories-of-excess-from-the-production-of-heavens-gate/}}</ref>

===Filming===
The budget was $5&nbsp;million, $4&nbsp;million of which was raised from [[Fred Silverman]] of [[NBC]].<ref name="AFI" /> Part of this deal involved Brooks doing a one-hour special for NBC.<ref name="lc"/> The remainder came from [[EMI Films]].<ref>{{cite news|title=At the Movies: Seriously, Folks, There's a Serious Mel Brooks|last=Ryan |first=Desmond|work=[[The Philadelphia Inquirer]]|date=3 Nov 1985|page=L.2}}</ref><ref name="spike">{{cite magazine|url=https://www.filmink.com.au/forgotten-british-moguls-barry-spikings/|first=Stephen|magazine=Filmink|last=Vagg|title=Forgotten British Moguls: Barry Spikings|date=26 October 2025|access-date=26 October 2025}}</ref>

For his second feature and first studio film, albeit one independently financed,<ref name="TimeOutInterview" /> Lynch provided the musical direction and sound design. Lynch also tried to design the make-up himself, but the design did not work.<ref name="AFI">{{cite web |title=The Elephant Man (1980) |url=https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/56402 |website=[[AFI Catalog of Feature Films]]}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news |last=Carroll |first=Rory |date=June 25, 2018 |title=David Lynch: As a father and husband 'you gotta be selfish' |url=https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/film/david-lynch-as-a-father-and-husband-you-gotta-be-selfish-1.3542802 |access-date=2024-02-05 |newspaper=The Irish Times |language=en}}</ref> The makeup, now supervised by [[Christopher Tucker]], was based on direct casts of Merrick's body, which had been kept in the [[Royal London Hospital]]'s private museum. The makeup took seven to eight hours to apply each day and two hours to delicately remove.<ref name="AFI"/> [[John Hurt]] would arrive on set at 5&nbsp;am and shoot his scenes from noon until 10&nbsp;pm. After his first experience of the inconvenience of having to apply the makeup and perform with it, he called his girlfriend, saying, "I think they have finally managed to make me hate acting."<ref name="Vincent">{{cite news |last1=Vincent |first1=Alice |title=When John Hurt broke the mould: the story behind The Elephant Man's radical make-up |url=https://www.telegraph.co.uk/films/0/john-hurt-broke-mould-story-behind-elephant-mans-radical-make/ |work=The Telegraph |date=31 March 2020}}</ref> Because of the strain on the actor, he worked alternate days.<ref name="AFI"/>

The film is bookended with [[surrealism|surrealist]] sequences, which is highly unusual for a biographical film according to studio executives, centered around Merrick's mother and her death.<ref>{{Cite news |last=James |date=2015-01-28 |title=Scattered Thoughts on 'The Elephant Man' - Dark Lane Creative |url=https://darklanecreative.com/scattered-thoughts-on-the-elephant-man-2/ |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260120031040/https://darklanecreative.com/scattered-thoughts-on-the-elephant-man-2/ |archive-date=2026-01-20 |access-date=2026-03-31 |work=Dark Lane Creative |language=en-GB}}</ref> Lynch used [[Samuel Barber]]'s ''[[Adagio for Strings]]'' to underline the climax and Merrick's own death. Composer [[John Morris (composer)|John Morris]] argued against using the music, stating that "this piece is going to be used over and over and over again in the future... And every time it's used in a film it's going to diminish the effect of the scene."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2018/01/28/obituaries/john-morris-composer-for-mel-brookss-films-dies-at-91.html|title=John Morris, Composer for Mel Brooks's Films, Dies at 91|first=Richard|last=Sandomir|date=January 29, 2018|access-date=January 29, 2018|newspaper=[[The New York Times]]}}</ref>

===Post-production===
Following their return from England with a print, Lynch and Sanger screened ''The Elephant Man'' for Brooks, who suggested some minor cuts but told them that the film would be released as they had made it.<ref name=FSR/>

==Lawsuit==
A [[West End theatre|West End]] [[The Elephant Man (play)|play of the same name]] was enjoying a successful Broadway run at the time of the film's production. The producers sued Brooksfilms over the use of the title.<ref>{{cite news|title=Title Fight for 'Elephant Man'|last=Schreger |first=Charles|work=Los Angeles Times|date=Aug 22, 1979|page=f10}}</ref><ref name="AFI" />

==Box office==
''The Elephant Man'' was a box office hit, grossing $26&nbsp;million in the United States.<ref name="bom"/><ref name=AFI/> In Japan, it was the second highest-grossing foreign film of the year with [[theatrical rental]]s of ¥2.45&nbsp;billion, behind only ''[[The Empire Strikes Back]]''.<ref>{{cite magazine|magazine=[[Variety (magazine)|Variety]]|date=7 March 1984|title=All-Time Foreign Grossers In Japan|page=89}}</ref>

==Reception==
On [[review aggregator]] [[Rotten Tomatoes]], it has an approval rating of 91% based on 67 reviews, with an average score of 8.6/10. The site's critical consensus reads, "David Lynch's relatively straight second feature finds an admirable synthesis of compassion and restraint in treating its subject, and features outstanding performances by John Hurt and Anthony Hopkins."<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/1006527-elephant_man |title=The Elephant Man (1980) |website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |access-date=April 5, 2025}}</ref> On [[Metacritic]], the film has a weighted average score of 78 out of 100 based on 16 critic reviews, indicating "generally favorable reviews".<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.metacritic.com/movie/the-elephant-man |title=The Elephant Man Reviews |website=[[Metacritic]] |access-date=October 28, 2020}}</ref> Audiences polled by [[CinemaScore]] gave the film a rare grade of "A+" on an A+ to F scale, making it the only Lynch film ever to achieve that grade.<ref name="1980Scores">{{Cite news |date=January 30, 1981 |title=CinemaScore |url=https://news.google.com/newspapers?id=vDkpAAAAIBAJ&sjid=VoMDAAAAIBAJ&pg=1884%2C8022868 |access-date=March 23, 2025 |work=[[Deseret News]] |location=[[Salt Lake City]] |page=2C}}</ref>

[[Vincent Canby]] wrote: "Mr. Hurt is truly remarkable. It can't be easy to act under such a heavy mask&nbsp;... the physical production is beautiful, especially Freddie Francis' black-and-white photography."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1980/10/03/archives/elephant-man-study-in-genteelness.html | title='Elephant Man,' — Study in Genteelness | newspaper=[[The New York Times]] | first=Vincent |last=Canby | author-link=Vincent Canby | date=October 3, 1980 | access-date=January 29, 2018}}</ref>

A small number of critics were less favourable. [[Roger Ebert]] gave it 2/4 stars, writing: "I kept asking myself what the film was really trying to say about the [[human condition]] as reflected by John Merrick, and I kept drawing blanks."<ref>{{cite web|date=1 January 1980|title=The Elephant Man|url=http://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/the-elephant-man-1980|access-date=September 12, 2012|website=[[RogerEbert.com]]}}</ref> In the book ''The Spectacle of Deformity: Freak Shows and Modern British Culture'', [[Nadja Durbach]] describes the work as "much more mawkish and moralising than one would expect from the leading postmodern surrealist filmmaker" and "unashamedly sentimental". She blamed this mawkishness on the use of Treves' memoirs as source material.<ref name="Durbach35" />

===Accolades===
{{Main|List of accolades received by The Elephant Man}}
''The Elephant Man'' was nominated for eight [[Academy Awards]],<ref>{{Cite web|title=David Lynch – Chapter 2: The Elephant Man and Dune – An Auteur In Hollywood|url=http://www.britishfilm.org.uk/lynch/elephant_man_dune.html|access-date=2017-11-23|website=The British Film Resource|archive-date=November 21, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171121143027/http://www.britishfilm.org.uk/lynch/elephant_man_dune.html|url-status=dead}}</ref> tying ''[[Raging Bull]]'' at the [[53rd Academy Awards]], including [[Academy Award for Best Picture|Best Picture]], [[Academy Award for Best Actor|Actor in a Leading Role]] (Hurt),<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1981/02/18/movies/elephant-man-and-bull-up-for-8-oscars-each.html|title='Elephant Man' and 'Bull' Up for 8 Oscars Each|last1=Harmetz|first1=Aljean|date=February 18, 1981|work=[[The New York Times]]|access-date=November 23, 2017|language=en-US|issn=0362-4331}}</ref> [[Academy Award for Best Production Design|Art Direction-Set Decoration]] ([[Stuart Craig]], [[Robert Cartwright]], [[Hugh Scaife]]), [[Academy Award for Best Costume Design|Best Costume Design]], [[Academy Award for Best Director|Best Director]], [[Academy Award for Best Film Editing|Best Film Editing]], [[Academy Award for Best Original Score|Music: Original Score]], and [[Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay|Writing: Screenplay Based on Material from Another Medium]].<ref name="NY Times">{{cite web |url=https://movies.nytimes.com/movie/15593/The-Elephant-Man/awards |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090412175700/http://movies.nytimes.com/movie/15593/The-Elephant-Man/awards |url-status=dead |archive-date=April 12, 2009 |department=Movies & TV Dept. |work=[[The New York Times]] |date=2009 |title=The Elephant Man |access-date=December 31, 2008}}</ref> However, it did not win any.

Industry experts were appalled that the film was not going to be honoured for its make-up effects when the [[Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences]] announced its nominations at the time. A letter of protest was sent to the academy's Board of Governors requesting to give the film an honorary award. The academy refused, but in response to the outcry, they decided to give the make-up artists their own category. A year later, the [[Academy Award for Best Makeup and Hairstyling|Academy Award for Best Makeup]] category was introduced with ''[[An American Werewolf in London]]'' as its first recipient.<ref name="AFI"/><ref>{{cite news | newspaper=[[The Independent]] | title=The Elephant Man | first=Roger |last=Clarke | date=March 2, 2007 | url=http://www.highbeam.com/doc/1P2-3735317.html| archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121104010345/http://www.highbeam.com/doc/1P2-3735317.html| via=[[HighBeam Research]] | url-access=subscription | url-status=usurped| archive-date=November 4, 2012}}</ref>

It won the [[BAFTA Award for Best Film]], and other [[British Academy of Film and Television Arts|BAFTA Awards]] for Best Actor (Hurt) and Best Production Design, and was nominated for four others: Direction, Screenplay, Cinematography and Editing.

The film is recognized by [[American Film Institute]] in these lists:
* 2005: [[AFI's 100 Years...100 Movie Quotes]]:
** John Merrick: "I am not an animal! I am a human being. I am a man." – Nominated<ref>{{cite web |title=AFI's 100 Years...100 Movie Quotes Nominees |url=http://www.afi.com/Docs/100Years/quotes400.pdf |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110313150620/http://www.afi.com/Docs/100Years/quotes400.pdf |archive-date=March 13, 2011 |access-date=2016-07-30 |website=American Film Institute}}</ref>

==Soundtrack==
The musical score of ''The Elephant Man'' was composed and conducted by [[John Morris (composer)|John Morris]], and it was performed by the [[National Philharmonic Orchestra]]. In 1980, the company [[20th Century Fox Records]] published this film's original musical score as both an [[LP record|LP]] album and as a [[Compact Cassette|cassette]] in the United States. Its front cover artwork features a masked John Merrick against a backdrop of smoke, as seen on the advance theatrical poster for the film.

In 1994, the first [[compact disc]] (CD) issue of the film score was made by the company [[Milan Records|Milan]], which specializes in [[film score]]s and [[soundtrack]] albums.<ref>{{Cite web |title=The Elephant Man Soundtrack (1980) |url=https://www.soundtrack.net/album/the-elephant-man/ |access-date=2024-02-05 |website=www.soundtrack.net}}</ref>

==In popular culture==
[[Michael Jackson]] used excerpts from the film in his song "Morphine" from the 1997 remix album ''[[Blood on the Dance Floor: HIStory in the Mix]]''.<ref>Jackson, Michael. ''Blood on the Dance Floor: HIStory in the Mix'' booklet. Sony BMG. p. 4.</ref>

[[The Jam]]'s former bassist [[Bruce Foxton]] was inspired strongly by the film, and in response wrote the song "[[Freak (Bruce Foxton song)|Freak]]" with the single's cover making a reference to the film.<ref>{{cite web |date=July 5, 2014 |title=Jam, The |url=http://nostalgiacentral.com/music/music-by-decade/1970s-music/jam/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240623071950/https://nostalgiacentral.com/music/artists-a-to-k/artists-j/jam/ |archive-date=June 23, 2024 |access-date=January 29, 2018 |website=Nostalgia Central}}</ref>

Actor [[Bradley Cooper]] credits watching the film with his father as a child as his inspiration to become an actor. Cooper played the character in a Broadway revival of [[The Elephant Man (play)|''The Elephant Man'' play]] in 2014.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Kyung Kim |first=Eun |date=2014-10-21 |title=Bradley Cooper on 'The Elephant Man:' It's why I wanted to become an actor |url=https://www.today.com/popculture/bradley-cooper-elephant-man-its-why-i-became-actor-1d80231082 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240205172148/https://www.today.com/popculture/bradley-cooper-elephant-man-its-why-i-became-actor-1d80231082 |archive-date=2024-02-05 |work=[[Today (American TV program)|Today]]}}</ref>

In [[The Simpsons (season 3)|season 3]], episode 21 of ''[[The Simpsons]]'', "[[Black Widower]]", [[Lisa Simpson|Lisa]] daydreams of [[Patty and Selma|Aunt Selma]]'s new boyfriend as the Elephant Man.<ref>{{cite book |last=Groening |first=Matt |author-link=Matt Groening |editor1-first=Ray |editor1-last=Richmond |editor1-link=Ray Richmond |editor2-first=Antonia |editor2-last=Coffman |title=[[The Simpsons episode guides#The Simpsons: A Complete Guide to Our Favorite Family|The Simpsons: A Complete Guide to Our Favorite Family]] |edition=1st |year=1997 |location=New York |publisher=[[HarperPerennial]] |lccn=98141857 |ol=433519M |oclc=37796735 |isbn=978-0-06-095252-5 |page=[https://archive.org/details/simpsonscomplete00groe/page/86 86] |ref={{harvid|Richmond & Coffman|1997}} }}.</ref>

In season 4, episode 13 of ''[[Seinfeld]]'', "[[The Pick]]", in defense of allegedly picking his nose, Jerry pleads to a surrounding crowd: "Are we not human? If we pick, do we not bleed? I am not an animal!"<ref>{{cite web |last1=Seinfeld |author1-link=Seinfeld |title=
"Jerry Gets Caught Picking His Nose {{pipe}} The Pick {{pipe}} Seinfeld" |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vluCBLrP2Tg?t=214 |website=youtube.com |publisher=[[NBC]] |language=en |format=video |date=December 16, 1992}}</ref>

The 1992 film ''[[Batman Returns]]'' parodies the iconic line "I am not an animal. I am a man." In one scene, [[Oswald Cobblepot (Batman Returns)|the Penguin]], after being called Oswald, angrily yells "I am not a human being! I am an animal!"<ref>{{cite book |last1=Smith |first1=Jim |last2=Matthews |first2=J. Clive |date=2002 |title=Tim Burton |location=London |publisher=Virgin Books |page=113 |isbn=0-7535-0682-3}}</ref>

British TV presenter [[Karl Pilkington]] has often cited the film as his favorite, and has referenced it frequently during his appearances on [[The Ricky Gervais Show]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=2012-09-21 |title=Ricky Gervais Explains The Mind Of Karl Pilkington |url=http://teamcoco.com/video/ricky-gervais-explains-the-mind-of-karl-pilkington |access-date=2015-09-26 |website=TeamCoco.com}}</ref>

Musician [[Michael Stipe]] loves the film and cites it as an inspiration for the [[R.E.M.]] song "Carnival of Sorts (Boxcars)".<ref>{{Cite web |date=2009-10-25 |title=The Story Of R.E.M. Without The Greatest Hits |url=https://www.npr.org/transcripts/114117204 |access-date=2021-03-08 |website=npr.org}}</ref> Another R.E.M. song, "[[New Test Leper]]", quotes the line "I am not an animal."<ref>{{Cite web |date=3 March 2022 |title=R.E.M. - New Adventures in Hi-Fi (Craft Recordings) |url=https://bigtakeover.com/recordings/REMNewAdventuresinHiFiWarnerBros |access-date=2024-02-05 |website=The Big Takeover |language=en}}</ref>

Musician [[Mylène Farmer]]'s song "[[Allan (song)#B-side: "Psychiatric"|Psychiatric]]" from the 1991 album ''[[L'autre...]]'' is a tribute to the film and [[John Hurt]]'s voice is sampled throughout the song, repeating several times: "I'm a human being, I'm not an animal".<ref>{{cite book |title=Le Dictionnaire des Chansons de Mylène Farmer |last=Cachin |first=Benoît |year=2006 |publisher=Tournon |language=French |page=211 |isbn=2-35144-000-5}}</ref>
